Our data

Data Sources & Methodology

We are a mirror of the public record, not an authority. This page explains where the record comes from, how we handle it, and how to check our work.

Last updated: October 18, 2026

Where the data comes from

Every report is built from official public records published by the jurisdiction itself. We onboard sources in a strict order of preference:

  • Official open-data APIs (for example, city open-data portals on the Socrata platform) — structured, dated, and updated by the jurisdiction.
  • Official bulk files published by the jurisdiction (CSV and similar formats).
  • Official public portals, only where the portal's terms allow the use.

A source whose terms do not allow use does not get used. We never bypass paywalls, logins, or access controls.

What every record carries

Each permit record is stored with its source jurisdiction, the jurisdiction's own identifier for the permit, the dates the jurisdiction reports, and two freshness stamps we add: the date the source last updated and the date we collected it. Both dates appear on live reports. If a source runs behind — some jurisdictions publish months late — the report says so. Freshness honesty is a feature, not an embarrassment.

The original record is kept exactly as the source published it before any cleaning, so any normalized figure can be traced back to what the source actually said.

Quality gates before anything publishes

  • Schema check — required fields present and correctly typed.
  • Duplicate check — a permit may appear once; repeat pulls update the record, never duplicate it.
  • Sanity check — dates, statuses, and values outside plausible ranges are flagged, not published.
  • Volume check — a feed that suddenly sends far more or far fewer records than its norm is held for review.
  • Freshness check — a feed silent past its normal cadence is labeled stale on its pages automatically.
  • Manual review — every new jurisdiction gets a full human review before its first report goes live.

Anything that fails a gate is held, not published.

Coverage and status

Wave 1 onboarding follows the highest-volume, cleanest-feed jurisdictions first: Texas metros, then Florida metros, then Maricopa County (Arizona), North Carolina metros, and Cook County (Illinois). The first feeds in onboarding are official open-data feeds, including those published by Chicago, Austin, San Francisco, and Seattle.

Now live: Cook County, Illinois — the City of Chicago feed passed every gate above and its report published on October 17, 2026: Building Permits in Cook County — 31,746 permits issued in the trailing 12 months, as reported by the City of Chicago.

Now live: Travis County, Texas — the City of Austin feed passed every gate above and its report published on October 17, 2026: Building Permits in Travis County (Austin) — 55,575 permits issued in the trailing 12 months, as reported by the City of Austin. The report covers permits issued by the City of Austin (whose permitting jurisdiction reaches into its extraterritorial jurisdiction and a few neighboring communities); it is not a blend of every jurisdiction in Travis County.

About project values in Travis County: we do not publish a dollar total for Austin yet, because the source's valuation field failed our sanity validation. Most Austin permits carry no valuation at all (about 91% are blank or zero); of the rest, most carry a $1 placeholder, and the largest project's full value ($830 million) is stamped identically on 77 sibling permits, so adding the field up as published would report roughly $71.8 billion — a number the underlying records do not support. Rather than publish a figure we cannot stand behind, the Travis County report publishes permit counts, trades, categories, and monthly volumes only, until a de-duplicated valuation passes validation. Counts are unaffected: they were verified against the source's own totals (September 2026 matched exactly, 5,129 permits).

Now live: San Francisco County, California — the City and County of San Francisco feed (DataSF, published by the Department of Building Inspection) passed every gate above and its report published on October 18, 2026: Building Permits in San Francisco — 20,170 permits issued in the trailing 12 months, as reported by the City and County of San Francisco.

How San Francisco permits are counted: San Francisco's feed publishes one row for every address or lot a permit touches, so a single permit can appear several times in the raw feed (in the trailing 12 months, 22,076 published rows represent 20,170 permits). We count each permit once, at the address the department flags as its primary address; the sibling rows are copies of the same permit at its other addresses, identical in status, dates, and cost, and counting them would overstate both permits and project value. Project values are the department's revised cost where a real figure is published, otherwise its estimated cost, taken from the primary record only; on that basis the report's totals were verified against the source (trailing-12-month value matched to the dollar, and September 2026 counts matched exactly, 1,298 permits). The San Francisco feed publishes no contractor names, so none appear in this report.

Now live: King County, Washington — the City of Seattle feed passed every gate above and its report published on October 18, 2026: Building Permits in King County (Seattle) — 6,348 permits issued in the trailing 12 months, as reported by the City of Seattle. The report covers permits issued by the City of Seattle only; other King County cities publish their permits separately and are not in this feed, so totals here are Seattle totals, labeled as such.

About project values in King County: we do not publish a dollar total for Seattle, for a different reason than Austin's. Seattle's estimated-cost field is well populated (6,347 of 6,348 permits carry a value), but the city stamps one development's full estimated cost onto each of its phase and sibling permits — demolition, grading, and phased construction permits for the same project each repeat the whole project's cost. Thirty-eight demolition permits at different addresses each carry the identical $24,102,600, and ten demolition permits on a single street each carry $50,000,000. Duplicated values over $1 million alone add about $2.0 billion of repeat counting — 38.5% of the $5.2 billion a raw sum would report — and the feed publishes no project linkage we could de-duplicate by. Rather than publish a figure we cannot stand behind, the King County report publishes permit counts, trades, categories, and monthly volumes only. Counts are unaffected: they were verified against the source's own totals (September 2026 matched exactly, 507 permits).

Now live: New York City, New York — all five boroughs publish as their own county reports from the NYC Department of Buildings' DOB NOW: Build – Approved Permits feed (NYC Open Data), published October 18, 2026: New York County (Manhattan) — 57,921 permits, Kings County (Brooklyn) — 39,063, Queens County — 26,934, Bronx County — 15,736, and Richmond County (Staten Island) — 8,029, for 147,683 permits issued in the trailing 12 months, as reported by the NYC Department of Buildings. One counting rule matters here: DOB's feed lists one row for every work type an issuance covers, so a single issuance appears as one to six rows. We count each issuance once — a permit on this site is one issuance of one DOB permit, identified by its job filing and issuance (tracking) numbers. DOB NOW excludes electrical, elevator, and limited-alteration permits, which the department publishes in separate datasets, so no electrical figures appear in the New York City reports. Four issuances in the window carried the feed's own placeholder job number instead of a real one; with no verifiable source identity, they are held and not published.

About project values in New York City: values publish for New York City, under a stated rule. The estimated cost in DOB's feed belongs to the whole job filing, and the department repeats it identically on every issuance of that filing — the initial permit and each renewal alike. Summing it per issuance would report $58.8 billion and count one filing's cost up to eight times, so we count each filing's stated cost once, on its most recent issuance in the window: $43,586,788,485 across 125,096 filings. That total was verified to the cent against an independently computed aggregation of the source feed before publication. The figures are the applicants' own estimates, carried as reported: 14,703 filings state $0 and 2,273 state $1, and 86.4% of filings state more than $1.

Now live: Philadelphia County, Pennsylvania — the City of Philadelphia feed (OpenDataPhilly, published by the Department of Licenses and Inspections) passed every gate above and its report published on October 18, 2026: Building Permits in Philadelphia County — 45,900 permits issued in the trailing 12 months, as reported by the City of Philadelphia. Philadelphia is a consolidated city-county, so the city feed covers the whole county.

How Philadelphia permits are counted: the city's table carries one row per permit, and the permit number is exactly unique across the trailing 12 months (45,900 rows = 45,900 permit numbers), so every row is counted once, as the permit the Department of Licenses and Inspections issued. Permits the city issues under a parent job — a trade permit tied to a building-permit job, for example — are separate permits with their own numbers, and are counted as the separate permits they are.

About project values in Philadelphia County: we publish no dollar total for Philadelphia, because the city's feed publishes no project-cost field at all — its 47 columns carry no valuation figure to validate or withhold (a different situation from Austin's and Seattle's, where a value field exists but failed validation). Rather than estimate one, the Philadelphia report publishes permit counts, trades, categories, and monthly volumes only. Counts are unaffected: they were verified against the source's own totals (September 2026 matched exactly, 3,649 permits).

Now live: Suffolk County, Massachusetts — the City of Boston feed (Boston Open Data, published by the Inspectional Services Department) passed every gate above and its report published on October 18, 2026: Building Permits in Suffolk County (Boston) — 36,367 permits issued in the trailing 12 months, as reported by the City of Boston. The report covers permits issued by the City of Boston only; Suffolk County also contains Chelsea, Revere, and Winthrop, which publish their permits separately and are not in this feed, so totals here are Boston totals, labeled as such.

How Boston permits are counted: the city's dataset carries one row per issued permit, and the permit number is exactly unique across the trailing 12 months (36,367 rows = 36,367 permit numbers), so every row is counted once, as the permit the Inspectional Services Department issued. Amendments and Certificates of Occupancy are separate permits the city issued, with their own permit numbers, and are counted as the separate permits they are. The dataset's only name field is the applicant's name; it is never requested from the source, and no names appear in this report.

About project values in Suffolk County: values publish for Boston. The declared value on every one of the 36,367 permits in the window follows the same stated format, 97.9% of permits state more than $1 (484 state $0 and 281 state $1), and there is no placeholder or sibling-stamp pattern at scale — the most-repeated large values are round applicant budgets. Our stored total was verified to the cent against an independently computed sum of the source field: $12,753,401,142.08 for the trailing 12 months. One quirk is carried as reported: a Certificate of Occupancy restates its project's declared value, so a project's value can appear on both its building permit and its certificate; the figures are the applicants' own declarations, printed as the city publishes them. Counts and values were both verified against the source (September 2026 matched exactly, 3,145 permits and $1,462,223,768.55).

Now live: Hamilton County, Ohio — the City of Cincinnati feed (Cincinnati Open Data, from the Buildings & Inspections Department) passed every gate above and its report published on October 18, 2026: Building Permits in Hamilton County (Cincinnati) — 9,788 permits issued in the trailing 12 months, as reported by the City of Cincinnati. The report covers permits issued by the City of Cincinnati only; other Hamilton County jurisdictions publish their permits separately and are not in this feed, so totals here are Cincinnati totals, labeled as such. One source fact to know: the city's dataset was replaced with a new extract on October 18, 2026, and every figure in this report comes from the current extract.

How Cincinnati permits are counted: the city's dataset lists one row for every work component a permit covers — a permit that covers excavation plus a new building appears as two rows under one permit number, sharing one application date, one issue date, one address, and one company of record. In the trailing 12 months, 10,417 published rows represent 9,788 permits. We count each permit once, identified by its permit number, and classify its trade from its components. The dataset publishes no applicant or owner names at all; the only name carried is the permit's published company of record, and the city's "OWNER" code for owner-permitted work is not a company name and is never shown as one. The dataset also carries no electrical permits at all, so no electrical figures appear in this report.

About project values in Hamilton County: values publish for Cincinnati, under a stated rule. Each component row states its own slice of the permit's estimated cost, so a permit's reported value is the sum of its components' stated costs — counted once per permit, never once per row. Every one of the 10,417 component rows in the window carries a stated figure, 97.1% of permits total more than $1 (278 total $0 and 4 total $1), and there is no placeholder or sibling-stamp pattern: the most-repeated large totals are round applicant budgets, and the largest permit — a $140 million new building — appears exactly once. Our stored total was verified to the cent against an independently computed sum of the source field: $1,240,768,378.84 for the trailing 12 months. Counts and values were both verified against the source (September 2026 matched exactly, 763 permits and $115,264,789.61).

How to cite our figures

Every published figure carries its county, period, and source. Please cite it the same way: "PermitsByCounty data, [County], [Month Year] — as reported by [source jurisdiction]." That chain is what makes a number checkable, and checkable is the whole point.

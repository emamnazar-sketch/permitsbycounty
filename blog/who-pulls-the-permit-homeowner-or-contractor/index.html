Who is responsible for pulling a permit — the homeowner or the contractor?

By PermitsByCounty Research Team · Published October 29, 2026 · Guides & Answers

QUICK ANSWER

If you hire a licensed contractor, the contractor should pull the permit — apply in their own name, list their license, schedule the inspections, and fix work that fails — while you, the homeowner, pay the city fee as part of the bid, as Angi explains in its guide updated April 9, 2026. You still carry the final duty to the city to make sure a permit exists before work starts. If you pull an owner-builder permit instead, you become the party of record, and code compliance, inspections, and worker-injury risk move to you.

TL;DR — the whole post in 20 seconds

  • Pulling and paying are two different jobs. The contractor pulls the permit. The homeowner pays the fee, usually inside the bid.
  • The name on the permit is the name on the hook. That person answers the inspector and fixes failed work.
  • You are still the backstop. If no permit exists, the city can fine you and stop the job.
  • Pulling your own permit is a real choice for work you supervise yourself. It is a bad trade when a hired contractor asks you to do it for their work.
  • Put one sentence in the contract before you sign: the contractor will obtain all permits and inspections the job needs.

Pulling a permit and paying for it are not the same job

Most confusion starts here, so split the two jobs apart.

Pulling means filing the application, signing it, giving the city a license number, handing in plans, answering review questions, scheduling inspections, and closing the permit.

Paying means covering the city fee. On a hired job, that money almost always comes from you, even when the contractor hands the payment to the city.

A licensed contractor includes permitting in the project bid, pays the city, and obtains the permit in their own name; the homeowner pays that cost as part of the project price; Angi lists an average building permit cost of $1,650 on the same guide

Source: Angi — Who Pulls Permits: Homeowner or Contractor? · Date pulled: October 29, 2026

That split is fair. You pay for a permit on your own house. The contractor files because the filing is technical, and their name on the permit ties them to the result.

Ask to see the fee as its own bid line. A bid with no permit line at all is a warning to slow down and ask why.

The short answer: the contractor pulls it for work they do

If a licensed contractor is doing the work, the contractor pulls the permit for that work. That is the normal setup across the trade, and standard contract forms say it in plain words.

The AIA A105 standard short-form owner-contractor agreement, Section 8.7.1, says the contractor shall obtain and pay for the building permit and other permits, governmental fees, licenses, and inspections needed to complete the work

The standard contract puts permits on the contractor — United States · AIA A105-2017 wording, checked October 29, 2026

Source: LegalClarity — Is It the Contractor’s Responsibility to Pull Permits?, summarising AIA A105 Section 8.7.1 · Date pulled: October 29, 2026

There are plain reasons the contractor is the right filer. They know which drawings the city wants, and how a reviewer expects the work described. And when an inspector asks a hard technical question, they ask the person named on the permit. That should be the person who did the work, not you.

Trade permits follow the same rule. The electrician pulls the electrical permit, the plumber pulls the plumbing permit. A general contractor usually coordinates the filings, but each trade stands behind its own work.

You are still the backstop, even when the contractor pulls it

Here is the part many homeowners miss. Delegating the filing does not erase your final duty to the city.

In most places the homeowner is legally responsible for making sure the right permits are in place, no matter who obtains them; if the contract does not clearly say the contractor must pull permits, the homeowner is ultimately responsible for fines or other fallout if they do not

The owner keeps the final duty — United States · Angi guide checked October 29, 2026

Source: Angi — Who Pays If Contractors Don’t Pull Permits? · Date pulled: October 29, 2026

Read that as a reason to verify, not worry. You do not need to file yourself. You do need proof the papers were filed, in the right name, for the right work, before work starts. That check takes minutes; the steps are near the end of this post.

A handshake promise is not enough. If a contractor never pulls a promised permit, the city still comes to the owner first. Your written contract gives you a clear claim back.

Why the name on the permit matters so much

A permit is not just a receipt. It names the party the city holds to the code for that job.

When a contractor pulls the permit, they are legally responsible for code compliance, passing inspections, and fixing deficient work, backed by their liability and workers’ compensation insurance; when the homeowner pulls it, that liability shifts to the homeowner

The permit holder carries the liability — United States · Guide checked October 29, 2026

Source: LegalClarity — Is It the Contractor’s Responsibility to Pull Permits? · Date pulled: October 29, 2026

In plain terms, the holder schedules each inspection, answers correction notices, and does the fixes. If framing fails because a joist is too small, that cost sits with the holder.

There is a quieter loss too. LegalClarity notes that several states run recovery funds for homeowners hurt by licensed contractors, and those funds usually require that the contractor held the permit. Pull it yourself, and the fund may have no contractor to hold to account.

When pulling your own permit does make sense

Owner-builder permits exist for a real reason. If you are doing the work yourself, or truly plan to run the job and hire licensed trades one by one, pulling the permit in your name matches the facts.

In that setup you are the general contractor in everything but title. You pick the trades, check licenses and insurance, set the schedule, call each inspection, and fix what fails. Some experienced owners do this well and save the markup. That saving is pay for real work and real risk, not free money.

State warnings spell out the risk in blunt language. California’s board tells owner-builders to read them before signing.

As an owner-builder, you assume full responsibility for all phases of your project; if a worker or an unlicensed, uninsured subcontractor is injured, you could be asked to pay for injuries through your homeowner’s policy or face lawsuits; unpaid subcontractors may file mechanics liens

California’s owner-builder warning — California · Board page checked October 29, 2026

Florida makes an owner sign the same warning as a disclosure before the permit issues.

Under Florida Statute 489.103(7), the owner-builder is the responsible party of record, must supervise onsite, must handle FICA and workers’ compensation for unlicensed workers employed directly, and a sale or lease within 1 year after completion is presumed to violate the exemption

Notice what both states are saying. The risk is not the form. It is becoming an employer and a builder on paper without meaning to. If you did not plan to carry payroll, workers’ compensation, supervision, and injury risk, do not sign for them to save time.

The red flag: a contractor who asks you to pull their permit

Angi puts it plainly: a contractor who refuses to pull permits, or says it is not necessary for them to pull them, is waving a definite red flag. LegalClarity gives the usual reasons behind that refusal — no valid license, lapsed insurance, or work the contractor expects will not pass inspection.

Watch for smaller versions of the same flag: a saving that cannot be shown as a bid line, a wish to start before the permit issues, no license number for the application, or a contract silent on permits whose author resists adding one sentence.

A licensed contractor in good standing wants their name on the permit. It proves they stand behind the work. Treat reluctance as information, and get a second bid.

One honest exception exists. On a small job you truly run yourself, with a licensed trade hired for one piece, you may hold the main permit and the trade holds theirs. The test is who is really in charge day to day.

What your contract should say, word for word

Never leave this point to memory or a verbal nod. One sentence does most of the work:

Contractor shall obtain and pay for all building permits, governmental fees, licenses, and inspections necessary to complete the work, and shall close each permit with a passed final inspection.

Then add three supports: name the building and trade permits the job needs, require each permit number before work starts, and hold the final payment until the final inspection passes and the permit shows closed. A permit left open can surface years later at a sale, as our guide on closing an open permit before selling explains.

If the contract is silent on permits, add the sentence before you sign. If the contractor refuses, that refusal answers the hiring question.

How to check that a permit was really pulled

Do not take a spoken yes as proof. Check the record yourself:

1. Search by address on your city or county portal. That record, not a contractor screenshot, is the source of truth.

2. Match three things: the scope matches your job, the status shows issued or active, and the contractor named is the firm you hired.

3. Check again mid-job. Permits can expire if work stalls, and a quick check is cheaper than finding an expired permit after the walls close.

4. Keep the closed record with your house papers. Buyers, lenders, and insurers may ask for it, and a closed permit is clean proof the work was inspected.

If the search shows no permit and work has started, ask in writing the same day. Work without a required permit can bring a stop-work order, a higher after-the-fact fee, and orders to open or remove work, as our guides on building without a permit and retroactive permits set out.

What if the contractor was supposed to pull it and did not?

Pause new work. Call the building department, give the address, and ask what is on file and what the office wants next. A prompt, honest call is almost always cheaper than waiting for an inspector to find the job.

Then read your contract. If it says the contractor will obtain the permits, you have legal recourse for that broken promise, per Angi’s guide above. If it says nothing, the fallout lands on you first — why the contract sentence matters before the job, not after.

Get the fix in writing: who files now, who pays any penalty or higher fee, and when inspections will be called.

Frequently asked questions

Who pulls the permit, the homeowner or the contractor?

For work a licensed contractor is hired to do, the contractor pulls it in their own name. You pull it yourself only for work you do yourself or truly run yourself as owner-builder.

Who pays for the building permit?

You do, usually as a line inside the contractor’s bid. The contractor pays the city as part of filing. Angi lists an average cost of $1,650; the real fee varies by city and job.

Can a homeowner legally pull a permit for a contractor’s work?

In many places the form will be accepted, but signing as owner-builder makes you the party of record for code, inspections, and worker risk, though someone else did the work. Florida and California make owners sign a disclosure saying exactly that.

What happens if my contractor does not pull a permit?

The city can stop the work, charge a higher after-the-fact fee, and order work opened or removed if it cannot be checked. As owner, you face the city first; a contract clause assigning permits to the contractor gives you a claim back against them.

Should the contract say who pulls the permit?

Yes, in one plain sentence, before you sign. Name the permits, require the permit numbers before work starts, and tie the final payment to a passed final inspection and a closed permit.

Is it a red flag if a contractor asks me to pull the permit?

Yes. Angi calls a refusal to pull permits a definite red flag, and the common reasons are a missing license, lapsed insurance, or work that may not pass inspection. Get a second bid from a licensed contractor who will file in their own name.

How can contractors find jobs from newly issued building permits?

By PermitsByCounty Research Team · Published October 18, 2026 · Guides & Answers

QUICK ANSWER

Yes — newly issued building permits are public records, and they are one of the earliest signals that work is about to start. A permit is usually filed days or weeks before a crew breaks ground, and it names the project, the type of work, the value, and the company that pulled it. Contractors who check new permits every week see jobs before they are posted anywhere else — and the raw data is free from the city or county that issued it.

The short version

  • Permits are public and early. Anyone can read them, and they show up days or weeks before work starts.
  • Each permit tells you four things: the job, the trade it needs, the project value, and the company to call.
  • The free method takes about 30 minutes a week. No paid lead service is required to start.
  • Speed matters. The first subcontractor to call is usually the one who makes the short list.

Why do building permits point to real jobs?

A permit is not a rumor, and it is not a “maybe” project. Before a city or county issues a building permit, somebody has already paid a fee, filed plans, and waited for an approval. People do not pay permit fees for jobs they might do someday. An issued permit means the project is real, funded, and cleared to start.

The second reason is timing. Permits are filed before the work begins — while the builder is still lining up crews, ordering materials, and setting the schedule. That gap is your window. By the time a job shows up on a bid board or a lead site, the general contractor often already has two or three names in mind. A fresh permit puts you in front of the builder before that list is written.

Think of it this way: a permit is tomorrow’s work, posted in public today. The only question is whether you look at it this week, or hear about the job after another crew is already on site.

What does one permit actually tell you?

A permit record looks dry at first. It is just a row of fields. But each field answers a question you would otherwise have to guess at:

  • Issued date — the day the permit was approved. This tells you how fresh the job is. Fresh is everything: a permit from this week is a project about to start.
  • Permit type — the kind of work: a new home, a roof, a solar install, a remodel, an electrical upgrade. This tells you whether the job needs your trade.
  • Project value — the dollar value written on the application. A $400,000 new home and a $6,000 water-heater swap are both “permits,” but they are not the same phone call. Value tells you the size of the job.
  • Status — “issued” means approved and ready to build. Permits still “in review” may change or stall, so issued permits are the ones to work first.
  • Who pulled it — the general contractor, builder, or company named on the permit. This is the name that matters. That company hires the trades.
  • Location — the project address or area, so you can judge the drive and stay inside the zone you actually serve.

One rule matters here: use the business facts on the permit — the company, the project, the trade. Never go after a homeowner’s personal contact details. The work flows through the builder or contractor on the record, and that is who you call.

The free method, step by step

Here is the whole method. It costs nothing but a little time each week, and it works in any county that posts its permits online.

Step 1: Pick your county and find its permit search. Most mid-size and large cities and counties publish issued permits on an open-data site or a permit search page, free to anyone. Three examples: the City of Austin posts every issued construction permit in a public dataset (Austin Issued Construction Permits), the City of Houston runs a permit search, and Miami-Dade County offers a permit search as well. Search your county’s name plus “issued permits” or “open data” and you will usually find yours.

5.6 million+ views

The City of Austin’s free Issued Construction Permits dataset alone has been viewed more than 5.6 million times — proof of how many builders, suppliers, and contractors already mine this public data.

Source: City of Austin open data portal · Checked October 18, 2026

Step 2: Filter by issued date and your trade. Set the issued date to the last 7 days. Then filter the permit type to your trade — roofing, plumbing, HVAC, electrical, solar, concrete, whatever you do. A short, fresh list beats a huge stale one every time.

Step 3: Sort by project value, high to low. Big value usually means a bigger job and a builder with a real budget. Work the top of the list first. Small jobs are fine filler work, but they should not eat the time you set aside for this.

Step 4: Write down who pulled each permit. For every permit in your trade, note the general contractor or builder named on it, the project, and the value. Names repeat fast in a local market — after a few weeks you will know exactly who is building in your county.

Step 5: Reach out fast, and keep it short and specific. Call or email the company within days, while the job is still being staffed. Name the project, name your trade, and say when you can start. A simple script that works:

“Hi, this is [your name] with [your company]. I saw the permit come through this week for the [new home / remodel / roof] over on [street or neighborhood]. We do [your trade] work here in [county], we are licensed and insured, and we have room in our schedule starting [date]. Would it help if I sent over a price for the [trade] part of that job?”

That is it. No pitch deck, no pressure. You are not begging for work — you are saving the builder a search.

Step 6: Repeat every week, and track the repeat builders. One week of permits is a list. Ten weeks of permits is a map of your market. Keep a simple note of every builder who pulls permits again and again — those are steady customers hiding in plain sight, and a short check-in call every month or two keeps you first in line when their current sub falls through.

How much work is actually out there?

Short answer: a lot, and it never stops. Permits are filed every working day, in every busy county, whether the economy is hot or slow — roofs still leak, families still remodel, and builders still build.

Mistakes that waste your time

  • Chasing old permits. A permit issued 60 or more days ago usually means the crews are already picked and working. Spend your calls on this week’s list.
  • Calling outside your trade or your area. A commercial permit two counties away is not a lead, it is a distraction. Filter hard: your trade, your ground, your size of job.
  • Going after homeowner personal information. Do not. It is not needed, it is not right, and it can get you in trouble. Work through the general contractor or company named on the permit — they are the ones hiring.
  • Giving up after one week. The first week builds the list. The money shows up when a builder sees your name every week and calls you the day his regular sub is booked out. Quitting early is the most common mistake of all.

A simple 30-minute weekly routine

You do not need a system. You need a habit. Here is the routine, one small block at a time:

  • Monday — pull the list (10 minutes). Open your county’s permit search, filter to the last 7 days and your trade, sort by value.
  • Tuesday — shortlist (5 minutes). Pick the permits worth a call: right trade, right area, real value. Note the builder on each one.
  • Wednesday — make the calls (10 minutes). Use the short script from Step 5. Specific and fast beats long and fancy.
  • Friday — follow up (5 minutes). Call back the no-answers once. Short and polite. Then you are done for the week.

Frequently asked questions

Is it legal to use permit data to find work?

Yes. Building permits are public records, and anyone can read them. Use the business contact details of the company on the permit, follow do-not-call rules, and never collect or use a homeowner’s personal information.

How fast should I call after a permit is issued?

Within a few days. Permits are usually filed 2 to 6 weeks before ground breaks, so a fresh permit means the builder is lining up trades right now. A permit that is 60 days old usually means the crew is already picked.

Do I need to pay for permit leads?

No. The raw permit data is free from the city or county that issued it. Paid services charge for convenience — they gather and sort the same public records for you. If your time is worth more than about 30 minutes a week, paying can make sense; the data itself is never the paid part.

Which permits should my trade watch?

Watch permits in your own trade type first — roofing permits if you roof, solar permits if you install solar. Then watch new-construction and remodel permits with real project value, because those jobs need almost every trade.

What if my county has no online permit search?

Call the county or city permit office and ask for the issued-permits list. Many offices post a weekly file or will email one. Ask for permits issued in the last 7 days, with the permit type, project value, and the company that pulled each one.

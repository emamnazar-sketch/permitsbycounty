How do I find plumbing jobs from building permits?

By PermitsByCounty Research Team · Published November 18, 2026 · Guides & Answers

QUICK ANSWER

You find plumbing jobs from building permits by watching building permits, not plumbing permits. Each week, pull new-home, addition, remodel, and commercial build-out permits in your area. Keep the ones with a general contractor named and no plumbing company named yet — those are your plumbing leads from building permits. Rank those builders by how many permits they filed in the last few months, then call with one real job in hand and ask to get on their bid list.

TL;DR — the whole post in 20 seconds

  • Do not start with the plumbing permit. The company on that permit already has the job.
  • Start with the building permit. New homes, bathrooms, kitchens, and build-outs all need plumbing, and the plumber is often not picked yet.
  • The best sign is a GC named and no plumber named. Paid services sell that exact screen.
  • Rank builders by permits filed in the last 3 months. Work the middle of the list first.
  • Call with one of their real jobs in hand, send your license and insurance the same day, and ask for the next bid list.

Why the plumbing permit itself is too late

Most plumbers look in the wrong place first. They search for plumbing permits, find a water heater or rough-in permit, and call about it. That job is gone. The company that pulled the plumbing permit is the company doing the work.

The open job showed up earlier. It showed up when the builder filed the building permit for the house, the addition, or the shop. At that point the plans show kitchens, bathrooms, and a water line, but no plumbing company has been picked yet. That gap is the whole chance.

Think about the order on a normal job. First comes the building permit. Then site work and framing start. Then the trades get booked so rough-in can happen on time — plumbing goes in early, before the walls close. If you wait for the plumbing permit, you missed the booking step. If you show up at the building permit, you are on time.

That is why companies pay for this screen. BuildLink ATX, an Austin permit-alert service, screens commercial building permits for projects with no plumbing permit on file yet for that trade. BuildIntel sells New York plumbing project leads built from permits. City Permit Leads sorts new permits by trade, including plumbing, and emails them to subs. Three sellers, same idea: read the building permit first, and move fast.

What to look for on the permit

Open a building permit and read five things. Each one takes seconds once you know the order.

First, the type of work. New construction, an addition, a bathroom or kitchen remodel, an ADU, and a commercial tenant build-out all need plumbing work. A re-roof or a fence does not. Spend your time on the first group. Bathrooms and kitchens matter most for plumbers — every added bathroom is rough-in, supply, drain, and fixtures.

Second, the address and the ZIP code. Work you can reach beats a long list you cannot. Map a week of permits and you will see clusters — a new street going up, a row of remodels in one area. Clusters mean one trip can cover several calls, and your crews stay close together.

Third, the declared value. The value on the permit is what the applicant told the city, not a final price. Still, it sorts the list fast. A $500,000 new home and a $12,000 bath refresh are different plumbing jobs. Use the value to pick the size of job your crew wants.

Fourth, who pulled the permit. In most offices the record names the builder or general contractor, often with a phone number. That name is your call. TarSignal, which reads North Carolina permit offices, puts it plainly: the permit names the address, the kind of work, the declared cost, and in most offices the contractor and a phone number.

Fifth, and most important, who is not named. If the record names an electrician or an HVAC company but no plumber, the plumbing slot may still be open. If it already names a plumbing company, move on. Do not spend a call on a filled job.

Plumbing leads from building permits: the weekly routine

You do not need to check permits every day. You need a steady routine you will keep.

Pick one morning a week. Monday works well because it catches the prior week in one batch. Pull the new building permits for your county or city for the last 7 days. Keep new homes, additions, ADUs, bathroom and kitchen remodels, and commercial build-outs. Drop anything that already names a plumbing company.

Now rank what is left by builder. Count how many permits each builder filed in the last 3 months, not just this week. A builder with one permit may be a homeowner or a one-off. A builder with eight permits in a quarter is a repeat buyer of plumbing work. That count is your priority list.

TarSignal gives a real picture of how lopsided that list can be. In Mecklenburg County, North Carolina, in September 2026, 608 new-home permits were filed by 143 different builders. Six builders filed 20 or more homes each. The other end matters too: 82 of the 143 builders filed just one home. Big production builders tend to work with trade partners they already have. The small and mid-size builders in the middle are the ones most likely to take your call and try a new plumbing company.

Write down three things for each builder on your short list: the count, one current job address, and the phone number from the permit. That is all you need to make a good call.

Shovels, a permit-data service, notes why speed matters here: building permits are filed 2 to 6 weeks before ground breaks, and months before a project shows up in industry reports. A fresh permit means the builder is lining up trades right now. A permit that is 60 days old usually means the crew is already picked.

How to make the call

Do not open with a pitch about your company. Open with their job.

Say the address. Say the work. For example: you saw their permit for a new home on a named street, you do rough-in and finish work for homes that size, and you want to bid the next one. That one sentence proves you did your homework. A plain call about looking for work does not.

Then ask for the bid list by name. Ask who handles preconstruction or estimating, and ask to be added to their bidders list for plumbing in your area. TarSignal describes the same step: ask the estimator or preconstruction manager to add you, and send your license, insurance, and a few similar jobs.

Send that packet the same day. Keep it short: license, insurance certificate, two or three similar jobs with values, and the areas you cover. If a builder asks for safety records or references, send them fast. Speed at this step is part of the test. Builders watch how you handle paper before they trust you with a schedule — and plumbing sits on the critical path, because rough-in has to pass before the walls close.

If the builder has no work open right now, ask when the next job bids. Put that date in your calendar and check that builder’s new permits the week before it. You will know about the job before the call if you keep watching the permits.

PermittedJobs, a paid permit-alert service for subs, works the same way: it sends new building permits filtered to your trade each weekday morning, with the general contractor’s name and phone number, and tells subs to call the GC and bid direct. The service sells the sorting. The call is still yours to make.

Service and replacement work is a second lane

New work is not the only plumbing work in the records. Water heater swaps, re-pipes, sewer lines, and gas lines need a plumbing permit in most places, and those permits tell you two useful things.

First, they show which companies are busy near you. If one shop pulls a lot of replacement permits in your ZIP codes, that is your real competition, named in public. In New York, BuildIntel lists the most active firms on plumbing projects over 12 months — Donato Plumbing, Inc with 194 projects, All Pro Plbg & Htg Inc with 190, and Par Plumbing Co., Inc with 170, on its count. You can see who holds your market without guessing.

Second, they show the streets and subdivisions where pipes and heaters are old enough to fail in batches. Homes built in the same year tend to need new water heaters and re-pipes in the same few years. You cannot call a homeowner from a permit record in most places — and you should not expect contact details the record does not carry. Use the pattern instead: pick your service area and your mail and ad timing from where the old work actually is.

Common mistakes

The first mistake is waiting for the plumbing permit. Covered above, and it is still the most common one. The permit that names the plumbing company is a record of a decision, not an open job.

The second mistake is calling the biggest builders first and stopping there. The top few builders file a lot of permits, but they also have the longest-standing trade partners. Work the middle of the list: builders filing three to fifteen jobs in a quarter, in your area, at your job size.

The third mistake is chasing every remodel. A paint-and-floor refresh with no bathroom or kitchen work has no plumbing in it. Read the scope before you call. If the permit shows no new fixtures, no moved drains, and no added bathroom, skip it and save the call for a job that needs you.

The fourth mistake is a list with no follow-up. One call rarely lands a bid list spot. Check each short-list builder’s new permits each week, and call again when a new job that fits you shows up. The second call is easier because you can name the new job too.

The fifth mistake is trusting a record without a quick check. Permit data can lag, a scope can change, and some offices publish no contractor at all. TarSignal notes that some offices publish no contractor, and a homeowner’s own permit names none. BuildLink ATX says the same thing from the other side: no trade permit on file does not prove the GC has not already hired someone. If the record is thin, say so in your notes and move on. Do not fill in gaps you cannot see.

Free way or paid way

You can do all of this free. City and county permit portals are public, and PermitsByCounty county reports are free. The cost is your time: an hour or two a week once you know your portal.

Paid services charge for speed and filtering. PermittedJobs charges for a weekday morning email filtered to your trades, with homeowner jobs removed. City Permit Leads lists a Trade List at $49 a month for one trade and a City Master List at $99 a month. BuildIntel and Shovels charge more for bulk permit data you can filter yourself. None of them has secret permits. They sell the sorting. If your hour is worth more than the fee and the filter is the exact one you need — GC named, no plumber yet — paying can make sense. If not, the free routine above gets you the same records.

Frequently asked questions

How do I find plumbing jobs from building permits?

Watch building permits, not plumbing permits. Each week, pull new-home, addition, ADU, remodel, and commercial build-out permits in your county. Keep the ones with a general contractor named and no plumbing company named yet. Rank those builders by permits filed in the last 3 months, then call with one real job in hand and ask to get on their bid list.

Where do I look up building permits for plumbing work?

On your city or county permit portal, and in the free PermitsByCounty county report for your county. Search by date and type of work first, not by the word plumbing. The early jobs are filed as building permits. The plumbing permit comes later and usually names the company already picked.

What is the best sign that a plumbing job is still open?

A general contractor named on a new building permit with no plumbing company named yet, and no plumbing permit on file for that address. Paid lead services sell that exact screen because it marks the gap between a job being filed and the plumber being booked. If a plumber is already named, the job is filled.

Should I call big builders or small builders first?

Call the middle first. The biggest builders file the most permits but tend to use trade partners they already have. Builders filing a handful of jobs in a quarter are easier to reach, and the owner or estimator often takes the call. TarSignal found 82 of 143 builders in one county-month filed just one home, so the list is mostly small builders.

Can I get service and replacement leads from permits too?

You can learn from them, with a limit. Water heater, re-pipe, and sewer permits show which companies are busy and which streets have old systems failing in batches. Most records do not give you a homeowner’s contact details, so use the pattern to pick your service area and timing, not as a call list.

Is it worth paying for a permit lead service?

It can be, if the service filters the way you work and your time costs more than the fee. PermittedJobs sells a weekday digest filtered to your trade, and City Permit Leads lists $49 a month for one trade. Both sell sorting and speed, not secret data. The same permits are public and free if you run the weekly routine yourself.

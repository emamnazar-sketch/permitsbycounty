How much does a building permit cost in Hillsborough County?

By PermitsByCounty Research Team · Published November 15, 2026 · Guides & Answers

QUICK ANSWER

In unincorporated Hillsborough County, a new single-family home permit costs $1.00 per square foot under roof, with a $380 minimum, and a $50 application fee is built into the plan review half of the fee. Flat trade permits are simpler: a general residential building permit is $130, roofing is $250, and a pool or spa permit is $475. Those figures come from Hillsborough County’s own Building Permit Fee Schedule, effective October 5, 2024, checked November 15, 2026. Inside the cities of Tampa, Temple Terrace, or Plant City, the city sets its own fees instead.

TL;DR — the whole post in 20 seconds

  • New homes and additions are priced by the square foot: $1.00 a square foot for a single-family home in unincorporated Hillsborough County, with a $380 minimum.
  • Most single-trade residential jobs carry a flat fee: $130 for a general building, electrical, mechanical, or plumbing permit, $250 for roofing, $475 for a pool or spa.
  • The county splits each new-construction fee in half: plan review first, inspection at issuance. The $50 application fee sits inside the plan review half.
  • Extra charges are real but small and listed: $125 for a reinspection, $140 for a residential plan revision, $50 to extend, transfer, or reinstate a permit.
  • Work done without a permit costs double. The county adds a penalty of 100% of the permit fee.
  • These are county fees for unincorporated areas only. Tampa, Temple Terrace, and Plant City run their own building departments and their own fee tables.

Hillsborough County building permit fees, in plain terms

Here’s what most people really want to know. They’re not after a fee schedule for its own sake. They just want to know what their own job will cost before they call a contractor or file anything.

The county prices jobs two ways. Which one you get depends on the work.

The first way is by the square foot. New construction, additions, and alterations are charged on the area under roof. A single-family home, which the schedule calls R-3 residential, pays $1.00 per square foot. Multi-family buildings pay $0.86. Business and mercantile buildings pay $0.85. Assembly, educational, institutional, and hazard occupancies pay $1.09. Factory, storage, and utility buildings pay $0.60.

The second way is simpler: a flat fee. Most repair and swap jobs — a roof, a water heater change-out, a pool, a demolition — have one set price in the schedule, and it’s the same whether the house is big or small.

One thing to sort out first, before any number matters: the county schedule only covers unincorporated Hillsborough County. That’s most of the county by land, including places like Brandon, Riverview, Valrico, Carrollwood, Lutz, and Apollo Beach. If the property sits inside Tampa, Temple Terrace, or Plant City, that city issues the permit and charges its own fees. A Tampa mailing address doesn’t settle it. Plenty of Tampa addresses are in the unincorporated county, and some Plant City addresses are too. Check the parcel first, then price the job.

What a new home or addition costs

Take a 1,800-square-foot single-family home. At $1.00 a square foot, the building permit fee is $1,800. That is the county’s permit fee for the building permit itself. It already covers the building, mechanical, electrical, and plumbing disciplines for that project, because the schedule says the square-foot fee includes those four disciplines.

Now picture a small addition, say 200 square feet. The math says $200, but there’s a floor. The minimum fee for R-3 residential work under this part of the schedule is $380, so the 200-square-foot addition pays $380, not $200. Small jobs hit the minimum all the time. Worth knowing before you budget around the per-foot rate alone.

Commercial minimums are higher. For occupancies other than R-3 residential and utility, the minimum is $500.

The county also prices partial work. A shell project — the structure without the finished interior — is charged at 67% of the total building permit fee. A phased project charges 40% for the foundation stage and 80% for the completion stage. Builders who phase work use these lines a lot, and homeowners almost never hear about them until the invoice shows up.

And the permit fee isn’t the whole county bill. The schedule itself notes that other fees can apply, such as fire and natural resources fees, depending on the scope. On top of that, new development in the county is assessed mobility and impact fees before a building permit is issued. Those are separate charges, set by their own ordinances, and they can be much larger than the permit fee on a new home. The county keeps a residential calculator for them. If you’re pricing new construction, treat the permit fee and the impact and mobility fees as two different lines, because they are.

Flat fees for the jobs homeowners actually pull

Most homeowner jobs never touch the square-foot table at all. They’re in the trade and miscellaneous tables, at flat prices.

For a single-family home in the unincorporated county, the common lines are these. A general building permit, for building work not listed elsewhere, is $130. Roofing is $250. Demolition is $190. An electrical, mechanical, plumbing, or gas permit is $130 each. Replacing exterior doors or windows, up to 20 openings, is $130; changing the size of the opening raises it to $250. Stucco, exterior plaster, and siding are $190. A generator is $250, or $320 with gas. A photovoltaic solar permit is $250.

Then there are the yard projects. A deck, shed, carport, pool enclosure, or screen room filed as a miscellaneous residential project is $380. An in-ground pool or spa is $475. An above-ground pool or spa is $150. A manufactured or mobile home set-up is $600, and a modular building installation is $720.

Commercial flat fees run higher on the same work. A general commercial building permit is $190, roofing is $380, demolition is $330, each trade permit is $190, a miscellaneous project such as a deck or carport is $500, and a commercial pool or spa is $875.

An example makes this clearer. Say a kitchen remodel is filed as three separate trade permits: building at $130, electrical at $130, and plumbing at $130. That stack adds to $390 in permit fees. A remodel filed instead as an alteration under the square-foot table is priced on area, with the $380 residential minimum. Which route your job takes depends on the scope, and the county’s answer on your specific plans is the one that counts. But you can see the shape of the bill before you file — and that’s the point.

How and when you pay

The county splits a new-construction fee into two halves. Half is plan review, and half is inspection. The plan review half is due when the plans go out for review. The inspection half is charged when the permit is issued.

Inside that plan review half sits a $50 application fee, due when you submit. So on the 1,800-square-foot home above, the $1,800 fee breaks into $900 for plan review (with the $50 application fee inside it) and $900 for inspection at issuance. You don’t pay the whole thing on day one, which helps with cash flow on bigger jobs.

Trade permits work differently. A trade permit covers a single trade, needs inspections but no plan review, and the whole fee is due at submission. Miscellaneous permits, which can cover more than one trade and do go through plan review, include the $50 application fee in the listed price.

All permit fees also carry the surcharges Florida law requires. The schedule says so in plain words at the end: all permitting fees will be assessed surcharges as required per established Florida Statutes. Those surcharges are small next to the fee itself, but they’re why the number at the counter can come out a few dollars higher than the schedule line you looked up.

If a project changes hands or stalls, the admin fees are modest and fixed. Extending a permit application costs $50. Extending the permit itself costs $50. Transferring a permit costs $50. Reinstating an expired permit costs $50. A plan revision costs $140 on a residential job and $350 on a commercial one. A reinspection costs $125 a visit, residential or commercial.

The penalty line, and the private-provider discount

Two lines in the schedule matter more than any fee in it.

The first is the penalty for work done without a permit. The county charges an additional 100% of the permit fee. In plain terms, the permit costs double when the county finds the work before you file. A $250 roofing permit pulled after the fact costs $500 in fees, before anyone talks about corrections. Pulling the permit first is the cheaper route every time.

The second is the private-provider discount. Florida lets applicants hire a licensed private provider for plan review or inspections instead of waiting on the county. When you do, the county’s own fee drops, because the county is doing less of the work. If a private provider does plan review only, the county charges 75% of the total permit fee. If a private provider does inspections only, the county charges 50%. If a private provider does both, the county charges 25%. You still pay the private provider separately, so this is a trade of county fees for speed and scheduling control, not a saving on its own. On a time-sensitive commercial job, that trade can be worth it.

There are also a few big-ticket admin lines worth knowing before you need them. A temporary certificate of occupancy costs $530 on a residential job and $825 on a commercial one. A preliminary plan review meeting costs $500 residential and $1,125 commercial. An after-hours inspection costs $310 residential and $375 commercial. None of these show up on a simple job. They all show up on a job that’s behind schedule — exactly when surprises hurt most.

How long the fee side takes

Fees are only half the cost of a permit. Time is the other half.

PermitPlace’s Hillsborough County guide, last updated in March 2026, puts residential plan review at 10 to 15 business days and commercial plan review at 15 to 45 business days, depending on the project. Those are the guide’s figures for unincorporated county work, and how long yours takes will depend on how complete the application is. A clean set of plans moves. A set that comes back with corrections starts a new round.

You apply through HillsGovHub, the county’s online portal. Development Services, the county department that issues these permits, sits at 601 E. Kennedy Boulevard in Tampa. Applications, inspections, and records all run through that portal now, so set up the account before the day you need to file.

Budget for both together. A homeowner pulling a $250 roofing permit is mostly buying two to three weeks of process, not a $250 product. A builder pricing a commercial job should carry the permit fee, the likely correction rounds, and the review window in the same estimate.

Common mistakes that raise the bill

The first mistake is pricing the wrong jurisdiction. A job inside Tampa city limits filed with the county, or the reverse, wastes the application step and can waste the fee timing too. Confirm the parcel’s jurisdiction before you price anything.

The second mistake is forgetting the minimums. A small addition priced at the per-foot rate looks cheap on paper and then lands on the $380 residential floor. Run the minimum check on every square-foot estimate.

The third mistake is leaving out the second bill. Impact and mobility fees on new development, fire or natural resources fees where they apply, and the state surcharges all sit outside the headline permit fee. None of them are hidden. They’re just on different pages — which is why they catch people off guard.

The fourth mistake is starting early. Work that begins before the permit issues is work without a permit, and the county doubles the fee for it. On a tight schedule, the temptation is real. The math doesn’t care.

The fifth mistake is letting a permit lapse and assuming it is gone. Reinstatement after expiration is a $50 line in the schedule. Ask about reinstatement before you budget a brand-new application.

Frequently asked questions

How much is a building permit for a new house in Hillsborough County?

For a single-family home in the unincorporated county, the building permit fee is $1.00 per square foot under roof, with a $380 minimum. A 1,800-square-foot home works out to $1,800 in permit fees. Impact and mobility fees, where they apply, are charged separately and can be larger.

How much does a roofing permit cost in Hillsborough County?

A residential roofing permit is $250 in the county schedule. A commercial roofing permit is $380. If the roof work was done without a permit, the county adds a penalty of 100% of the fee, so the residential roofing permit would cost $500 in fees.

Do I pay the whole permit fee when I apply?

Not on a new-construction permit. The county splits that fee in half: the plan review half, which includes a $50 application fee, is due when plans go in, and the inspection half is due when the permit is issued. Trade permits are different. The full trade fee is due at submission.

Does the City of Tampa use the same fees?

No. The county schedule in this post covers unincorporated Hillsborough County only. Tampa, Temple Terrace, and Plant City each run their own building department, with their own portals and fee tables. A Tampa mailing address does not tell you which one covers a property. Check the parcel.

How much is a pool permit in Hillsborough County?

An in-ground pool or spa permit is $475 for a residential job and $875 for a commercial one. An above-ground residential pool or spa is $150. A pool enclosure or screen room filed as a miscellaneous residential project is $380.

What does it cost if my permit expires?

Reinstating an expired permit is listed at $50 in the county schedule, the same as a permit extension or a transfer. That is the admin fee. If inspections or plan changes are also needed, those carry their own lines, such as $125 for a reinspection.

How can real estate agents find listings from new-home permits before a house hits the MLS?

By PermitsByCounty Research Team · Published November 21, 2026 · Guides & Answers

QUICK ANSWER

Yes — watch the new-home permits. Builder permit leads for realtors start with one public record: the building permit a builder pulls before construction starts. That permit names the builder, the lot address, and the type of home, and it is public months before the finished house reaches the MLS. A single-family home took an average of 8.8 months from permit to completion in the Census Bureau’s 2025 Survey of Construction, as reported by the National Association of Home Builders — so an agent who reads permits meets the builder at the start of that clock, not at the end of it.

Why a permit beats the MLS by months

The MLS tells you about a house when the builder is ready to sell it. The permit told you about the same house before the slab was poured.

Think about the order of events on a spec home — a house a builder starts without a buyer already lined up. First the builder buys or controls the lot. Then plans get drawn. Then the builder files for a building permit, and the city or county issues it. Only then does dirt move. Framing, roofing, systems, finishes, and final inspections follow. The listing usually shows up near the end of that run, when the builder wants buyers to see a nearly finished product.

So the permit is the first public sign that a specific house, on a specific lot, by a specific builder, is coming. If you sell homes in that area, that sign is worth more to you in week one than in month seven. In week one you can still become the agent attached to that house. In month seven you are one of forty agents calling about a listing somebody else already holds.

This is not a trick or a loophole. Permit records are public records. Builders know they are public. The edge goes to the agent who bothers to read them on a schedule instead of once in a while.

What one new-home permit tells you

A permit record is short, but for an agent it carries the four facts that matter.

It names the builder or the company that pulled the permit. That is your contact target — the decision maker who will pick a listing agent or sell to your buyer.

It gives the lot address and usually the ZIP code. You can drive the street, check what sold nearby, and judge whether the finished house fits your buyers.

It states the type of work — new single-family home, duplex, teardown and rebuild, or an accessory unit. New construction and teardown permits are your listings pipeline. A water heater permit is not.

Many records also carry a declared value. Treat that number as the builder’s stated construction figure, not a future list price. It still tells you the rough tier of the finished home, which tells you which of your buyers to keep in mind.

What a permit does not carry matters too. It does not promise the house will be sold through an agent, or sold at all. Some builders sell their own homes. Some spec homes get a buyer mid-build. And a permit can stall — the builder may build slowly or let the permit expire. Read permits as early signals, not as sure things.

Builder permit leads for realtors, step by step

Here is the working routine. It takes one short session a week once it is set up.

First, pick your ground. One county, or the part of one county where you actually sell. Permit volume is high in growing counties, and a tight area keeps the list short enough to work by hand. In Travis County, Texas, our own records counted 422 new-construction permits issued in October 2026 alone. Nobody works 422 leads. The agents who win with this method work the slice of the county they know street by street.

Second, pull the new-home permits on a fixed day each week. You want permits issued in the last seven days, filtered to new residential construction and residential teardowns. Skip remodels, additions, repairs, trade permits, and pools. Those records matter to contractors. They are noise for listing work.

Third, sort by builder. Group the week’s permits by the company name on them. A builder with one permit is a one-house call. A builder with five permits in your area is a small pipeline, and pipelines are where listing agreements come from. In that same October 2026 Travis County count, the 422 permits named 132 different builders and contractors — most of them small, which is exactly the group that needs agent help.

Fourth, do the two-minute check before you call anyone. Drive or look up the lot. Note what is around it, what similar homes sold for, and whether the builder already runs listings with another agent. Two minutes of homework turns a cold call into a local conversation.

Fifth, reach out while the site is still dirt. Introduce yourself as the agent who sells that street. Bring one useful thing: a recent comparable sale, a buyer you have who wants that area, or a straight read on what the finished home should list for. You are not asking for a listing on day one. You are becoming the name the builder knows before the listing decision gets made.

Sixth, keep a simple watch list. Builder name, lot address, permit date, and one line on where the build stands. Check back at framing and again near the finish. Builders remember the agent who followed the house, not the agent who called once in the first week.

Which permits are worth an agent’s time

Not every new permit deserves a call. Three kinds carry almost all the value.

Spec homes come first. A spec builder starts houses without a signed buyer and sells them during or after the build. Those homes need buyers, and most need an agent to find them. A builder pulling repeated new-home permits in the same area, with no sales office and no big brand behind the name, is the clearest signal on the page.

Teardowns come second. A demolition permit on a residential lot, or a teardown paired with a new-home permit, usually means an older house is coming down and a new one is going up in a built-out area where buyers already want to live. Infill builders work this pattern street by street, and a local agent who knows the street is worth real money to them.

Small builders come third, and this is a sorting rule more than a permit type. Big production builders run their own sales teams and their own listings. The builder with two to ten homes a year usually does not. Your odds of becoming that builder’s agent are far better, and one small builder can hand you several listings a year.

Skip the rest. Owner-builder permits, where the homeowner pulls the permit to build their own house, rarely turn into listings — that owner is building to live there. Remodel and addition permits mean an owner is improving a house, not a builder creating one to sell. Keep them out of your list so the list stays worth reading.

What to say at the first meeting

Keep it plain and local. You are not pitching a program. You are a neighbor who read a public record and knows the street.

Say who you are and where you sell. Name the street, not just the city. Builders can tell in ten seconds whether you know the area or you are working a script.

Say what you saw. You noticed the new-home permit on the lot, you know what the last two homes on nearby streets sold for, and you have buyers asking about that pocket. Every part of that sentence is checkable, which is why it works.

Offer something small and real. A pricing read on the finished house. A walk-through opinion on finishes buyers in that area pay for. An introduction to a buyer who wants new construction in that ZIP code. One concrete offer beats a promise to work hard.

Then ask one question: how do you usually handle sales on your homes? If the builder already has an agent, you have lost nothing — you still know about the house months before your buyers see it listed, and you can bring a buyer instead. If the builder sells alone and sounds tired of it, you are in the right conversation at the right time.

Mistakes that waste the head start

Calling too late is the big one. A permit you read three months after it issued still beats the MLS, but the builder may have picked an agent in those three months. Read weekly.

Working too wide is the second. An agent who downloads a whole metro’s permits drowns in records and calls nobody. A tight area, worked every week, beats a big list worked once.

Treating every permit as a listing is the third. Some of these houses will sell with the builder’s own agent, some to a buyer the builder already has, and some will stall. Your real product is early knowledge: you know what is being built in your streets before almost anyone else. That knowledge wins buyer consultations even when it never wins the listing itself.

The last mistake is going quiet. Builders start homes all year. The agent who checks in at permit, at framing, and at finish is the agent standing there when the listing decision gets made.

Frequently asked questions

How do I find homes before they are listed for sale?

Watch new-home building permits in your county. A permit is public when it issues, names the builder and the lot, and comes months before the finished house reaches the MLS. Filter to new construction and teardown permits, sort by builder, and work that short list every week.

How far ahead does a building permit come before a house is finished?

On average, a single-family home took 8.8 months from permit to completion in the Census Bureau’s 2025 Survey of Construction, and homes built for sale averaged about 7.4 months. Your head start is most of that window if you read the permit in its first week.

Do builders listed on permits already have a real estate agent?

Some do. Large production builders usually run their own sales teams. Small and mid-size builders often do not, and they are the names worth your time. Even when a builder has an agent, knowing about the house early lets you bring a buyer before the listing goes public.

What does it cost to get builder permit leads as a realtor?

Doing it yourself costs only your time: county permit records are public. Paid feeds sort the records for you. PermittedJobs charges $99 a month for weekday realtor alerts and caps each county at one realtor partner, as checked November 21, 2026.

Are teardown permits worth tracking?

Yes. A residential teardown usually means a new home is coming on that lot, often in a built-out area with strong buyer demand. Teardown and new-home permits together are the core of an infill watch list.

Is it legal and fair to contact builders from permit records?

Permit records are public records, published by the city or county that issued them. Contacting a builder about a public project is ordinary business outreach. PermitsByCounty publishes business and permit facts only — never homeowner names or personal contact data.

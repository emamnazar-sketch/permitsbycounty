Do I need a permit to install solar panels?

By PermitsByCounty Research Team · Published November 16, 2026 · Guides & Answers

QUICK ANSWER

Yes — almost everywhere. Just about every home solar install in the United States needs a building permit and an electrical permit from your city or county, per LegalClarity’s solar permit guide checked November 13, 2026. A lot of cities bundle the two into one solar permit application, but both reviews still happen. You’ll also need your utility’s written Permission to Operate before the system can run, and that’s a separate approval — it’s not part of the permit. Plan on the permit fee running about $100 to $500 for a standard home system in most places.

TL;DR — the whole post in 20 seconds

  • Yes. A rooftop solar install needs a building permit and an electrical permit in almost every city and county.
  • The building side checks the roof: added weight, racking, and fire rules. The electrical side checks wiring, grounding, and rapid shutdown.
  • Many places bundle both reviews into one solar permit. A battery or a main panel upgrade can add its own permit.
  • Passing the city inspection isn’t the last step. Your utility has to give you written Permission to Operate before you turn the system on.
  • In SolarAPP+ cities a standard system can get its permit the same day. The full run from application to Permission to Operate is often four to eight weeks.

Do I need a permit to install solar panels on my roof?

Yes. Start there, then have your own building department confirm the details for your address.

The U.S. Department of Energy puts it plainly on its rooftop solar page: permitting and inspection are required before a solar array is allowed to produce electricity on the grid, and local governments generally require the installer to get a permit for rooftop panels before they can be installed. After the work, a local inspector checks the array, and only then does the utility connect the system so it can start making power.

Permitting and inspection are required before a solar array is allowed to produce electricity on the grid; local governments generally require installers to obtain a permit for rooftop panels before installation, followed by a local inspection and then the utility connection

The number of extra approvals changes by address. The permit itself does not.

That goes for a ground mount and a carport array, too. The mount changes — the need for a permit doesn’t. Ask before you buy, not after the panels are already on the roof.

Why does solar need two permits, not one?

Because a solar array is really two kinds of work at once. It’s a structure bolted to your roof, and it’s a small power plant wired into your house.

The building permit covers the structure. Panels, racking, and wiring add about 3 to 4 pounds per square foot to a roof, and a system that covers a few hundred square feet can add 1,000 pounds or more. The reviewer checks whether the roof can carry that load, how the racking attaches, and whether the array meets the fire rules for rooftop systems.

Solar panels, racking hardware, and wiring typically add 3 to 4 pounds per square foot to a roof, and a system covering a few hundred square feet can reach 1,000 pounds or more in total added weight

The weight the building review is checking — United States · Guide checked November 13, 2026

Source: LegalClarity — Do I Need a Permit for Solar Panels? Rules & Requirements · Date pulled: November 13, 2026

The electrical permit covers the power side. The wiring runs from the panels to an inverter and then to your main panel, and it has to meet the National Electrical Code. The reviewer checks wire sizing, breaker ratings, grounding, and conduit runs. One rule catches people off guard: rapid shutdown. If the system shuts down or the grid drops out, the voltage at the panels has to fall to safe levels within 30 seconds, so firefighters can work on the roof without live wires in the way.

A grid-connected system also has to shut itself off during an outage, so it cannot push power into lines crews think are dead. That protection is called anti-islanding, and your inverter needs the UL 1741 listing that proves it.

In a lot of cities, you’ll never see two separate pieces of paper. Both reviews sit inside one solar permit application. That’s a filing shortcut, not a missing review.

What does the permit application need to show?

Your installer normally puts the whole package together. Still, it helps to know what’s in it, because a thin package is the most common reason a solar permit gets kicked back.

GreenLancer’s solar permit guide, checked November 13, 2026, lists what most reviewers expect to see. A site plan and roof layout showing setbacks, fire paths, and where the equipment sits. Electrical one-line and three-line diagrams that show the wiring. Spec sheets for the modules, the inverter, and the racking, with the engineering and attachment details. Structural load calculations covering dead and live loads, wind, snow, uplift, and pull-out forces. And a rapid shutdown and labeling plan that meets the National Electrical Code rules for photovoltaic systems, in Article 690.

Most reviewers expect a site plan and roof layout with setbacks, fire pathways, and equipment placement; electrical one-line and three-line diagrams; module, inverter, and racking spec sheets; structural load calculations for dead and live loads, wind, snow, uplift, and pull-out forces; and a rapid shutdown and labeling plan under NEC Article 690

LegalClarity adds a structural letter to that list: a licensed engineer confirms the roof can handle the added load. Some cities waive it for a standard roof in good shape. Many do not.

Watch for one plain mistake: if the diagram names one inverter model and the spec sheets list another, the reviewer flags it and the file stops moving. Ask your installer to check that every model number matches before the file goes in.

A battery or a main panel upgrade can add its own permit. Our guide to solar permit cost covers the panel-upgrade permit in full. If your quote includes either one, ask which permit covers each piece.

How long does the solar permit take?

It depends on which door your city uses, and the gap between the two doors is wide.

The slow door is a manual plan review. LegalClarity puts that review at a few business days in some places and three to four weeks or longer in others, with battery storage and ground-mounted arrays tending to run long. After the install, most inspections can be scheduled within a few days to two weeks, depending on how busy the department is.

The fast door is SolarAPP+. That is the automated platform built by the National Renewable Energy Laboratory, part of the U.S. Department of Energy. Your installer enters the system design, the platform runs code checks against the building, electrical, fire, and structural rules, and an application that passes gets its permit right away. LegalClarity counts over 345 jurisdictions using the platform.

Over 345 jurisdictions now use SolarAPP+, the automated permitting platform developed by the U.S. Department of Energy’s National Renewable Energy Laboratory; installers enter the system design, the platform runs automated code-compliance checks, and applications that pass receive a permit instantly

The SolarAPP+ fast door — United States · Guide checked November 13, 2026

Source: LegalClarity — Do I Need a Permit for Solar Panels? Rules & Requirements · Date pulled: November 13, 2026

California wrote the fast door into state law. Under Senate Bill 379, most California cities and counties have to run an automated online platform for residential solar up to 38.4 kilowatts, with the big cities and counties due by September 30, 2023 and smaller cities over 5,000 people due by September 30, 2024. NRG Clean Power’s California guide, updated August 5, 2026, says a code-compliant system in a city like Fresno, San Jose, or Stockton gets its permit the same day it is submitted.

California Senate Bill 379 requires automated online permitting for residential solar up to 38.4 kW in most California jurisdictions, with compliance deadlines of September 30, 2023 for large cities and counties and September 30, 2024 for cities over 5,000; a code-compliant system can receive its permit the same day it is submitted

Either way, the permit is only the front end. LegalClarity puts the full run from permit application to Permission to Operate at four to eight weeks in most areas. The install itself is a day or two of work. The waiting sits in the review, the inspection slot, and the utility step that comes last.

Is the permit the same as permission to turn the system on?

No. This is the mix-up that leaves finished systems sitting dark on roofs.

The city or county permit lets you build the system. The inspection says the built system matches the approved plans. Neither one lets you run it. Your utility has to approve the grid connection under an interconnection agreement, and after your install passes the city inspection, the utility does its own review and issues Permission to Operate, often shortened to PTO.

LegalClarity is blunt about the order: you cannot legally turn on your system and send power to the grid until you have PTO, and PTO typically arrives one to three weeks after you pass inspection. NRG’s California guide gives a wider range for the big California utilities, two to six weeks after inspection, and calls the utility step the real timeline driver in 2026. Both guides agree on the rule itself. The system stays off until the utility says yes in writing.

Permission to Operate typically arrives one to three weeks after the municipal inspection passes; the homeowner cannot legally turn the system on or export power to the grid until it arrives

Ask your installer two questions when you sign: Who files the permit, and who files the interconnection paperwork? If the contract only talks about the permit, the PTO step may land on you by default.

Do I need anyone else’s approval besides the city?

Sometimes, yes. Two more gates show up often enough that you should check them before you sign, not after the permit is issued.

The first is your homeowners association. If your home sits in one, you will almost certainly need to send it your plans before the panels go up. Associations mostly care about how the system looks: where the panels sit and whether they show from the street. About 25 states have solar access laws that stop an association from banning solar outright. Even there, fair placement rules are usually allowed. Read your association papers before you sign.

Roughly 25 states have solar access laws that prevent a homeowners association from outright banning solar panels or imposing restrictions that significantly raise costs or reduce system performance; an association can usually still require reasonable rules on placement and appearance

The second gate is a historic district. A preservation board may need to approve where the panels go before the building department issues the permit, and panels that show from a public street get the closest look. Neither gate replaces the permit. They sit in front of it.

Can I install solar panels myself without a contractor’s permit?

You can pull the same permit yourself in a lot of places. What you can’t do is skip it.

Most cities let a homeowner take out an owner-builder permit for a home they live in. The permits and inspections are the same ones a contractor would face. The change is who carries the blame if the work misses code: you do.

The inspection bar doesn’t drop for a self-install, and you’ll file the utility papers yourself. Fail the inspection and it means rework, another fee, and more waiting.

One licensing line matters. In most states, the electrical work has to be done by, or watched by, a licensed electrical contractor. A common middle path is to do the mounting and racking yourself and hire a licensed electrician for the wiring and panel hookup. Ask your building department before you buy a panel: can a homeowner pull an electrical permit for solar here, and is a specific license required for the wiring?

How much does the solar permit itself cost?

The permit is a small line in a solar budget. LegalClarity’s licensing guide puts permit fees for a home system under 10 kilowatts at $150 to $400 in most places, and its homeowner guides give a wider $100 to $500 range, with a few high-cost cities charging more. California caps most home solar permit fees by state law at $450 for a system up to 15 kilowatts, plus $15 for each kilowatt above that. Our solar permit cost guide works through that cap and the city examples in full.

Ask for the permit fee as its own line in the quote. If a quote shows no permit fee at all, ask why. Usually it just means the installer plans to file and pay it.

What happens if I install solar panels without a permit?

The same three problems that follow most unpermitted work, with one extra: a system that cannot legally run.

Code enforcement can issue a stop-work order and fines, and in some places the fines add up daily. The hardest outcome is an order to take the whole system off the roof at your cost. A permit after the fact is sometimes possible, but it costs more, because the department may ask you to open up wiring and mounts that are already covered.

Insurance is the quiet risk. If an unpermitted system causes a fire or roof damage, the insurer has grounds to deny the claim, and that denial can reach past the panels to the house itself.

Sale time is where the gap surfaces. Buyers, inspectors, and title searches tend to find unpermitted solar, and most states make a seller disclose it. A buyer and their lender read it as a risk, and the sale can stall. And through all of it, the utility has no reason to grant Permission to Operate for a system with no passed inspection.

If panels are already up with no permit, call the building department and ask what it needs to bring the system onto the record. Get the answer in writing.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a permit to install solar panels if I own my home?

Yes. Owning the home does not remove the permit. Almost every home solar install needs a building permit and an electrical permit from the city or county, whether a contractor does the work or you pull an owner-builder permit and do it yourself.

Is a solar permit one permit or two?

It is two reviews: a building review for the roof structure and an electrical review for the wiring. Many cities bundle both into a single solar permit application, so you may file once and pay once, but both reviews still happen before the permit issues.

Do I need a permit for a ground-mounted solar array?

Yes, in almost every place. A ground mount still needs the electrical permit, and the building side covers the footing, the racking, and setback rules instead of the roof. Ground mounts often take longer in review than a standard rooftop system.

Can I turn my solar system on as soon as the inspection passes?

No. Passing the city or county inspection is not the last step. Your utility has to finish its interconnection review and give you written Permission to Operate. That usually arrives one to three weeks after the inspection passes, and the system should stay off until it does.

Do I need HOA approval to install solar panels?

In most associations, yes. You send the association your plans before the install, and it reviews placement and street view. About 25 states have solar access laws that stop an association from banning solar outright, but fair placement rules are still allowed in those states.

What happens if solar panels were installed without a permit?

The city can fine you, order the work stopped, or in the worst case order the system removed. A permit after the fact is sometimes possible, but the building department may ask you to open covered wiring and mounts for inspection. The gap can also hurt an insurance claim and stall a later sale.

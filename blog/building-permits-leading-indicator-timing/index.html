How far in advance does a building permit signal new construction demand?

By PermitsByCounty Research Team · Published November 12, 2026 · Guides & Answers

QUICK ANSWER

For most new work, a building permit gives you about 2 to 6 weeks of warning before ground breaks, and months of warning before the big material orders land. Shovels, which sells permit data to suppliers, says building permits are filed 2 to 6 weeks before ground breaks. The Census Bureau's Survey of Construction puts the average wait from permit to start at 1.4 months for a single-family home completed in 2025. That is why a permit is not just an economics phrase. It is a working clock. You can plan stock and crews around it, and time your calls by it.

TL;DR — the whole post in 20 seconds

  • The short clock is weeks: permits are filed 2 to 6 weeks before ground breaks, and the average single-family home started 1.4 months after its permit in 2025.
  • The long clock is months: that same average home took 8.8 months from permit to finished, so demand keeps arriving long after the start.
  • Size changes the clock. Small builds tend to follow a permit in 3 to 6 months at the outside, and large developments can run 24 months or more.
  • A permit has an outer edge. Under the model building code, work that has not started within 180 days leaves the permit invalid.
  • Read every permit with two dates in mind: when ground is likely to break, and when your stage of the work is likely to be ordered.

Two clocks, not one

A permit starts two clocks at once, and mixing them up is where most bad planning starts.

The first clock is short. It runs from the permit to the day work starts on the ground. That is the clock a concrete crew, a site-services firm, or anyone selling the first load cares about.

The second clock is long. It runs from the permit to the day the building is done. Inside that long stretch, each trade and each material gets ordered at its own point. Lumber is not ordered on the same week as cabinets. A permit that looks old to a framing supplier can still be early news to a finish supplier.

So no single number answers the title question. The start has a short answer, and the full build has a longer one. A few checks tell you which one fits the permit in front of you.

How far ahead is the start?

Start with the supplier’s own measure. Shovels tells building-material suppliers, in plain words, how early the signal is.

Building permits are filed 2 to 6 weeks before ground breaks, and months before a project appears in industry reports

How early a permit signal is — United States · Supplier page checked November 12, 2026

Source: Shovels — Contractor Intelligence for Building Materials Suppliers · Date pulled: November 12, 2026

Filed is the key word there. A filing can sit in review, so a filed permit is the earliest sign, and an issued permit is the firmer one. By the time you see an issued permit, part of that window may already be gone. Read permits weekly, not monthly, if your work is in the first stage.

The Census Bureau measures the same gap a different way, from the permit itself to the start, across every single-family home finished in a year. Its average is a bit longer than the Shovels window, and it is an average, not a promise for one job.

1.4 months on average from authorization to the start of construction, for single-family homes completed in 2025; the wait ran from 1.0 month in the East North Central and West North Central divisions to 1.8 months in New England

Average wait from permit to start, single-family homes — United States · Homes completed in 2025

Source: NAHB Eye on Housing, summarizing the U.S. Census Bureau Survey of Construction, published September 8, 2026 · Date pulled: November 12, 2026

Put the two together and you get a working range for a normal house: expect ground to break somewhere between about two weeks and about two months after you see the permit, with the average near six weeks. A permit that is already three months old with no sign of a start is not early any more. It is a permit to check, not a permit to plan stock around without a call first.

How long does the demand keep coming?

The start is only the first order. A house keeps buying for months after ground breaks, and that is the part of the signal most readers underuse.

8.8 months on average from authorization to completion for single-family homes completed in 2025 — 1.4 months to start, then 7.4 months to finish

Permit to finished home, single-family — United States · Homes completed in 2025

Source: NAHB Eye on Housing, summarizing the U.S. Census Bureau Survey of Construction, published September 8, 2026 · Date pulled: November 12, 2026

Who builds the home stretches or shrinks that long clock a lot. The same Census figures, broken down by builder type, show why two permits issued on the same day can mean demand on very different schedules.

Homes built for sale took 7.4 months from permit to completion in 2025; homes built by a hired contractor took about 11.7 months; homes built for rent took about 12.4 months; owner-built homes took 14.3 months

Build time by who builds it — United States · Homes completed in 2025

Source: NAHB Eye on Housing, summarizing the U.S. Census Bureau Survey of Construction, published September 8, 2026 · Date pulled: November 12, 2026

A production builder moves fastest, because the plan and crew are set before the permit lands. An owner building one custom home moves slowest, because choices get made as the job goes. If a permit names a builder you know, plan on the short end. If it names an owner, plan on a long job with quiet gaps, and do not read a quiet month as a dead job.

Why do bigger projects run on a slower clock?

Shovels looked at Florida permit data across 125 jurisdictions and put the size split in one place. Small work follows its permit fairly soon. Large work can sit a long time between the permit and the first day on site, because financing, phasing, and site work all have to line up before a big job can start.

Construction typically follows in 3 to 6 months for smaller builds, and up to 24 months or more for large residential developments; after construction starts, home sales follow in 12 to 18 months

Small builds against large developments — Florida, 125 jurisdictions · Outlook published February 25, 2026

Source: Shovels — Florida Housing Market Outlook: What Building Permit Data Says · Date pulled: November 12, 2026

That 3-to-6-month figure for smaller builds is slower than the 2-to-6-week ground-break window on the supplier page, and the gap is worth naming instead of hiding. The two pages measure different things in different places: one is a national supplier rule of thumb for when ground breaks after filing, and the other is a Florida read of when construction follows across a whole market, slow jobs included. Your county will sit somewhere in that spread. The safe way to use both is this: treat the first weeks as the likely start for a ready, financed, single job, and treat a few months as the honest outer range before you call a permit cold.

Commercial and multi-family work runs slower again, and it also files in pieces. One building can draw a site permit, a shell permit, and then trade permits as each stage is approved. Each later permit is a fresh signal inside the same job, and often a better one, because a trade permit usually means that stage is close, not just planned.

What gets ordered at each stage?

A permit does not mean one order. It means a line of orders, in a fairly fixed order, spread across the build.

Site work and foundation come first, right at the start. Concrete, rebar, grading, portable toilets, fencing, and dumpsters belong to this stage. If you sell any of those, the short clock is your clock, and a permit that is six weeks old may already be late for you.

Framing comes next, and with it the biggest lumber and engineered-wood orders. Roofing and rough-in trades follow the frame. That is when wiring, pipe, and duct work go in, while the walls are still open.

Suppliers of interior and mechanical goods tend to enter later. Construction Monitor, which sells weekly permit data, describes that pattern directly.

Suppliers of concrete, lumber, HVAC systems, and plumbing supplies use permit data to anticipate demand; suppliers often come into play during the mid to later stages of construction, once the foundation and framing are complete, when materials for interiors and mechanical systems are delivered

When suppliers enter a job — United States · Guide checked November 12, 2026

Source: Construction Monitor — How to Leverage Building Permit Data · Date pulled: November 12, 2026

Finishes come last: drywall, cabinets, counters, fixtures, flooring, paint, and appliances. On an average house that finished in 2025, those orders sit five to eight months after the permit, not five to eight weeks. A finish supplier who throws away a permit because it is three months old is throwing away the exact signal they need.

Our guide to how suppliers quote a job before the material order is placed walks that stage order as a quoting method. This post is the clock underneath it: match your stage to the right part of the timeline, and the same permit list reads as early, on time, or late depending on what you sell.

Where does the signal get weak?

A permit is a strong signal. It is not a guarantee, and four things weaken it.

First, some permitted work never starts, or starts much later than planned. Money falls through, a sale falls through, a builder reshuffles a crew. The Census averages count homes that got finished. They say nothing about permits that died, so your own follow-up has to carry that check.

Second, a permit can be kept alive. Under the model building code used across much of the country, a permit becomes invalid if work does not start within 180 days, or if work stops for 180 days. Extensions can be granted in writing for cause, usually in stretches of up to 180 days each. So six months is a useful outer edge for the start signal, not a hard wall.

A permit becomes invalid if the authorized work does not start within 180 days after issuance, or if the work is suspended or abandoned for 180 days; the building official may grant extensions of up to 180 days each on a written request showing cause

The 180-day outer edge — United States, model code · Rule checked November 12, 2026

Source: International Building Code §105.5 model rule, as stated in the Town of Farmington, Connecticut building permit extension form · Date pulled: November 12, 2026

Third, permits and buildings are not the same unit. One permit can cover a building of several homes, and one large building can draw many permits. Count jobs and addresses before you count rows, or a single project will look like a boom.

Fourth, the national numbers hide local speed. The Census wait to start ran from 1.0 month in parts of the Midwest to 1.8 months in New England in 2025, and total build time ran from 7.6 months in the South Atlantic division to 13.5 months in New England. A clock that works in Texas will mislead you in Massachusetts. Build your range from the counties you actually serve, starting with the national figures here and tightening them as your own permit lists age.

None of this makes the signal weak overall. The Census Bureau treats its permit survey as a headline measure for a reason.

The Building Permits Survey is a designated principal economic indicator and the only source of current and consistent small-area data on new residential authorizations; the Conference Board uses it in its index of leading economic indicators, and private businesses use it for market planning, material use, and investment analysis

Why the permit count is a headline measure — United States · Survey description checked November 12, 2026

Source: U.S. Census Bureau — Building Permits Survey, About the Report · Date pulled: November 12, 2026

Note what that survey covers, though: new privately owned housing units only. It does not count remodels, roofs, pools, or commercial work. A county permit list is wider than the national survey, which is why the local list, not the national rate, is the tool for planning a route or a stock order.

How do you plan around the clock?

Read each new permit in four quick passes, and write the dates down. A permit you only glance at gives you none of its timing value.

First pass: note the issue date and the type. A new-home or new-building permit starts the full clock. A trade permit inside a known job starts a much shorter one, because that stage is usually close.

Second pass: size the job from the record. One house, a small building, or a large development? Set the start range from the size: weeks for a single ready house, a few months for a small project that may still be lining things up, and quarters, not weeks, for a large phased development.

Third pass: set two call dates, not one. Set an early call for the stage right after the start, and a later call for your own stage if you sell mid-build or finish goods. On an average house, a finish supplier’s second date lands months after the first, and that gap is the whole point of the signal.

Fourth pass: recheck before you spend. Look for a later permit on the same address, a status change, or an inspection sign that work has started. If the permit is near six months old with no start and no later record, make the check call before you reserve stock or promise a slot. Treat it as a question, not a loss.

Over a few months, this habit gives you your own local clock. You will learn which builders in your counties start in three weeks and which ones sit for three months.

Frequently asked questions

How far in advance does a building permit signal new construction demand?

By weeks for the start and by months for the full build. Shovels puts permit filing at 2 to 6 weeks before ground breaks. The Census Survey of Construction puts the average single-family start at 1.4 months after the permit, and the average finish at 8.8 months after it, for homes completed in 2025. Large developments can signal work 24 months or more ahead.

Is a filed permit the same signal as an issued permit?

No. A filing is earlier and softer, because review can still change or stop the job. An issued permit is firmer: the work is approved and the start clock in the model code, 180 days to begin, is running. Suppliers who read filings get more warning and more dead ends. Readers of issued permits get less warning and a cleaner list.

Why do two sources give different lead times, 2 to 6 weeks and 3 to 6 months?

They measure different sets of jobs in different places. The 2-to-6-week figure is Shovels’ supplier rule for when ground breaks after filing. The 3-to-6-month figure is Shovels’ Florida market read for when construction follows a permit on smaller builds, slow jobs included. Use weeks as the likely start for a ready single job, and a few months as the outer range before you treat a permit as cold.

When should a finish supplier act on a new-home permit?

Later than a foundation or framing supplier, and on purpose. Interiors and mechanical goods tend to enter in the mid to later stages, after foundation and framing, per Construction Monitor. On an average 8.8-month build, that puts finish orders months after the permit. Note the permit when it issues, set a later call date, and do not drop it for being a few months old.

How long can a permit sit before it goes stale?

Under the model building code, a permit becomes invalid if work does not start within 180 days, or if work stops for 180 days, though written extensions of up to 180 days each can be granted for cause. Local rules vary, and some places use a longer first window. As a planning habit, treat any permit near six months old with no start as one to verify with a call before you plan around it.

Does a rise in permits mean prices or sales will rise too?

No, and this post does not claim that. A permit shows that work was approved and is likely to start. It is a supply and activity signal. The Census Bureau uses permit data as a leading economic indicator and businesses use it for market planning and material use, but a permit count alone does not predict prices, sales, or returns on any single job.

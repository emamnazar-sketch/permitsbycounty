How can real-estate investors use building permits to find flips before they list?

By PermitsByCounty Research Team · Published October 28, 2026 · Guides & Answers

QUICK ANSWER

Real-estate investors use building permits in two ways: they watch newly issued permits to see which houses and streets are being fixed up before anything lists, and they watch permits that expired, stalled, or were withdrawn to find owners whose project stopped. One permit alone is a weak signal. In a 2026 BiggerPockets thread, Ohio investors said the value comes from stacking signals — a permit plus a code violation, tax trouble, or a long ownership — and tracking which stacks turn into real conversations.

TL;DR — the whole post in 20 seconds

  • A fresh, large rehab permit on an unlisted house usually means a flip is already under way — that owner is a future buyer more often than a seller.
  • An expired, stalled, or withdrawn permit is the seller signal: work started, money ran short or life changed, and the house sits half done.
  • No permit in many years, stacked with a violation or tax trouble, points to a tired owner. One signal alone is mostly noise.
  • Several mid-size permits on one block tell you money is moving into that street before sale prices show it.
  • Check the record before you offer. Open permits stay with the property, so the last owner’s paperwork can become your cost.

Why permits show up before listings do

A listing is the last step, not the first. Before a house is photographed and priced, someone pulls a permit or stops a job halfway. Each move leaves a public record.

A permit record is plain. It names the address, the kind of work, the stated value, who applied, and the dates. It also shows status: issued, in progress, finaled, expired, or withdrawn. Read in that order, one record tells you what is happening at a house months before a sign goes in the yard. That early look is why people pay for this data sorted.

You do not need to pay that to start. City and county portals publish the same raw records for free. Paid reports save sorting time; they do not hold different facts.

Signal one: a fresh, large rehab permit

Start with size. A small permit is upkeep. A large one is a plan.

Under $10,000 is mostly upkeep such as a water heater or small repair; $10,000 to $75,000 covers kitchens, baths, roofs, and foundations; $75,000 to $250,000 on a single-family house almost always means a full rehab or flip; over $250,000 is new building or a major project

Source: Texas Signals — Reading Building Permits Like a Pro · Date pulled: October 28, 2026

Those bands are one guide’s way of sorting Texas permits, not a national rule. Values are stated by the applicant, and some cities record $0 or $1. Use the bands to sort a list, not to price a house.

Be honest about what a fresh rehab permit means. If a crew is mid-flip, that house is spoken for. Its real uses are three. First, that owner and contractor are active buyers: the same name on several high-value permits marks a serious operator, and the guide above calls that person a possible buyer for your next wholesale deal. Second, the houses next door just got more interesting — their owners have not priced in the rehab beside them yet. Third, the street itself is a signal: a rising count of mid-size and large permits on a block says money is moving in before sale prices prove it.

Signal two: the permit that stopped

This is the closest thing permits give you to a seller.

A permit that is applied for and then withdrawn, or work hit by a stop-work order, can point to a project that went wrong or money that ran short; half-finished work and approved plans that never break ground often lead owners to think about selling

Stopped work as a selling signal — United States · Guide checked October 28, 2026

Source: Brevitas — Turning Public Records into Off-Market Deal Leads · Date pulled: October 28, 2026

Look for three shapes: a permit issued months ago with no inspection since, one that expired with work unfinished, and one withdrawn soon after issue, which often means bids came in higher than planned.

Go and look before you write to anyone. No crew, no dumpster, materials under a tarp — that is a stopped job. If the house looks finished and lived in, the open record may be just a missed final inspection: a paperwork problem, not a seller, though it still matters if you buy the house.

Be a person in this contact, not a script. Many stopped projects sit behind a hard season — a death, a lost job, a vanished contractor. A short, plain note saying you buy as-is, at the owner’s pace, gets replies that pressure does not. If the owner says no, log it and move on.

Signal three: no permits at all, for a long time

The third signal is silence. A house with no permit in many years, owned by the same person for a long time, is often a house nobody has kept up — and keeping it up is the cost its owner is tired of carrying.

On its own, silence proves nothing. Plenty of long-held houses are loved and sound. It becomes useful stacked with something else: a recent code violation, tax delinquency, an owner who lives elsewhere, or work that clearly needs doing. An investor guide on free public data makes the same point: cross-check permit history to flag houses with unpermitted work or no renovation in more than twenty years, then let the other records confirm — https://dev.to/avabuildsdata/free-data-sources-real-estate-investors-are-sleeping-on-and-how-to-actually-use-them-3obk

One more read: a small, fresh permit on a long-quiet house can mean the owner is sprucing it up before a sale. Property data services list renovation permits among the signs they watch for houses being readied for market, alongside an owner’s mailing address differing from the house address. Small and fresh, after years of nothing, is worth a polite early contact.

Stack the signals, then keep score

In a 2026 BiggerPockets thread, an Ohio investor stacking building permits, code violations, and tax delinquency to find sellers before they list said no single signal can be trusted alone; recent code violations within the last 60 days added real signal, and tracking results by signal stack over 20 to 30 contacts showed which stacks were worth paying for

Stacking signals, from working investors — Columbus, Cincinnati, Cleveland, and other Ohio markets named in the thread · Thread checked October 28, 2026

Source: BiggerPockets — What data signals do you trust most for finding off-market deals in Ohio? · Date pulled: October 28, 2026

Copy that habit from day one. Keep a simple sheet: which signals were present, how recent, and three outcomes — did the owner answer, did you have a real talk, did anything reach an offer. After a few dozen contacts, your own numbers tell you which stack works in your streets. A stack that works in Cincinnati may do nothing in Austin.

A workable stack looks like this: a stalled or expired rehab permit plus a house that still looks unfinished (start here); no permits in many years plus a recent violation or tax flag and a long ownership; a small fresh permit after years of silence plus an owner living elsewhere; and, weakest, a full rehab under way next door plus a tired house on the same block — a watch list, not a mailing list.

The steps, in order

  1. Pick one small area and learn it cold. One city, a handful of ZIP codes. Depth in one place beats a thin scrape of ten.
  2. Pull the newest issued permits each week and sort by work and value. Keep rehabs, additions, and demolitions. Set small upkeep aside, unless a cluster of it on one block tells you the stock is aging into work.
  3. Pull the other direction too: old, expired, and withdrawn permits. Most portals let you search by status or date. This list is shorter and, for sellers, usually stronger.
  4. Read each house’s full permit history, not just the newest line. It shows upkeep, neglect, additions, and whether past work was ever finaled.
  5. Stack and score. Add the free violation, tax, and ownership checks. Rank by how many signals overlap and how fresh the newest one is.
  6. Look at the house. In person if you can, before you spend a stamp or a minute of the owner’s time.
  7. Make a short, plain approach. Name the house, say you buy in its current state, give one easy way to reply, and stop. PermitsByCounty does not publish homeowner names or personal contact data, and neither should your list: work from public records and lawful contact routes you find yourself.
  8. Check the record again before any offer. This step saves deals, and it gets its own section below.
  9. Log the outcome against the stack. Every time.

Before you offer, read the flip the way a lender will

Finding a house early is half the job. The other half is not inheriting its permit problem.

Open permits do not go away when the owner sells the house; they stay with the property however many times it changes hands, and the new owner can be on the hook for penalties the old owner ran up

Open permits stay with the house — United States · Guide checked October 28, 2026

Source: NewSilver — How To Check Permits On A House · Date pulled: October 28, 2026

So, on any flip you are buying, compare the listing’s claims against the permit record, room by room. New kitchen, new bath, added space, converted garage: each should leave a record showing a final inspection. Recent major work with no permit at all is a different house than the one in the photos. It can stall an appraisal, a loan, and your own resale.

Price what you find. An open permit with finished work may cost only an inspection visit to close. Unpermitted work can cost a retroactive permit, opened walls, or removal. Our guides on checking permit history before you buy, closing an open permit before a sale, and getting a retroactive permit walk through each fix in order.

What permits cannot tell you

Permits show work, not wishes. They do not tell you an owner wants to sell, what a house is worth, or whether the numbers clear your costs. Coverage varies too: some cities publish full records, some name the contractor and some do not, and work done with no permit leaves no record at all. Treat every permit lead as a reason to check, never as a verdict — the data is only worth what the conversations prove.

Frequently asked questions

Can you really find a flip before it lists using permits?

You can find the house early, yes. A permit is filed before work starts, so a weekly read of new, expired, and withdrawn permits puts you weeks or months ahead of any listing. Whether it becomes a deal depends on the owner.

Which permit signal is strongest for a seller?

A stopped one. An expired, stalled, or withdrawn rehab permit on a house that still looks unfinished points to an owner whose plan failed. A fresh large permit points the other way: that flip is already claimed, and its owner is a buyer to know.

How much does it cost to work permit data as an investor?

The raw records are free on city and county portals. Sorted reports cost money: Cincinnati and Dayton Permit Reports charges $149 a month for its Remodel report and $499 for All-Access, checked October 28, 2026. Your real cost either way is the weekly hour to read, stack, and log.

Do I need to worry about open permits on a flip I buy?

Yes. Open permits stay with the property when it sells, and the new owner can inherit the old owner’s penalties and unfinished inspections. Read the full history before you offer, and price any fix into the deal.

What other records should I stack with permits?

The free ones: code violations, tax status, and ownership length and address. Ohio investors on BiggerPockets in 2026 said overlap plus freshness is where the signal is.

Does PermitsByCounty give me owner phone numbers or emails?

No. We publish public permit and business facts, sorted by county, trade, and month — never homeowner names or personal contact data. Finding a lawful way to reach an owner is your own step.

How can I look up building permits by address for free?

By PermitsByCounty Research Team · Published October 18, 2026 · Guides & Answers

QUICK ANSWER

Yes — in most places it is free. Building permits are public records, and most cities and counties let you search them online by address at no cost. You type the address into the permit search for the city or county that issued the permits, and you can see what work was permitted, when, and whether the permit was closed. Where PermitsByCounty covers a county, we gather those records into one place so you do not have to fight the government portal.

The short version

  • Permits are public records, and the lookup is usually free. Most cities and counties let anyone search them online by address.
  • The trick is searching the right office. The city issues permits inside city limits; the county issues them outside. Search the wrong office and you find nothing.
  • Each result shows the basics: the type of work, the date, the status, the reported value, and the company that pulled the permit.
  • Buying a house? Check before you close. An open permit — or big work with no permit at all — is a red flag worth settling first.

First — who issued the permit? (the step everyone gets wrong)

There is no single national permit database. Every permit is issued by one local office, and that office is the only one holding the record. Inside city limits, the city’s building department issues the permits. Outside city limits — in an unincorporated area, which simply means land that is not part of any city — the county issues them.

This is where most lookups go wrong. You search the county site for a house that sits inside a city, the search comes back empty, and it looks like the house has no permit history at all. The records exist — you were asking the wrong office.

Three examples make it plain. Austin’s permits live in a City of Austin dataset. Chicago’s permits live in the City of Chicago’s data portal. But a house in an unincorporated part of Cook County is not in Chicago’s system — it is in Cook County’s own records. Same metro area, different record books.

The fast way to tell: if the address is inside a city, start with the city. If the city search finds nothing, try the county before you decide the work was never permitted.

Step by step: the free building permit lookup by address

Step 1: Write down the exact address. Get the full street address, the unit number if there is one, and the ZIP code. Permit systems are picky — “104 Main St” and “104 Main Street, Apt 4” can return different results.

Step 2: Find the right search. Here are four real ones, all free: the City of Austin’s Issued Construction Permits dataset, the Chicago Data Portal building permits, the City of Houston’s permit search, and Miami-Dade County’s permit search. If your city or county is not on that list, search the web for your city or county name plus “permit search” — most offices have one.

Step 3: Search by the address. Start with the full address. If nothing shows, widen the search a little at a time: drop the unit number, then the ZIP code, then search the street name alone and scan down the list. Small typing differences hide real records.

Step 4: Open each permit and read it. A list of permit numbers does not tell you much by itself. Open each one — the next section explains every field in plain words.

Step 5: Save what you find. Print the results or save a copy. If you are buying the house, hand the list to your agent or your home inspector. It gives them the exact questions to ask the seller.

How to read what you find

A permit record is a short row of fields. Here is what each one means:

  • Permit number — the office’s ID number for that one permit. If you ever call the office, this is the number to give them.
  • Type — the kind of work: a roof, a remodel, electrical, plumbing, new construction, and so on.
  • Issue date — the day the office approved the permit.
  • Status — where the permit stands. “Issued” means approved, and the work may start. “Finaled” or “closed” means the work passed its final inspection and the file is complete — the clean ending you want to see. “Open” means the work was approved but never got that final sign-off. “Expired” means the permit ran out of time before it was closed.
  • Reported value — the project cost written on the application. It is the applicant’s estimate, not an official valuation of the house.
  • Who pulled it — the contractor or company named on the permit. That is a business fact from the public record.

One boundary worth knowing: a permit is a record about work on a property. It tells you what was built, changed, or fixed — it is not a file on the people who live there.

Buying a house? The two checks that matter

If you are buying, do this lookup before you close, not after. Two things matter most, and both are easy to miss.

Check 1: open permits. An open permit means work was approved and started but never passed its final inspection, so the file was never closed. After you buy, that open file can become your problem: closing it may take inspections, fees, or fixing work you did not do. In Florida, open permits are known for surfacing in the days right before closing, and they can hold up a sale until they are sorted out. Finding them early — while the seller can still deal with them — is the whole point of checking.

Check 2: big work with no permit at all. A new addition, a converted garage, a finished basement — with no permit record behind it — may be unpermitted work, meaning no inspector ever looked at it. That does not prove the work is bad, and this post cannot tell you what it means for your sale. Ask the seller about it directly, and talk it through with your agent or your inspector before you close.

One honest note: we are not lawyers, and permit rules differ from city to city. For anything that could affect a purchase, check with your agent, your inspector, or another local professional who knows your area.

If your search finds nothing

An empty result has four common causes. Work through them before you conclude anything:

  • Wrong office. You searched the city for a county property, or the county for a city property. Try the other office.
  • Older paper records. Many offices keep older permits on paper or in scanned files that never made it into the online search.
  • Address format. The office may record the address slightly differently from how you typed it. Try the variations from Step 3.
  • The work was never permitted. Also possible, especially on older houses and do-it-yourself projects.

The next step is simple: call the permit office and ask. Staff search these records all day, and a records request is free or cheap in most places. Ask for the full permit history for the address, and ask how far back their records go.

Frequently asked questions

Is it really free to look up building permits by address?

Yes, in most places. City and county permit searches online are free to use, and the records themselves are public. The only cost that ever shows up is a small copy fee when an office has to pull and copy an old paper file for you.

Can I see permits for a house I don’t own?

Yes. Building permits are public records, so anyone can look them up — buyers, neighbors, contractors, anyone. You do not need the owner’s permission, and you do not need a reason.

How far back do permit records go?

It varies by office. Big cities often have 10 to 30 or more years of permits searchable online. Older records usually still exist, but they sit in the office’s paper or scanned files — you request those from the permit office directly.

What does an open permit mean for a house sale?

An open permit means work was approved and started but never passed its final inspection, so the file was never closed. It can delay a closing until it is resolved, and after the sale it can become the buyer’s problem. Ask your agent how it is handled in your area before you close.

Will the owner be notified that I looked up their permits?

No. Permit records are public, and searching them is not reported to the property owner. Offices do not send notices when someone views a public permit record online.

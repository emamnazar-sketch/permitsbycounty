Do I need a permit for a kitchen remodel in Palm Beach County — and how much are the fees?

By PermitsByCounty Research Team · Published November 14, 2026 · Guides & Answers

QUICK ANSWER

Yes — for most real remodels, you do. If your kitchen job touches plumbing, wiring, gas, or a wall — say you’re moving a sink, adding circuits, running a gas line, or taking out a wall — you’ll need a permit in Palm Beach County. That’s per the 2026 Palm Beach County kitchen fee guide, checked November 14, 2026. Paint, flooring, and new cabinets in the same layout, with nothing behind the walls changed, usually don’t need one. Fees are based on the value of the work, with a $75 minimum per permit, and a mid-range kitchen valued at $35,000 to $70,000 usually pays about $800 to $2,500 all-in across the building, electrical, plumbing, and mechanical permits.

TL;DR — the whole post in 20 seconds

  • Move any plumbing, wiring, gas, or a wall and you’ll need a permit. Only changing how the kitchen looks? You usually won’t.
  • Fees are based on the value of your work, and each permit has a $75 minimum.
  • A mid-range kitchen — $35,000 to $70,000 in value — usually pays about $800 to $2,500 for the full set of permits.
  • Outside city limits? You’ll file through the county’s ePZB portal. Inside a city like Boca Raton, you file with the city instead.
  • Skip the permit and the county can charge you double fees — and that open record can slow down a later sale.

Do I need a permit for a kitchen remodel in Palm Beach County?

It depends on what you’re changing, not on what the room is called.

Florida Building Code Section 105.1 says you need a permit for work on a building, and for work on its electrical, gas, mechanical, or plumbing systems. A kitchen packs all four into one small room. That’s why most full remodels end up needing permits — even when the cabinets alone wouldn’t need one.

PermitFlow’s Palm Beach County guide (checked November 14, 2026) puts it in plain local terms: you need a county building permit if you’re building, altering, renovating, demolishing, or adding to a building — and its no-permit list includes swapping kitchen cabinets without touching the wiring or plumbing.

A Palm Beach County building permit is required to build, alter, renovate, demolish, or add to an existing building, or to install plumbing, HVAC, or electrical equipment in one; replacing kitchen cabinets without reconfiguring electrical or plumbing is listed as work that does not require a permit

So before you sign a contract, ask one question: does this job move, add, or extend any pipe, wire, gas line, duct, or wall? If the answer’s yes for even one of them, plan on a permit for that part of the work.

Which kitchen work needs a permit, and which does not?

Cosmetic work that leaves everything where it sits is usually exempt. Painting cabinets, swapping cabinet doors, adding a backsplash, replacing a faucet in the same spot, swapping an appliance on the same hookups — all of that sits on the no-permit side of the line in the Florida guides we checked for this post.

Work that changes how the kitchen runs sits on the other side of the line. Moving or adding a sink? That’s plumbing. Running a new gas line for a range is gas work. Adding or moving outlets, adding circuits, or upgrading the panel is electrical. Moving a range hood duct is mechanical. Taking out a wall — or opening the kitchen into another room — is structural.

Cabinets are where most people get tripped up. Swap the boxes in the same spots, with no plumbing or wiring changes, and you may not need a permit at all. But add an island with a new circuit, move a sink into that island, or put a dishwasher where there wasn’t one, and those parts of the job do need permits.

Replacing cabinet boxes in the same locations, with no plumbing or electrical changes, may need no permit; an island with a new circuit, a sink moved to the island, or a new dishwasher brings plumbing and electrical work that does need permits

One more Florida rule helps small jobs — though it rarely helps a kitchen. Under Florida Statutes Section 553.79(1)(g), cities and counties have to exempt work valued at less than $7,500 on a single-family home from a building permit. But the exemption doesn’t cover electrical, plumbing, structural, mechanical, or gas work, and it doesn’t apply if the home sits in a FEMA Special Flood Hazard Area. A paint-and-hardware job can fit. A remodel that moves a sink can’t.

Work valued at less than $7,500 on a single-family home is exempt from a building permit in Florida, but the exemption excludes any electrical, plumbing, structural, mechanical, or gas work and does not apply to a home in a FEMA Special Flood Hazard Area; the exemption took effect in Collier County’s process on July 1, 2026

If your job sits anywhere near that line, get the building department’s answer in writing before work starts. A verbal yes at the counter is hard to prove later.

How much are the fees in Palm Beach County?

There’s no single flat kitchen fee. The county charges for each permit your job needs, and each fee is figured from the value of the work — labor plus materials.

A Boca Raton remodeler’s 2026 guide (checked November 14, 2026) reports the county schedule this way: the official Palm Beach County Planning, Zoning and Building fee schedule, updated March 2, 2026, lists residential alterations — kitchen remodels included — as priced by value, with a $75 minimum. And that minimum is per permit, not per job. A kitchen that needs four permits starts at four minimums before the value part even gets added.

Palm Beach County kitchen remodeling permit fees in 2026 start at a $75 minimum for most residential alterations and are calculated based on project valuation; the county fee schedule, updated March 2, 2026, lists residential alterations as calculated by value with a $75 minimum

For a mid-range kitchen valued between $35,000 and $70,000, the same guide breaks it down like this: the building permit runs about $400 to $1,200, the electrical sub-permit about $150 to $400, the plumbing sub-permit about $150 to $350, and a mechanical permit for a range hood about $75 to $200. Add it up and a typical job with electrical, plumbing, and a range hood lands between $800 and $2,500 in total permit costs.

For a kitchen valued at $35,000 to $70,000: building permit about $400 to $1,200, electrical sub-permit about $150 to $400, plumbing sub-permit about $150 to $350, mechanical permit about $75 to $200; total permit cost for a job with electrical, plumbing, and a range hood usually $800 to $2,500

PermitFlow’s county guide also lists an older fee ladder you can use to check the math. As of December 2023, the base construction fee was $75 for the first $2,000 of value, plus 2.50% of the next $8,000, plus 1.75% of the next $90,000, plus 1.00% of the next $200,000, and 0.75% of the rest. On a $50,000 job, that works out to $75 plus $200 plus $700 — a $975 base fee, right inside the building-permit range above. The county now works out the real fee in its portal when you file, so treat the ladder as a sanity check, not a quote.

By scope, the Boca guide puts a cosmetic job at $0 to $75; a cabinet-and-countertop swap in the same layout at $300 to $600; a mid-range remodel with a new layout at $800 to $1,800; a full gut renovation at $1,800 to $3,200; and a luxury custom kitchen at $3,000 to $5,500 or more. High-end work valued over $100,000 can push permit costs past $3,000 on its own. Those numbers cover just the city or county fees. What your contractor charges to prepare and run the permit is separate — the same guide puts that handling cost at about $200 to $500 on many jobs. Ask to see it as its own line.

Failed inspections add small fees that grow fast: the county guide lists $50 for a first re-inspection, $100 for a second, and $200 for a third. Fail three times on one trade and you’ve paid about as much as that trade’s permit cost.

County or city — where do I file?

Here’s the Palm Beach County trap — it catches people every week. The county Building Division only issues permits for unincorporated areas. If your home is inside a city, that city runs its own building department, its own portal, and its own fees, even though every job follows the same Florida Building Code.

If you’re unincorporated, you’ll file through the county’s ePZB portal — it has a fee estimator, so you can price the job before you file. In Boca Raton, you file through Boca eHub, not ePZB. The Boca guide puts Boca Raton’s first plan review at 5 to 30 days, with each correction round adding about 20 to 25 days, while a straightforward unincorporated county kitchen takes about 5 to 15 business days.

West Palm Beach shows the same split from the city side. A 2026 West Palm Beach guide (checked November 14, 2026) says most kitchen remodels there need a permit, work touching plumbing, electrical, gas lines, or structure has to be permitted and inspected through the City of West Palm Beach Building Division, and fees commonly land between $200 and $800 depending on scope. Same county, different office, different range.

In the City of West Palm Beach, a kitchen remodel that touches plumbing, electrical, gas, or structure must be permitted and inspected through the city Building Division, with permit fees commonly between $200 and $800 depending on scope; a cosmetic swap of countertops and hardware may not require a permit

Before you file anywhere, check which government covers your address. Filing in the wrong system doesn’t hold your place — it starts the clock over.

What do I need to file, and what happens next?

Get your paperwork together before you open the portal. The guides we checked for this post all list the same core items: a completed permit application with a clear written scope and the value of the work, floor plans showing the old and new layouts, an electrical plan if the wiring changes, a plumbing diagram if fixtures move, and your contractor’s license and insurance on file.

One Florida paper step catches people who haven’t built here before: the Notice of Commencement. On a job over $5,000, you’ll need a certified copy of the recorded Notice of Commencement in place before the first inspection. You record it with the Palm Beach County Clerk of Courts. Without it, that first inspection doesn’t happen — even if the permit’s issued and the work’s done.

From there, inspections follow the work. Rough electrical and rough plumbing get checked before the walls close. A hood duct is checked as mechanical work. The final inspection closes the set. Keep that closed permit record — it’s the paper a buyer, a lender, or an insurer will ask for later.

One small step can save you real money. Before you file, run your job’s value through the county fee estimator in ePZB — or ask your city for its own estimate — and compare it with the permit line in your contractor’s quote. The two numbers should sit close. If the quote’s permit line is way above the estimate, ask what’s inside it. Handling time, drawing work, or a cushion for re-inspections are all fair charges, but you should see them named. And at the end, keep every fee receipt with the closed permit. Years later, that thin folder is what proves your kitchen was permitted, inspected, and finished on the record.

Thinking of pulling the permit yourself as an owner-builder? The county and Boca Raton both allow it for a home you live in. You’ll sign an owner-builder affidavit, take on code compliance yourself, and Florida law limits a quick resale: an owner-builder can’t sell the home within one year of the final inspection without disclosing that they did the work. For a full gut kitchen, most homeowners let their licensed contractor pull the permits in the contractor’s name.

What happens if I remodel without a permit?

The county makes you pay for that choice twice over. The Boca guide says it plainly: start work without a permit in Palm Beach County and you’ll face doubled fees, plus stop-work orders and possible fines. Some nearby cities in the county charge more — one city guide here lists a penalty of up to three times the permit fee, plus the fee itself.

And the money is only part of it. Unpermitted kitchen work sits in the public record as a gap, not a closed permit. Title searches look for open and unpermitted work before a closing, and our guide to closing an open permit in South Florida shows how often that search lands 10 to 14 days before the closing date. A buyer can ask for a price cut, an escrow holdback, or a closed permit before money moves. An insurer can also question a claim tied to wiring or plumbing that was never inspected.

If the work’s already done, call the building department that covers your address and ask what it needs to get the kitchen on the record. Expect to file the same permits after the fact, pay the higher fee, and open up finished work wherever an inspector can’t see the pipes or wires any other way.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a permit to replace my kitchen cabinets in Palm Beach County?

Usually no — not if the new cabinets sit in the same layout and you’re not reconfiguring the electrical or plumbing. PermitFlow’s county guide lists that exact job as work that doesn’t require a permit. But if the cabinet job adds an island circuit, moves a sink, or adds a dishwasher, those parts do need permits.

How much does a kitchen remodel permit cost in Palm Beach County?

Fees are based on the value of the work, with a $75 minimum per permit. A mid-range kitchen valued at $35,000 to $70,000 usually pays about $800 to $2,500 in total across the building, electrical, plumbing, and mechanical permits. A cosmetic job can pay nothing at all, and a luxury kitchen can run $3,000 to $5,500 or more.

Where do I apply for a kitchen permit in Palm Beach County?

If your home is in an unincorporated area, you’ll apply through the county’s ePZB portal. If it’s inside a city, you apply with that city — Boca Raton uses Boca eHub, and West Palm Beach uses its own Building Division. Check which government covers your address before you file.

How long does a Palm Beach County kitchen permit take?

A straightforward unincorporated county kitchen usually takes about 5 to 15 business days for its first plan review. Boca Raton runs about 5 to 30 days for the first review, and each correction round can add about 20 to 25 days. The main thing in your control is filing a complete application the first time.

Do I need a Notice of Commencement for a kitchen remodel?

Yes, for most jobs over $5,000. You’ll need a certified copy of the recorded Notice of Commencement in place before the first inspection, and you record it with the Palm Beach County Clerk of Courts. Without it, the inspector won’t do the first inspection.

What happens if my kitchen remodel fails an inspection?

You fix what the inspector flagged and pay a re-inspection fee — the county guide lists $50 for a first re-inspection, $100 for a second, and $200 for a third. Your permit stays open until the final inspection passes, and an open permit can slow down a later sale.

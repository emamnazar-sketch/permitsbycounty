How can I find out if my neighbor has a building permit?

By PermitsByCounty Research Team · Published November 17, 2026 · Guides & Answers

QUICK ANSWER

Yes, you can look, and it’s free. Building permits are public records, and any person can search them without giving a reason. Go to the permit portal for the city or county that covers your neighbor’s address, search by that street address — try the parcel number if the address finds nothing — and open the record. It shows the work that was approved, who pulled the permit, the dates, and the status. If no record shows up, that alone doesn’t prove anything: the work may not need a permit, the record may not have posted yet, or you may be in the wrong city’s system.

TL;DR — the whole post in 20 seconds

  • Permits are public records. You don’t need to own the house, live next door, or give a reason to look one up.
  • Start with the street address. If that fails, try the street number and name without the suffix, then the parcel number from the county assessor.
  • Read four things on the record: the description of work, who pulled it, the issue date and expiry, and the inspection status. Finaled or finalized is the clean end.
  • No record can mean no permit, no permit needed, a portal lag of days or weeks, or the wrong city or county system. Check those before you worry.
  • If work that plainly needs a permit has no record, the next step is the building department — by phone, an online form, or 311 where a city has it. The department, not this post, decides what happens next.

You are allowed to look

This is the part people get wrong. They feel like checking a neighbor’s permit is snooping, so they either do nothing or they go straight to a complaint. It’s neither. A building permit is a public record, the same kind of record as a deed. The building department issued it, the file sits in a public system, and any person can read it without giving a reason.

That cuts both ways, and it helps to know it. Your neighbors can look up your permits too. That’s not a threat. It’s how the system is built to work — the work on a house is visible to the street it sits on, and the paper behind it is visible too.

You don’t need the owner’s name to start, and you don’t need their permission. You need one thing: the address. A parcel number helps when the address search fails. Both are public too.

Get the address and the parcel number first

Write down the full street address of the house, exactly as mail is addressed to it. Then, if you can, get the parcel number too. Every county tax assessor gives each piece of land its own number — it is called an assessor’s parcel number, a parcel ID, or a tax parcel number, depending on the county. You can find it free on the county assessor or property appraiser site by searching the same address.

Why bother with a second number for the same house? Because addresses are messy in real systems. Units share one address. A street was renamed years ago. The portal stored the address one way and you typed it another way. The parcel number points at the land itself, so it cuts through all of that. It’s your backup key, and on some portals it’s the better first key.

The owner’s name can help on the few portals that let you search by owner, and the assessor page shows that too. You do not need it for most searches. Address first, parcel second, owner only if the portal asks.

The search, step by step

Step one: find the right portal. Search the web for the city or county name plus the words building permit search or permit lookup. The first result from a government site is almost always the right one. Quality varies a lot. Some portals are clean and fast. Some look ten years old and still work fine.

Step two: name the right office before you type anything. A house inside a city is permitted by that city. A house outside the city line is usually permitted by the county. The two records don’t cross. This is the most common reason a neighbor search comes back empty — the permit was in the other system the whole time. Houston is a clean example: the City of Houston runs its own Permit Portal, where users can search for permits and follow a permit from submittal to issuance, while an address in unincorporated Harris County sits in the county’s system instead. Miami-Dade makes the same split: the county’s Building Permit Selection Menu searches by permit number, process number, or address for county-jurisdiction properties, and a house inside the City of Miami sits in the city’s own system.

Step three: enter the address the way the portal wants it. Here’s the tip that trips up most people: many systems want only the street number and the street name, without the suffix. Type 123 Main rather than 123 Main Street or 123 Main St. Miami-Dade says the same thing in its own words — enter the address with no city, state, or ZIP, and use the abbreviation rather than spelling out street, avenue, or road. If the full address finds nothing, shorten it. If the address still finds nothing, use the parcel number.

Step four: read the list, not just the first line. The results show every permit on file for that property — a permit number, a short description of the work, the date it was issued, and a current status for each one. An older house can carry a long list. Find the one that matches the work you are seeing now, by date and by description. Click into that record for the full details.

Step five: check for a second database. Some places split their records by date, with older permits in a separate archive or legacy search. Chicago is open about this limit: its Building Permit Application Status site covers applications started in the last 3 years, or 36 months, and older applications and issued permits may not appear there — the city’s permit records search and its open data are the route for older work. If the work you are asking about is older, look for the archive before you call the search done.

Step six: note what you found, with the date you looked. Permit number, description, status, and the day you checked. Portals change as inspections post, so a note with a date is worth more than a memory.

How to read what you find

Finding a permit is half the job. Reading it is the other half.

The description of work is the boundary of what was approved. It uses plain terms — new roof, bathroom remodel, a deck of a stated size. Read it against what you can see. If the crew next door is building a second-story addition and the only permit on the address says re-roof, those two things do not match. That mismatch is a fact you can state. What it means for that house is a call for the building department, not for either neighbor.

The record also names who pulled the permit — usually the licensed contractor — often with a license number, plus the property owner’s name. Many records list an estimated value for the work, as reported on the application. A value that fits the work you see is a quiet sign the filing was honest about scope.

The dates tell you where the job sits in its clock. Every permit has an issue date, and most places set an expiry window. A common pattern is that a permit goes void if work has not started within six months to a year of issuance, or if work stops for a similar stretch. A permit issued two years ago with no inspection activity since then has almost certainly expired, and the holder would need to renew or reapply before carrying on. Austin shows the public side of this plainly: its Austin Build + Connect Public Search needs no registration, and a person can search and view general permit information for active permits without an account.

The inspection history is often the most useful part. Jobs are inspected at set stages — foundation, framing, electrical rough-in, plumbing, and a final walk-through — and the record tracks each one as passed, failed, or still pending. You’re not reading whether the work is good. You’re reading whether the city’s own checks have happened.

Then the status pulls it together. Active means the permit is paid for and work is under way. Suspended means it went a long stretch without a passed inspection. Work stopped means the department ordered a halt. Expired means it was never completed and is no longer valid. Finalized — some systems say finaled — means all inspections passed and the project is complete. That’s the clean end you want to see on any house on your street, yours included.

Does that work even need a permit?

Before you spend worry on an empty search, ask whether the work you are seeing is the kind that needs a permit at all. Rules vary by place, but the pattern is steady across the country.

Work that usually needs a permit: room additions, garages and carports, and changes to load-bearing walls; new construction; electrical panel upgrades and new circuits; plumbing changes; HVAC installs and water heater replacement; roofing; decks; fences in many areas; pools; sheds above a certain size; window or door work that changes the opening; kitchen and bath remodels that move plumbing or electrical; finishing a basement; and demolition of walls, outbuildings, or structures.

Work that usually does not: painting, flooring, swapping cabinet hardware, and playground equipment. A patio poured at grade without footings often sits outside the rules too.

If the work next door is paint and flooring, an empty permit search is the right answer, not a warning sign. If it is a room addition with new plumbing, an empty search means more. When you’re not sure which side of the line a job falls on, the building department can tell you whether that kind of project needs a permit in your area. That’s a question they answer every day, and asking it isn’t a complaint.

If your search shows nothing

Work through the plain reasons first, in this order.

The work may not need a permit. See the list above.

The record may not have posted yet. Online databases don’t all update in real time. A permit issued yesterday might not show for several days, or even weeks, in some places. Active work with an empty portal can be a lag. It can also be no permit at all — the lag explains a gap, it doesn’t settle it. Wait a week and search again before you treat empty as final.

You may be in the wrong system. City and county records don’t cross, and a suburb runs its own. Miami-Dade County alone holds 34 municipalities besides the unincorporated area, each with its own building department for its own addresses — the county search showing nothing for a City of Miami address is expected, not suspicious. Search the other system.

The address format may be off. Shorten it, drop the suffix, try the parcel number, and check whether the portal splits old records into an archive, the way Chicago’s status site stops at 36 months.

The record may be offline. If a place has no usable portal, or the file you want is not posted, go in person. Bring the address, the owner’s name, and the parcel number. Staff can pull the file and let you review it on site, and copies usually cost a small per-page fee, typically under a dollar. You can also file a written public records request. Every state has its own open records law — it goes by names like Public Records Act, Open Records Act, or Sunshine Law — and that state law, not the federal Freedom of Information Act, is the route for a local building department. Describe exactly what you want, for example all building permits issued for that address in the last five years. Most states require an agency to respond within five to ten business days, though the exact timing and fees vary.

If the work looks like it needs a permit and there is none

First, what this post won’t do: it won’t call your neighbor’s work illegal. Whether a specific job needed a permit, and what follows if it did not have one, depends on the local code, the exact scope, and facts only an inspector on site can check. That call belongs to the building department.

What you can do is plain, and it’s the standard route. Contact your local building department — by phone, through an online form, or by calling 311 if your city has that service. State what you searched, on what date, and what work you can see from your own property. You generally don’t need proof that no permit exists. The department checks its own records and, if it decides to, sends an inspector to look.

Two honest notes about that step. Some places let you file without giving your name, but policies vary, and full anonymity is not always guaranteed, because complaint records can become public once a case closes. And an inspector’s visit is a check, not a verdict you get to direct — the department decides what it finds and what it requires.

If an inspector does confirm work was done without a required permit, the usual path is that the department can order work to stop, and the owner then has to apply for a permit after the fact, bring the work up to code, and pass the required inspections. In serious cases a department can order part of the work torn out and redone, and fines vary by place and can add up if an order is ignored. That’s the department’s process, described here so you know what a call sets in motion — not a promise about any one house.

There is also a smaller step that costs nothing and often works better. Talk to your neighbor first, if you can do it calmly. Plenty of empty searches end this way: the permit is real and sitting in the other city’s system, or it posted a week after you looked, and a two-minute talk finds it. A permit number ends the question for both of you.

The HOA layer, if you have one

If your street sits in a homeowners association, there may be a second approval on top of the building permit. Most HOAs require an architectural review application before exterior work like additions, fences, or paint changes. That approval is separate from a city or county permit. Having one doesn’t mean the owner has the other, and a project may need both.

In most states, HOA members have a right to inspect official association records. If you want to know whether a project got HOA approval, you can put a written request to the association’s management company or board, following the process in the governing documents. Keep the two files apart in your head: the HOA file tells you about the association’s rules, and the permit file tells you about the building department’s approval. Only the second one answers the question in this post’s title.

Frequently asked questions

How can I find out if my neighbor has a building permit?

Search the permit portal for the city or county that covers your neighbor’s address, using that street address. Try the street number and name without the suffix if nothing comes back, then the parcel number from the county assessor. Open the record that matches the work and read its description, status, and inspection history. Anyone can do this — permits are public records and no reason is needed.

Is it legal for me to look up my neighbor’s permit?

Yes. Building permits are public records in every state, and looking them up is a normal public search, online, in person, or by a written records request under your state’s open records law. You do not need the owner’s permission and you do not have to say why you are looking.

What if I search and find no permit for my neighbor’s work?

Do not jump to a conclusion. The work may not need a permit, the record may not have posted yet — some portals lag days or weeks — you may be searching the wrong city or county system, or the address format may be off. Shorten the address, try the parcel number, search the other system, and look again in a week. If work that plainly needs a permit still shows no record, ask the building department.

Can I find out who pulled my neighbor’s permit and what it covers?

Yes, that is on the record. A permit shows a description of the work that was approved, the name of who pulled it — usually the licensed contractor, often with a license number — the owner’s name, the issue date, and often an estimated value. The description is the boundary: work well outside it is not covered by that permit.

What does it mean if my neighbor’s permit says expired or suspended?

Expired means the permit was never completed and is no longer valid — the clock ran out, commonly after six months to a year with no start or no progress. Suspended means it went a long stretch without a passed inspection. Neither status proves anything about the quality of the work. It means the file is not closed, and the holder would generally need to renew or reapply to carry on under it.

Should I report my neighbor if I cannot find a permit?

That is your call, and a calm talk first solves many of these — the permit is often in the other system or posted late. If you do act, the route is your local building department, by phone, an online form, or 311 where a city offers it. Say what you searched and what work you can see. The department checks and decides. Do not expect to stay fully anonymous everywhere — complaint records can become public after a case closes.

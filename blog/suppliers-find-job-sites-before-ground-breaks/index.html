How can building-material suppliers find job sites before ground breaks?

By PermitsByCounty Research Team · Published October 26, 2026 · Guides & Answers

QUICK ANSWER

Building-material suppliers find job sites before ground breaks by watching newly filed and newly issued building permits in the places they deliver to. Shovels, checked October 26, 2026, says building permits are filed 2 to 6 weeks before ground breaks, and months before a project shows up in industry reports. Filter those permits by project type, trade, and place, rank the builders who pull them again and again, and call while the job is still early enough that the material orders are not all placed yet.

TL;DR — the whole post in 20 seconds

  • A permit is an early signal, not a late one. It is filed 2 to 6 weeks before ground breaks, per Shovels.
  • One permit record can give you the project address, the type of work, the builder or contractor name, and the approval date. Some records also show a project value.
  • Filter first. Keep only the project types and trades that buy what you sell, in the area your trucks can reach.
  • Rank builders, not just jobs. A builder with ten permits this season is worth more of your time than a one-time permit.
  • Time the call to the material. Concrete and framing come early. Roofing, mechanical, and finish materials come later, so a permit can feed calls for months.

Why do permits show a job site so early?

A builder cannot start most work legally until the permit is in hand. So the permit comes first, and the digging, framing, and deliveries come after. Shovels puts a number on that gap. It is the clearest public timing figure we found for this question, from a company that sells permit data to suppliers.

Building permits are filed 2 to 6 weeks before ground breaks, and months before a project appears in industry reports or distributor surveys

Two to six weeks is not a lot of time. It is enough to get a name, make a call, and get a quote in. It is not enough to check permits once a month. Suppliers who do well with this check often, in small batches, and act the same week. A permit also means a real project, with a real address and a builder who has paid a fee and filed plans — not a rumor or a rendering in a news story.

What does one permit record actually tell a supplier?

You do not need a fancy system to read a permit. A dealer guide for building-materials sellers lists what a single filing usually carries.

A permit filing gives the exact project address, what work is being done, the contractor name, and when the permit was approved, and sometimes the project value

What one permit record carries — United States · Dealer guide checked October 26, 2026

Source: SalesJack — How to Find Active Construction Projects: A Guide for Building Materials Dealers · Date pulled: October 26, 2026

Read those fields like a sales rep. The address tells you if the job is inside your delivery area. The work type tells you which materials it will need, and in what order — a new house needs concrete, then framing, then roofing, while a roof permit needs a short list fast. The contractor name tells you who to call, and if that builder is already your customer. The approval date tells you how fresh the lead is. The project value, when a city publishes it, helps sort big jobs from small ones. Not every city publishes a value, and some are not reliable, so use it to sort, not as a promise of spend.

How do you turn permits into construction leads for suppliers, step by step?

This is the working routine. Most suppliers who fail at this do not fail because the data is bad. They fail because nobody owns the routine, so it stops the first busy week.

Step 1: Pick your ground and your products first. Write down the counties or ZIP codes your trucks serve well, and the two or three product lines you most want to grow. A lumber yard, a concrete plant, and an HVAC supplier should not work the same list.

Step 2: Filter new permits every week. Pull the newest permits for your ground. Keep new construction, additions, and the remodel and trade permits that use your products. Drop the rest. A fence permit does not help a framing supplier. Deleting fast is part of the job.

Step 3: Sort by builder, not just by job. Group the permits you kept by contractor name. Shovels tells suppliers to filter by trade type, project category, and geography to surface active builders. That same sort works with a free county report and a simple sheet. The builder with steady work is worth a visit, a price sheet, and a second call.

Step 4: Time your call to the material stage. Construction Monitor notes that suppliers often come in during the middle and later stages, after foundation and framing, when interior and mechanical materials are delivered. So if you sell concrete or framing, call the week the permit lands. If you sell HVAC, plumbing, roofing, or finish goods, note the permit now and call when that stage is close. One permit can give you two or three well-timed calls.

Step 5: Call with the job in your hand. The SalesJack guide makes this plain: knowing the address, the project type, and the start lets you call with something useful to say, not just ask if they need lumber. Name the job, name what you stock for that stage, and ask when they want pricing. Builders get vague calls all day. A call about their own permitted job stands out.

Step 6: Put the follow-up on a calendar. Tie the next check-back to the build stage, not a random day. A short list you actually work beats a long list you printed once and forgot.

This does not only work in a few big cities. Permit data is published city by city and county by county, and the pool is large — but uneven, which matters. More on that in the limits below.

What are the limits a supplier should know before starting?

First, coverage is uneven. The SalesJack guide says not every town reports permits with the same speed or care, and rural markets often have gaps. If your best town barely publishes permits online, this method will be thin there. Check your own ground before you build a routine on it.

Second, a permit is early, but not always first. A fast builder may have lined up key suppliers before the permit posts — the same guide calls permit data a slightly trailing signal. You are most often catching a job early in the build. That is still early enough for most product lines.

Third, volume can bury you. Thousands of permits can be filed across a mid-sized territory. If your filtered list is too long to call in a week, your filters are too wide. Narrow the ground or the project types until it fits the hours you have.

Fourth, names need care. The same builder can appear under slightly different business names, and a permit may name a general contractor while buying for your product sits with a trade contractor not named yet. Confirm who buys your line on the first call.

Last, permit records are public business facts about a project. They are not homeowner contact lists, and this site does not publish homeowner names or personal contact data. Your customer is the builder on the job.

Frequently asked questions

How early can a supplier find a job site from a permit?

Shovels says permits are filed 2 to 6 weeks before ground breaks. Check new permits weekly. A permit seen the week it posts gives you time to call before most later-stage orders are placed.

What should a supplier look at first in a permit record?

Start with the address, the type of work, the contractor name, and the approval date. Those four fields tell you if the job is in your area, if it needs your products, who to call, and how fresh it is. A project value, when the city publishes a reliable one, helps you sort big jobs from small ones.

Which suppliers benefit most from permit watching?

Construction Monitor names concrete, lumber, HVAC systems, and plumbing supplies as lines that use permit data to plan inventory and shipments. Roofing and electrical suppliers benefit too, because those trades pull their own permits.

How do I find the builder if a permit has no contractor name?

It happens. Some permits are filed by the owner, and trade contractors are named later. Check the same address again in a few weeks for trade permits, or stop by the site once work starts and read the job sign. Do not invent a name to fill the gap.

Is it better to buy permit data or use free county reports?

It depends on size. A local supplier in one or two counties can often run the free route. A multi-yard supplier may save enough rep hours with a paid service to justify the cost. Either way, the early call with the job’s details is what wins the order.

Can I use permit data to plan stock, not just sales calls?

Yes. A run of new permits in one area is a demand signal for the weeks ahead. Group your kept permits by product stage and ZIP code, and share that count with whoever orders stock.

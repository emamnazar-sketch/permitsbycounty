Do I need a permit for a deck, pool, or fence — and what will it cost?

By PermitsByCounty Research Team · Published October 29, 2026 · Guides & Answers

QUICK ANSWER

In most U.S. cities, you need a permit for a deck unless it meets all four model-code tests at once — 200 square feet or less, no more than 30 inches above the ground, not attached to the house, and not serving a required exit door — under International Residential Code Section R105.2, checked October 28, 2026. A pool that can hold more than 24 inches of water almost always needs a permit, and a fence needs one in many places once it passes 6 feet, with lower limits in front yards. Angi’s 2026 permit-cost table puts average permit fees at about $100 for a deck, $150 for a fence, $250 for an above-ground pool, and $1,250 for an in-ground pool.

TL;DR — the whole post in 20 seconds

  • A deck is permit-free only when it is small, low, freestanding, and not at an exit door — all four at once. Miss one and you need a permit.
  • A pool over 24 inches deep needs a permit in the model rules, and every in-ground pool needs one. The safety barrier is part of the job.
  • A plain backyard fence under 6 feet needs no building permit in many places, but height, front-yard, floodplain, and pool rules change that fast.
  • Fees are small next to the build: Angi’s 2026 averages are about $100 for a deck, $150 for a fence, and $250 to $1,250 for a pool.
  • No permit does not mean no rules. Setbacks, height limits, and association rules still apply, and unpermitted work can draw a stop-work order and a sale problem later.

Do I need a permit for a deck?

Start with the four tests. Most cities base deck rules on the International Residential Code (IRC). Its exemption in Section R105.2 is narrow.

Permit-free only if 200 square feet or smaller, no more than 30 inches above grade, freestanding, and not serving a required exit door — all four at once

When a deck can skip the permit — United States, model code adopted with local changes · Rule checked October 28, 2026

Source: LegalClarity deck guide, summarizing IRC Section R105.2 · Date pulled: October 28, 2026

Miss one test and you need a permit. A 150-square-foot deck bolted to the house needs one. A low platform outside a bedroom’s only exit door needs one. A deck with a roof, wiring, or a gas line needs one, and that work may need its own trade permit.

Your city can be stricter. Some cities lower the height limit to 18 or 24 inches or drop the exemption altogether. Treat the four tests as a starting point, then check your building department before you buy lumber.

Zoning is a separate check: setbacks from property lines and lot coverage. Building review checks safety — footing depth, beam and joist sizes, stairs, and guards. Some cities bundle both reviews; others want zoning first. Expect inspections at footings, sometimes framing, and a final. A deck over 30 inches up needs guards at least 36 inches high, with openings too small for a 4-inch sphere to pass, per the same guide. Call 811 before you dig footings.

How much does a deck permit cost?

The fee is usually the smallest line in the project.

Angi lists an average deck permit at about $100 in its 2026 permit-cost table, and $230 to $500 in most areas in its deck-building guide; LegalClarity puts a typical deck permit at $50 to $600, figured flat, per square foot, or at 0.5% to 2% of build cost

Deck permit fees — United States · 2026 data, checked October 28, 2026

Source: Angi — How Much Does a Building Permit Cost? [2026 Data]; Angi — How Much Does It Cost to Build a Deck? [2026 Data]; LegalClarity deck guide above · Date pulled: October 28, 2026

Why the wide range? A flat-fee city charges one number. A valuation-based city charges more as the build grows. Plans and a site plan cost money too, outside the fee. Simple reviews can finish in one to three weeks, and some cities approve simple decks at the counter. An incomplete first filing is the most common delay.

Do I need a permit for a pool?

Yes, in almost every case. The model definition turns on 24 inches of water.

A structure that can hold over 24 inches of water counts as a swimming pool, including in-ground, above-ground, hot tubs, and spas; a kiddie pool up to 24 inches needs no permit in Naperville’s table, an above-ground pool 24 to 48 inches needs a permit and a barrier, and all in-ground pools require a permit

When a pool needs a permit — Naperville, Illinois, applying the model-code definition · City page checked October 28, 2026

Source: City of Naperville — Swimming Pool Permit · Date pulled: October 28, 2026

Depth the pool can hold is what counts, not how full you keep it. Electrical work for a pump, lighting, or a heater can need a separate trade permit too.

The barrier is the part owners most often miss. The fence around the water has to pass too, and in many cities it must be in place before the pool is filled.

Barrier at least 48 inches high, no more than 2 inches of clearance at the bottom, no opening that lets a 4-inch sphere pass; gates open away from the pool, close and latch on their own, latch release at least 54 inches up or protected on the pool side

Pool barrier rules — United States model rules, shown in Florida and municipal guides · Rules checked October 28, 2026

Source: Florida Stat. 515 summary; City of Easley pool rules (IPMC 303.2) · Date pulled: October 28, 2026

An above-ground pool wall 48 inches or higher can serve as part of the barrier in some cities, if the ladder can be removed or locked. A house wall can serve as one side in some places, but doors to the pool then usually need alarms. Get your city’s call in writing before you rely on either.

How much does a pool permit cost?

Pool fees run higher, because review covers structure, electrical, and the barrier.

Angi’s 2026 table lists average fees of about $250 for an above-ground pool and about $1,250 for an in-ground pool; LegalClarity puts a typical pool fence permit at $50 to $500

Pool permit fees — United States · 2026 data, checked October 28, 2026

Source: Angi — Building permit cost [2026]; LegalClarity — Pool fence guide · Date pulled: October 28, 2026

Those are permit fees only — not the pool, equipment, or the barrier fence itself. Get the barrier priced with the pool quote, not after. A pool built but not fillable because its barrier failed is the most expensive order to do these jobs in.

Do I need a permit for a fence?

Often, no — until height or place triggers one.

In many places a fence under 6 feet needs no building permit, a taller one does, and the permit typically costs $20 to $200; front-yard and corner-lot fences face lower limits, and a pool fence almost always needs a permit

When a fence needs a permit — United States · Guide checked October 28, 2026

Source: LegalClarity — Fence structure guide · Date pulled: October 28, 2026

Austin shows how local the answer gets. The city requires a permit if a fence sits in a floodplain, is more than 7 feet high at any point, or is more than 6 feet high along a public right-of-way that is not an alley — that last case also needs a city letter of authorization. A fence stepping up a slope can cross the line at one end, so measure the tallest point.

Even with no permit, height limits, setbacks, corner sight lines, and easement rules still bind you. So do homeowners association rules, which are private and can limit height, material, and color. Check both before you order panels, and call 811 before you dig post holes.

How much does a fence permit cost?

When required, a fence permit is usually the cheapest of the three.

Angi’s 2026 table lists an average fence permit at about $150, and LegalClarity puts the usual range at $20 to $200. Masonry, retaining, and over-height fences cost more to review than a standard wood run. If a quote lumps “permit” into one line, ask for the city fee and the handling charge as two lines.

One place that lists all three: Cook County

Unincorporated Cook County, Illinois lists Deck, Fence, and Pool among the permits you can apply for online, alongside driveway, flatwork, re-roof, and shed permits.

Online e-permits for Deck, Fence, and Pool work; minimum filing items are the application, a plat of survey, the scope of work, contractor letters of intent, a $100 deposit, and the estimated cost of work

Cook County e-permits for deck, fence, and pool — Unincorporated Cook County, Illinois (not Chicago or incorporated suburbs) · County page checked October 28, 2026

Source: Cook County Building and Zoning — E-Permits · Date pulled: October 28, 2026

That list is a good model for most cities: a form, a lot drawing, a plain statement of the work, who is doing it, and what it costs.

How do I apply? The steps in order

1. Name the job in your city’s words. Deck, pool, and fence are separate permit types in most systems. A pool with a deck and a barrier fence may be one filing or three. Ask first.

2. Check zoning before you draw. Confirm setbacks, height limits, lot coverage, floodplain, and easements. This is the cheapest moment to move a line.

3. Check your association, if you have one. Get its approval in writing before you file, so you do not pay to permit a design it will reject.

4. Draw a simple site plan. Show the house, property lines, the new work with sizes, and distances to each line. For a pool, show the barrier and gates. For a deck, add framing and footing details.

5. File a complete set and pay the fee. Incomplete sets are the top delay. Have a second person check the set against the city’s checklist.

6. Build only after the permit is issued, and call for each inspection. Plan the pool barrier inspection before filling, and do not pour deck footings before that inspection passes.

7. Close the permit. The job is not legally done until the final inspection passes. Keep the closed record for a sale, refinance, or insurance claim.

What goes wrong most often?

First, reading the deck exemption as “small decks need no permit” — attachment alone defeats it. Second, treating the pool barrier as landscaping; it is a code item that can hold up the whole pool. Third, forgetting the slope on a fence — one high end can trigger the permit. Fourth, skipping the permit because a neighbor did. Enforcement often starts with a complaint, and retroactive fees are typically higher, with removal possible for work that cannot meet code, as our guides on building without a permit and retroactive permits set out.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a permit to build a deck under 200 square feet?

Size alone does not exempt it. It must also be no more than 30 inches up, not attached to the house, and not serving a required exit door — and your city may be stricter.

Do I need a permit for an above-ground pool?

In most places, yes, once it can hold more than 24 inches of water. Naperville requires a permit and a barrier at 24 to 48 inches deep, and a permit for deeper pools too. A kiddie pool up to 24 inches is the usual exception.

How high can I build a fence without a permit?

In many places, 6 feet in a side or back yard is the line, with lower limits in front yards and on corner lots. Austin requires a permit over 7 feet at any point, over 6 feet along a public right-of-way, or in a floodplain.

How much does a permit cost for a deck, pool, or fence?

Angi’s 2026 averages are about $100 for a deck, $150 for a fence, $250 for an above-ground pool, and $1,250 for an in-ground pool. Wider guides put a deck permit at $50 to $600 and a fence permit at $20 to $200.

Can my yard fence count as my pool barrier?

It can, if it meets the barrier rules: at least 48 inches high, small openings, low ground clearance, and gates that close and latch on their own and open away from the pool. Many yard fences fail on gate hardware or gaps.

What happens if I build without a required permit?

You risk a stop-work order, a retroactive permit at a higher fee, daily fines in some cities, and an order to fix or remove work that cannot meet code. Unpermitted work must usually be disclosed when you sell.

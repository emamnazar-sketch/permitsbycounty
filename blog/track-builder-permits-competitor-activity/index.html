How can I track a competing builder’s permits to see where they are building next?

By PermitsByCounty Research Team · Published November 23, 2026 · Guides & Answers

QUICK ANSWER

You track a competing builder’s permits by pulling that builder’s name in the permit records for your county, week after week, and writing down each new job — the address, the type of work, the value, and the date. That running list is how you track builder permits competitor activity in plain form: it shows where the builder is working now, which neighborhoods they keep coming back to, and where their next starts are likely to land, weeks before the signs and listings show up.

TL;DR — the whole post in 20 seconds

  • Permits are public records. Anyone can look up a builder by name, and the record shows the job before the marketing does.
  • Track weekly, not once. One permit is a job; a string of permits in one area is a pattern you can plan around.
  • Write down the same five fields every time: builder name as spelled, address, work type, value, and issue date.
  • Watch the edges of their territory. A first permit in a new subdivision or ZIP is often the start of a run, not a one-off.
  • Paid services already sell this. PermittedJobs lists builder activity — who is pulling permit after permit — as a named feature.

Why a rival’s permits tell you more than their ads

A builder’s ads tell you what they want you to know. Their permits tell you what they are actually doing. A permit is filed before the work starts, it names a real address, and it carries a date and a declared value. Put a few months of those filings in a row and you can see the shape of a competitor’s business without guessing.

You can see how busy they really are. A builder who talks big but files two permits a quarter is in a different spot than a quiet builder filing ten a month. You can see what kind of work they chase — new homes, additions, remodels, commercial build-outs — because the permit type says so. And you can see where they work, street by street, which is the part most contractors never bother to check until a rival’s signs are already up in a neighborhood they thought was theirs.

This is different from checking one builder before you bid their job. That is a one-time look at a builder you might work for. Tracking is ongoing. You pick the two or three builders you compete with most, and you keep a running file on them the way a coach keeps notes on the next opponent.

What one permit record gives you

Start with what a single record holds, because the tracking system is just a stack of these read the same way every time.

The address tells you the ground. Not the city in general — the actual street, subdivision, and ZIP. Builders are creatures of habit. Most of them work a territory they can drive in a day, and their permits draw that territory on a map for you.

The work type tells you the product. New construction, an addition, a kitchen remodel, and a commercial tenant improvement are different businesses wearing the same company name. If a rival who used to do remodels starts filing new-home permits, their business just changed, and yours might need to as well.

The declared value tells you the size of the job. Treat it as a size signal, not a price quote — owners and builders declare values for fee purposes, and the number can run low. But a builder whose average permit value doubles over a year is moving upmarket, and that leaves the smaller work behind for somebody.

The dates tell you the pace. Issue dates show whether a builder files in a steady stream or in bursts — a burst often means a new subdivision phase or a spec-home run starting. And the contractor name, spelled exactly as the office recorded it, is the thread that ties the whole file together.

How to track builder permits competitor activity each week

The system is simple on purpose. If it takes more than about twenty minutes a week, you will stop doing it, and a system you stop doing tells you nothing.

Pick your names first. Two or three rival builders is plenty — the ones whose signs you see on your own streets and whose bids you keep losing to. Write each name down exactly as it appears on a permit, and note the spelling variants too. Permit offices record names the way they were typed on the application, so one builder can show up under two or three spellings. Count the variants as the same builder in your own notes, but never assume two similar names are the same company until an address or phone number matches.

Then set one weekly slot. Same morning, same coffee, same list. Pull each builder’s new permits for your county, and add one line per job to a running sheet: date, address, ZIP, work type, value. That is the whole entry. Five fields, one line, no commentary yet.

Once a month, read the sheet instead of adding to it. That is where the patterns live. Count each builder’s permits for the month. Mark the ZIP codes that repeat. Circle the first permit in any area you have not seen them in before. Note any change in work type or average value. Ten quiet minutes with the month’s lines tells you more than a year of jobsite gossip.

That lead time is why the habit pays. A permit you read this week names a job that has not broken ground yet. By the time the framing is up and the neighborhood notices, you have known for weeks.

Reading the map they are drawing

The single most useful thing tracking gives you is the map. Plot each rival’s permits, even roughly — a list grouped by ZIP works if you never open a map app — and three things stand out fast.

First, the home turf. Every active builder has streets where they file again and again. That is where their crews, their subs, and their supplier runs already are. Competing there means fighting a builder on ground where their costs are lowest. It can still be worth it, but go in knowing it.

Second, the direction of travel. Builders expand the way water flows — one street past the last job, one subdivision over. When a rival’s permits start landing on the edge of their usual ground, the next jobs usually keep going that way. If that edge points at your territory, you have early warning, measured in weeks, not a surprise measured in lost bids.

Third, the new beachhead. A single permit far from a builder’s usual ground is worth a note all by itself. Sometimes it is a one-off — a past customer’s second home, a favor job. But when a second and third permit land near it over the next two months, that was the first shovel of a real move into new ground. Your sheet catches the second permit. Memory alone usually does not.

What to do with what you learn

Tracking is only worth the twenty minutes if it changes a decision. Here is where it usually does.

If you sell to builders — as a sub or a supplier — a rival’s permit run is your call list. A builder starting six homes in a new subdivision needs every trade on those jobs, and the permits tell you the start is coming while the crews are still being lined up. You are not poaching. You are showing up early for work that is publicly posted.

If you compete for the same customers, the map tells you where to spend your own marketing. Do not spread a thin budget across the whole county. Put it on the streets where your rival is gaining ground and on the edge they are moving toward. Homeowners there are about to see building activity, and activity makes neighbors think about their own projects.

If a rival is slowing down, that is information too. A builder whose monthly count falls for three straight months may be finishing a phase — or losing steam. Either way, the homeowners and subs in their territory are about to look around. Being visible in that moment costs little and can pay for the whole year’s tracking habit.

And if a rival suddenly changes work type — remodels to new homes, homes to commercial — do not copy them blindly. Ask why first. They may have landed one big contract, or they may have seen demand move before you did. The permit values and the pace over the next month or two will tell you which one it is.

The mistakes that make tracking useless

The first mistake is the one-time binge. A contractor gets curious, pulls a rival’s whole history in one sitting, feels informed, and never looks again. Permit data goes stale fast. A picture from six months ago shows where a builder was, not where they are building next. The weekly habit is the product. The binge is just trivia.

The second mistake is merging names too fast. Two builders with similar names are not the same builder, and folding their permits together invents a busier competitor than the real one. Keep spellings separate until an address, a phone number, or a license record ties them together.

The third mistake is reading one permit as a trend. One big job can be a favor, a family project, or a one-time contract. Trends in this game start at three permits pointing the same way — same area, same work type, close in time. Hold your judgment until the third line lands on the sheet.

The fourth mistake is treating the declared value as the contract price. It is a fee number. Use it to compare a builder’s jobs against their own other jobs — bigger, smaller, steady — and not as proof of what they charge.

Frequently asked questions

Is it legal to look up a competitor’s building permits?

Yes. Building permits are public records. Anyone can search them by address, and in most offices by contractor name as well. You are reading the same records a homeowner, a buyer, or a reporter can read. What you do with the business judgment you build from them is ordinary competition.

How often should I check a rival builder’s permits?

Once a week is enough for most contractors. Permits are filed 2 to 6 weeks before ground breaks, so a weekly check still puts you ahead of the work. Check monthly at the very least — any slower and the early warning is gone.

What if a builder shows up under two different name spellings?

Keep both spellings on your sheet and count them together only in your own notes once you are sure they are the same company — a matching phone number, address, or license record is the proof. Permit offices record names as typed, so variants are common, but two similar names can also be two different builders.

Can I tell where a builder will build next, or only where they already are?

Both, within limits. The permits themselves show current and recent ground. The prediction comes from the pattern: builders usually keep moving in the direction their last jobs point, and a first permit in a new area is often followed by more nearby. Treat it as an early signal to watch, not a promise.

What is the difference between tracking a competitor and researching a builder before I bid?

Researching a builder is a one-time check on a builder you might work for — their pace, their record, whether their jobs finish. Tracking a competitor is ongoing: the same names, checked weekly, so you see territory and pace change over time. The records are the same. The habit and the purpose are different.

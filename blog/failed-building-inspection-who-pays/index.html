What happens if my project fails a building inspection — and who pays to fix it?

By PermitsByCounty Research Team · Published November 1, 2026 · Guides & Answers

QUICK ANSWER

A failed building inspection what happens next is simple at first: the inspector leaves a correction notice listing each item that does not meet code, that part of the work cannot move forward or be covered up, and it must be fixed and re-inspected before it passes. Who pays follows the cause. If the contractor’s work caused the failure, the contractor almost always pays for the fix and the re-inspection, per LegalClarity, checked November 1, 2026. If your change, your materials, or a hidden old problem caused it, the cost usually falls on you.

TL;DR — the whole post in 20 seconds

  • A fail is a written list of what to fix, tied to code sections — not the end of the project.
  • Do not cover the work. Work hidden before approval can be ordered opened up.
  • The party whose work or decision caused the failure usually pays for the fix and the re-inspection fee.
  • Fixed-price: contractor-caused rework stays inside the agreed price. Time and materials: more can land on the owner.
  • Hold the next milestone payment, in writing, until the re-inspection passes.

A fail means fix and re-inspect, not start over

Most failed inspections are normal. An inspector finds a few items not ready or not to code, writes them up, and leaves. One stage pauses. The project does not die.

EngineerFix puts it plainly: the inspector issues a formal document, often called a correction notice. It lists the problems, from code violations to unfinished work, names the code sections missed, and says what must be done. It goes to the contractor who holds the permit. The homeowner may also get a copy.

Keep that notice. It is the job list for the next step, and your proof in a payment dispute. Photograph the items before anyone touches them.

Watch the severity. A short list is a pause. A safety problem can bring a stop-work order on top of it — no work at all until it is lifted. Our stop-work guide covers that case.

The rule that controls the next move: do not cover the work

The model code rule is short. Section 109.6 of the 2024 International Existing Building Code says work shall not be done beyond the point of each inspection without approval, and portions that do not comply shall be corrected and not covered until the code official allows it.

Work shall not be done beyond the point of each successive inspection without approval; portions that do not comply shall be corrected and shall not be covered or concealed until authorized

Drywall over a failed rough-in makes the problem bigger. The inspector can require the cover to come off, at the cost of whoever caused the failure.

What to do in the first 48 hours

1. Read the notice line by line. If an item is vague, ask the inspector’s office, in writing, what will pass.

2. Check the permit record. A fail that is not recorded can stall the re-inspection.

3. Agree who owns each item. Mark every line with your contractor: their work, your change, your material, your designer’s plan, or a hidden old condition. That meeting settles most of the money question.

4. Fix only what the notice covers. New scope in the middle of a correction can need a plan change, and that can slow the re-inspection.

5. Photograph the fixed work. Photos of each corrected item are cheap proof, for the inspector and for your file.

6. Pay any fee due, then schedule. Many cities schedule nothing while a fee sits unpaid.

7. Be ready this time. Work finished, site accessible, approved plans on site, and a person there to answer questions. A wasted trip can be charged like a failure in some cities.

New York City shows how a big city sorts this. A certifiable objection, one that needs paperwork and not a visit, can be cleared by sending documents through DOB NOW: Inspections. A non-certifiable objection must be fixed in the field, then re-inspected. Ask your city which of your items are paperwork-only. Those can clear in days.

Certifiable objections are removed with documentation sent through DOB NOW: Inspections; non-certifiable objections must be rectified in the field, then re-inspected

Who pays to fix it: the cause decides

No single rule says the owner pays or the builder pays. LegalClarity and EngineerFix land on the same test: find the cause. The cost follows it. Failures fall into three groups.

Contractor-caused. Poor work, the wrong material, or work that does not match the approved plans. A licensed contractor is expected to know and follow the code. If an electrical rough-in fails because the wrong wire size went in, opening the wall, fixing the wire, and closing it again is the contractor’s cost.

Owner-caused. You supplied the fixture that does not meet code. Your own designer drew a plan with a code error, and the builder built what you handed him. You insisted on a change the contractor warned against in writing. The cause is yours, and the fix and fee usually are too.

Hidden conditions. Old wiring, rot, or a past owner’s bad work inside a wall. The contractor who opened the wall did not cause it. Unless your contract promised to handle finds like this, the correction is normally a project cost, by written change order.

When a contractor’s work fails an inspection, the contractor almost always bears the cost of fixing it and getting it re-inspected; the homeowner’s exposure is limited to cases where their own decisions caused the violation

Your contract type changes the answer

Under a fixed-price contract, the builder agreed to deliver the whole scope, inspections passed, for one price. Contractor-caused rework is absorbed inside that price, not added to your bill.

Under a time and materials contract, you pay for labor and materials as they are used. Correction hours can be billed to you, unless the failure came from clear negligence. Get the cause agreed in writing before the rework hours run.

A change order can move the line. If you signed one covering the item that failed, it may put the cost on you. Read the change orders before you argue from the main contract.

Read three contract parts first: scope of work, any clause promising code-true work, and the payment schedule. Work that breaks them is a breach, and bringing it into line is the contractor’s cost.

Who pulled the permit matters. If your contractor pulled it, the city treats them as the responsible party. If you pulled it for a contractor’s work, the city may look to you first.

The re-inspection fee: small money, real delays

The fix is the big cost. The fee is the small one that still stops the job. Cities charge for the inspector’s return trip.

Three cities show the spread. New York City charges $225 for each re-inspection after a failure to correct, under its fee rule in place since 2021. Chicago’s fee table sets $100 per re-inspection. Littleton, Colorado, charges no fee on a first fail, $80 when the same item fails a second time, and a three-day permit hold after a third failure.

$225 for each re-inspection made necessary by a failure to correct a condition, where the failure results in a violation

Who pays the city’s fee follows the same cause test. Contractor’s error, contractor pays. Your change or a hidden condition, it is a project cost. Either way pay it fast: while a fee is due, many cities schedule no new inspections at all.

The fee is rarely the real damage. The delay is. A failed stage pushes the trade behind yours, the delivery, the move-in date. Put the re-inspection date in writing the day the fix is agreed.

Your payment lever, and its limit

If payments are tied to stages — foundation done, rough-in passed, final passed — a failed inspection means that stage is not reached. You can hold the next payment until the work passes. LegalClarity calls the payment schedule your most practical tool. Money not yet paid is a calm, strong argument.

Use it cleanly. Hold only the failed stage’s payment. Say so in writing, attach the notice, and name what releases the money: a passed re-inspection. Keep paying for passed work on time.

Know the limit. A contractor who disagrees can file a mechanic’s lien, a claim against your home’s title, even over work that failed. Your defense is the paper: the failed report, photos, contract clauses, and your letters. In many states an unlicensed contractor cannot file a valid lien at all — one more reason the license check belongs before the job.

If the contractor will not fix it

Most builders fix their own failures. It is cheaper than a fight, and their license is on the permit. When one will not, move in steps, each one in writing.

First, a direct talk, report in hand, item by item. Many failures end here.

Second, a formal written demand, sent in a way that proves delivery. Name the report, list each item, cite the contract, and set a fair deadline. Thirty days is a common cure period, though your contract may name another.

Third, the outside routes. A complaint to the state licensing board costs nothing; boards can investigate, fine, order money back, or suspend a license. If the contractor carries a surety bond, you can claim against it. Check your contract for a mediation or arbitration step before you sue. For smaller amounts, small claims court is often the practical route.

Fourth, a replacement contractor — only after a final written chance to fix the work by a set date. Courts expect that chance. Then keep every estimate, invoice, and before-and-after photo. That file is your damages proof.

A 30-day cure period is common unless the contract names a different one; surety bond amounts for residential work commonly range from $10,000 to $25,000

Before the next inspection on any project

The cheapest failed inspection is the one that never happens. Tie each payment to a passed inspection, not a finished-looking stage. Put a code promise in the contract, with correction and re-inspection costs on the contractor when their work causes them. Have the contractor pull the permit. Verify license and insurance before signing. Keep one project file from day one: contract, change orders, permit, every report, and photos.

Frequently asked questions

What happens if my project fails a building inspection?

You get a correction notice listing each item that missed code. That stage cannot move forward or be covered until the items are fixed and a re-inspection passes. A re-inspection fee may be due before the next visit.

Who pays to fix a failed building inspection?

The party whose work or decision caused the failure. Contractor errors are the contractor’s cost, fix and fee. Owner changes, owner-supplied materials, faulty owner-hired plans, and hidden old conditions usually fall on the owner.

Can I keep building after a failed inspection?

Not past the failed stage, and do not cover the failed work. Other, unaffected work may be allowed — ask the inspector’s office. A safety failure can bring a stop-work order that pauses everything.

How much is a re-inspection fee?

LegalClarity puts most fees between $50 and $150. New York City charges $225 after a failure to correct, Chicago charges $100, and some cities waive the first fee.

Can I hold payment until the re-inspection passes?

Yes. If payments are tied to stages, a failed stage is not a reached stage. Hold only that payment, in writing, with the report attached.

What if my contractor refuses to fix the failed work?

Give a written demand with a fair deadline — 30 days is common. Then complain to the state licensing board, claim against the surety bond if there is one, follow any mediation or arbitration step in your contract, and, after a final written chance to cure, hire a replacement.

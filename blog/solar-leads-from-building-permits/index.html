How do I find solar leads from building permits?

By PermitsByCounty Research Team · Published November 3, 2026 · Guides & Answers

QUICK ANSWER

Find solar leads from building permits by working the permits around solar, not just solar permits. A solar permit usually means that install is already sold, so use it to map where solar is moving and which installers are busy. Your live leads are new roof permits, new builds, and panel upgrades in the same streets — a homeowner with a new roof has a 30 to 60 day window to decide on solar, per PermitVector’s Texas guide, checked November 3, 2026. In one 30-day count across 47 US cities and counties, 1,439 permits were classed as solar, per a permit-data analysis of permits issued September 20 to October 19, 2026.

The honest catch with solar permits

Read this first.

When a solar permit is issued, an installer has normally already sold the job and filed the paperwork. The contractor of record is the firm doing the work. Calling that homeowner to sell solar is calling someone who just bought it.

That does not make solar permits useless. It changes what you use them for. You can see which streets are saying yes and which installers are winning the work near you, and you can see how big the jobs are. That is a map. Your selling list comes from permits filed just before a solar decision.

The four solar signals, in order

Work these in order. The first signals are open work; the last one is a map.

1. A new roof permit. This is the strongest solar signal there is. A homeowner who just paid for a roof has a sound surface for panels and years before the next tear-off. One permit-lead guide puts the decision window at 30 to 60 days after the roof work. Work roof permits from the last two months first, then keep up weekly.

2. A new build. A new house needs power decisions early, and some owners add solar right after they move in. A new-build permit gives you the address, the builder, and the timing. Look for streets and builders where solar is already going in nearby — the next street over is the easier conversation.

3. An electrical panel upgrade. Solar needs panel capacity. A home that just upgraded its service panel has removed one of the common blocks to an install. A stand-alone upgrade is a stronger signal than one tied to a kitchen remodel, but both beat a cold list with no signal at all.

4. Solar permits themselves, used as a map. Group solar permits by ZIP and by installer. A ZIP with steady permits month after month is a market you can work street by street. An installer name that keeps appearing is your competitor picture: who is busy, and where.

What the solar numbers actually show

Solar is a smaller market than roofing. A small list worked well beats a big list skimmed.

In a 30-day count of 77,044 permits across 47 US cities and counties, 1,439 were classed as solar. New York City led with 357, then Chicago with 237, then San Diego County with 224. Read those as demand by place, not leads sitting open.

The work is concentrated. The most active filer was Sunrun Installation Services Inc, with 150 permits, and a second Sunrun company added 35 more. One brand filing about 185 permits in 30 days tells a local installer where the big firms work hard — and where the gaps they leave are.

Stated values help you sort, with care. The median stated solar value was $39,864 in New York City and $21,827 in Chicago. A stated value can be a whole-system price or a partial figure, so use it for rough job size only. Never quote a price off a permit value.

Your weekly routine, step by step

Do this once a week, on the same day. Solar lists are short, so work every row on yours.

Step 1: Pick your ground. Start with the ZIPs your crews already serve. Roof age, shade, and utility rules change street by street. Add ground only when the first list is worked clean every week.

Step 2: Pull three permit types. From your portal, pull the newest roof permits, new builds, and panel upgrades. Pull solar permits too, but file those under your map, not your call list. Write down the filter words you used, so next week matches this week.

Step 3: Read every description. Cities name this work in different ways: solar, photovoltaic, and PV all appear. The 30-day count above classed a “re-roof and add solar” permit as solar — a fair warning to read the words, not the label alone. Drop duplicates.

Step 4: Sort by date, then by street. Newest roof permits first, inside that 30 to 60 day window. Then group by ZIP and street, so one trip covers a patch where solar is already visible nearby.

Step 5: Mark who is already served. If a solar permit names an installer, that home is done — note the installer and move on. If a roof permit names a roofer, the roof job is taken, but the solar decision after it is still open. Keep the two lists apart.

Step 6: Work the list in one block. Call, visit, or mail in sorted order, following local do-not-call and solicitation rules. Note the date and result for each address. No answer twice moves a home to a follow-up list.

Step 7: Update your map monthly. Count solar permits by ZIP and by installer. Streets that keep adding panels are your best streets next month too.

How to read a record in 60 seconds

Open the permit and answer five questions. Address: is it in your ground? Description: roof, new build, panel upgrade, or solar itself? Date: inside your window? Status: issued or active, or finaled long ago? Contractor: who is named, and does that name close the job or open it?

A solar permit with an installer named goes on the map. A fresh roof with no solar yet goes on the list. Anything outside your ground, or months old, gets dropped. Dropping fast is the skill.

Watch for two traps. One project can appear twice, as a building permit and a trade permit — count it once. And a stated value can be a placeholder, so treat it as a rough size guide only.

The free route first

Start free. Your city or county portal, or its open-data dataset, is the source every paid feed reads from. Chicago, Austin, San Francisco, New York City, Philadelphia, and the other places PermitsByCounty tracks publish permits as public records, and most let you search by address, date, or permit type without an account.

PermitsByCounty’s county reports are the second free stop. Where a feed is live and checked, the report shows issued permits by trade and month, so you can see whether solar and roofing are moving before you dig row by row. If a feed is not live yet, the page says so.

Free costs time, not money. Solar lists are short — in most counties you can pull a week’s worth in well under an hour. If that is true for you, free wins.

When a paid feed is worth it

A paid feed sells you saved time, nothing more. It pulls permits from many portals, sorts each one by trade, and flags the job that tends to come next — a new roof flagged as a solar signal is the clearest example. It does not create demand. It saves hours.

Prices checked in November 2026 show the spread. PermitVector charges $19.99 a month for one county, plus $9.99 for each extra county, with no per-lead fee. A 2026 comparison lists PingPermit at $20 a month, PermitGrab at $149 a month, Shovels at $599 a month for 25,000 credits, and Construction Monitor solar subscriptions from $31 a month to $1,281 a month for San Diego County. The same public records sit under every price, so you are buying sorting and speed, never the records.

Before you pay, trial one county. Does it cover your ground? Does it arrive while the window is open? Does each row match the portal? If not, do not pay.

Also ask: does the feed’s solar count mean direct permits, adjacent signals such as roofs, or both? A big number built from roofs can still be useful, but know what you bought.

What not to do with a solar permit list

Do not pitch solar to a homeowner whose solar permit already names another installer as if the job were open. Use that permit for the map, and for follow-on work only where it honestly fits, such as battery storage later.

Do not buy homeowner phone numbers a seller cannot trace to a public record, and check local do-not-call rules before you call or visit. A permit gives you an address and a project. How you reach the owner is a separate question.

Do not promise savings or pay-back times from a permit record. A permit says nothing about a home’s bills, roof direction, shade, or utility rates. Those come from a real site visit, not from a public filing.

Do not chase old solar permits as fresh leads. A finaled install from last season belongs on your map — streets with panels sell panels — but it is not a prospect.

Frequently asked questions

Are solar permits themselves good leads?

Usually not as direct leads. A solar permit normally means an installer already sold that job, and the record often names that installer. Grouped by ZIP and installer, solar permits still show you where solar is moving and who your competitors are.

What is the best permit signal for solar?

A new roof permit. The homeowner has a fresh roof that can carry panels for years, and one guide puts the solar decision window at 30 to 60 days after the roof work. New builds and panel upgrades come next.

Where do I find solar permit leads for free?

Start with your city or county portal or open-data dataset, and filter for roof permits, new builds, and panel upgrades, newest first. PermitsByCounty’s county report, where live, shows solar and roofing activity by month.

Does a permit give me the homeowner’s phone number?

Usually no. A permit normally carries the address, the work, the dates, and the contractor of record, not a verified homeowner phone number. If a seller promises one on every row, ask which record it came from.

How much does a solar permit feed cost?

Prices checked in November 2026 ran from about $20 a month for a small lead feed to $599 a month for a large credit-based plan, with one solar subscription ranging from $31 to $1,281 a month by county. Trial one county and check rows against the portal before you pay.

Can I tell from permits which solar installers are busiest near me?

Yes, where records name the contractor. In one 30-day count, the top solar filer held 150 permits, and Chicago named a contractor on 100% of its solar records while New York City named one on 51%. Check what your own county publishes before you rely on it.

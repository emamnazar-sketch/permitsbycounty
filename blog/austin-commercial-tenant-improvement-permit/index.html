What permits do I need for a commercial tenant improvement in Austin?

By PermitsByCounty Research Team · Published November 24, 2026 · Guides & Answers

QUICK ANSWER

For a commercial tenant improvement in Austin you need a commercial building permit from the City of Austin Development Services Department, filed through the Austin Build + Connect (AB+C) portal, plus separate trade permits for the electrical, plumbing, and mechanical work inside the job. PermitPlace puts a typical Austin commercial tenant improvement permit at $2,000 to $8,000 in city fees. Small interior finish-outs of 5,000 square feet or less can qualify for the city’s Quick Turn-Around review, which can finish plan review in one business day.

TL;DR — the whole post in 20 seconds

  • One job, several permits: a commercial building permit covers the build-out, and licensed trades pull their own electrical, plumbing, and mechanical permits under it.
  • City fees for a typical tenant improvement run $2,000 to $8,000, per PermitPlace’s Austin guide. Your design and contractor costs sit on top of that.
  • File through AB+C. The Commercial Plan Review Division reviews the plans against the 2024 International Building Code and Austin’s local amendments.
  • Standard commercial review takes 15 to 25 business days for the first round, and most jobs go through correction rounds before the permit issues.
  • A small, simple finish-out can move faster: Quick Turn-Around review covers tenant finish-outs of 5,000 square feet or less with no added square footage.

What a tenant improvement is, in the city’s eyes

A tenant improvement — most people shorten it to TI, and Austin also calls it a tenant finish-out — is the work that turns a leased commercial space into your space. New interior walls, a moved bathroom, added lighting, a kitchen line for a cafe, a new HVAC run. The shell of the building stays. What changes is the inside, and who uses it.

That last part matters more than most first-time tenants expect. If the new use differs from the old use — a retail bay becoming a restaurant, a warehouse bay becoming a gym — the city treats the job as a change of use as well as a remodel. A change of use can pull in parking, occupancy, and life-safety rules the old tenant never had to meet. The Commercial Plan Review Division reviews new construction, remodels, revisions, changes of use, and certificates of occupancy, so all of that sits in the same office and usually in the same review.

Cosmetic work is different. Paint, flooring swapped like for like, and fixtures replaced in the same spot with no wall, plumbing, or electrical changes often need no building permit at all. The line is whether you touch structure, systems, exits, or the use of the space. If you are not sure which side your job falls on, ask before you sign the lease work letter — the city’s intake chat can tell you if a plan review is required for your project.

The permits you need for an Austin commercial tenant improvement permit job

Start with the building permit itself. The commercial building permit is the parent permit for the whole finish-out. Your plans show the layout, the walls, the doors and exits, the occupancy type, and the life-safety details, and the city reviews them against the 2024 International Building Code and the Building Technical Codes with Austin’s local amendments.

Under that parent permit, the trades pull their own permits. Electrical work — new circuits, panels, lighting — gets an electrical permit, pulled by a licensed electrician. Plumbing work — moved drains, new water lines, added fixtures — gets a plumbing permit. Heating, cooling, and duct work gets a mechanical permit. You do not pull these yourself as the tenant in most cases. Your general contractor coordinates them, and each licensed trade signs for its own scope.

Some jobs add more. A restaurant adds health and grease-related review. Work that changes the storefront or the exterior can add site and sign permits. Fire sprinkler and alarm changes go through fire review. None of this is guesswork you have to do alone: the AB+C application asks about your project type and scope, and the submittal requirements follow from your answers.

How to file, step by step

First, get your plans together. For a commercial job you will usually need architectural plans, and where the work touches them, structural and MEP drawings — mechanical, electrical, and plumbing — plus energy code paperwork. A licensed design professional stamps the drawings for most commercial work. Skipping the designer to save money is the most common false economy in TI jobs: an unstamped or thin plan set comes back with corrections, and every correction round costs you weeks of rent on a space you cannot open.

Second, submit through the Commercial Building Permit Web Form and link the project to your AB+C account. AB+C is where you upload files, track the review, answer comments, schedule inspections, and pay fees. The city’s intake staff can help you link the project to your account and clarify the fees for your review — use that chat before you file if anything is unclear.

Third, sit through plan review. Reviewers check building code, structural, fire safety, energy, and accessibility points, often across several disciplines at once. You get comments back as one list. Answer every comment, revise the sheets they name, and resubmit. Most commercial jobs go through two or three rounds before approval.

Fourth, pay the remaining fees and get the permit issued. Only then does construction start. Work that starts before issuance can draw a stop-work order and after-the-fact fees, and it puts your opening date — the date your lease cares about — at risk.

Fifth, build, call for inspections as each stage is ready, and close with a final inspection. A passed final is what leads to your certificate of occupancy or compliance, the paper that says you can legally open the doors.

How long it takes — and the faster lane for small jobs

Plan on the standard clock first. A first review in 15 to 25 business days, then your correction time, then a re-review. A clean, simple job with a good plan set can move through in the low end of that range. A change of use, a restaurant, or a job with structural changes sits at the long end or beyond it.

Now the faster lane. The city’s Quick Turn-Around review can complete plan review within one business day when a project qualifies. The limits are plain: the project must be 5,000 square feet or less, and the allowed work is interior remodels that do not add new square footage and tenant finish-outs inside an existing building. Exterior updates can qualify too if they do not grow the building or raise its height by more than 6 feet. If your finish-out fits that box, ask for the Quick Turn-Around application by name when you file.

There is also a Small Project application for simple jobs that can be reviewed on a short clock. It replaced the old 7 business day review. Between the two, a small office or retail finish-out with no use change has a real shot at a permit in days or a couple of weeks instead of months — but only if the plan set is complete on the first try. Fast lanes do not fix thin plans. They just return them faster.

What it costs, honestly

The city fee is the small part of a TI budget, but it is the part people ask about first, so here it is plainly. PermitPlace puts a typical Austin commercial tenant improvement permit at $2,000 to $8,000 in city fees. Where you land depends on the size of the space, the value of the work, and how many disciplines review it. Trade permits, re-inspection fees, and any expedited review charges sit on top.

The bigger costs are around the permit, not in it. Design fees for the architect and engineers. The build itself. And the cost nobody budgets for: time. Every extra correction round is another stretch of rent, payroll delay, and a pushed opening date. That is why the complete-first-submittal advice in this post is worth more than any fee tip. The Austin Permit Estimator can give you a fee picture for your own project before you file — run your numbers there instead of guessing from a neighbor’s job, because fees follow your scope, not theirs.

One more cost to check before you sign: your lease. Many TI leases split costs between landlord and tenant, and a tenant improvement allowance can cover part of the build. What the allowance covers is a lease question, not a city question — but permits and plan fees are worth naming in that talk, because they are real money and they land early.

The mistakes that slow Austin TI jobs down

The first is signing a lease before anyone checks the use. If your use is not allowed in that space, or triggers upgrades the space cannot take, no permit fixes it. Check the use and the certificate of occupancy history for the space before the lease, not after.

The second is filing thin plans to start the clock. The clock does not reward you. A thin set earns a long comment list, and your correction round takes as long as a full review would have. File complete.

The third is forgetting the trades. Tenants picture one permit. The job actually needs the building permit plus the electrical, plumbing, and mechanical permits, each pulled by the right licensed trade. If your contractor waves that off, get a second opinion before work starts.

The fourth is building before issuance. It happens on TI jobs because the lease clock is loud. It is still the fastest way to a stop-work order, doubled fees, and a worse opening delay than the review would have caused.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a permit for a commercial tenant improvement in Austin?

Yes, in almost every case where the work touches walls, plumbing, electrical, mechanical systems, exits, or the use of the space. A commercial building permit from Austin Development Services covers the finish-out, and licensed trades pull separate electrical, plumbing, and mechanical permits. Paint and like-for-like cosmetic swaps with no system changes are the usual exception.

How much does an Austin commercial tenant improvement permit cost?

PermitPlace puts a typical job at $2,000 to $8,000 in city fees. Your exact fee follows the size and value of your project and the reviews it needs. The Austin Permit Estimator can price your own scope before you file. Design and construction costs are separate.

How long does a tenant improvement permit take in Austin?

Standard commercial plan review runs 15 to 25 business days for the first round, and most jobs take 6 to 12 weeks in total with correction rounds, per PermitPlace. Small finish-outs of 5,000 square feet or less can qualify for Quick Turn-Around review, which can finish plan review in one business day.

What is AB+C and do I have to use it?

AB+C — Austin Build + Connect — is the city’s online portal for permit work. You submit the commercial building application through the city’s web form, link it to your AB+C account, upload plans, track review comments, schedule inspections, and pay fees there. It is the normal route for an Austin commercial tenant improvement permit.

Can I start work while my permit is in review?

No. Construction starts after the permit is issued. Starting early can bring a stop-work order and added fees, and it can delay your certificate of occupancy — the paper you need to open. If your lease start is tight, the fix is a complete first submittal or a fast-lane review, not early work.

What happens at the end of a tenant improvement job?

You call for inspections as the work reaches each stage, pass the final inspection, and the city issues a certificate of occupancy or compliance for the space. That certificate is what lets you legally occupy and open. Keep the permit record and the certificate with your lease papers — a future buyer, landlord, or lender will ask for them.

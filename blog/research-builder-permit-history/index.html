How can I research a builder before I bid their job?

By PermitsByCounty Research Team · Published November 6, 2026 · Guides & Answers

QUICK ANSWER

Research a builder before you bid by reading their permit history first, then checking the paper behind it. Pull every permit under the builder’s name for the last two to three years and look at four things: how much they file, what kind of work it is, where it sits, and whether those permits actually close. Then verify the license in your state’s own lookup, check the safety record in OSHA’s free search, and ask two subs who have worked for them how they pay. A builder who files steady work, closes permits clean, holds an active license, and pays on time is a different bid from one who doesn’t — and most of that record is public before you price the job.

TL;DR — the whole post in 20 seconds

  • Start with the permit record: volume, work type, ZIP codes, and job sizes over two to three years tell you if this builder does your kind of work at your size.
  • Count closings, not just filings. Old open or expired permits are a warning. Permits that reach final inspection and close are the good sign.
  • Verify the license yourself in the state lookup. Don’t trust the number printed on a truck.
  • Check safety free: OSHA’s Establishment Search shows inspections and citations, and an EMR above 1.0 means more injury claims than average.
  • Finish with people: two subs and one supplier who have worked with the builder will tell you how draws and final payment really go.

How to research builder permit history in one afternoon

You don’t need a paid service for this, and you don’t need a week. You need the builder’s exact legal name and about two hours.

First, get the name right. Builders file permits under a legal company name, a DBA, and sometimes a land company for each subdivision. Ask for the exact name that will be on your subcontract, then search permits under that name and any DBA they give you. If the permit name and the contract name do not match, write it down. The company signing your contract may not be the company with the track record you checked.

Second, pull two to three years of permits, not the last 30 days. A short window makes a seasonal builder look dead and a one-project builder look busy. Third, sort what you pulled by year, work type, ZIP code, and stated value where the county records one. Those four patterns are the research. Fourth, run the paper checks: license, safety, insurance, and bonding. Fifth, make three phone calls. Paper tells you what a builder has done. People tell you what it was like to get paid for doing it.

What the permit record shows you first

Volume comes first, and it needs context. A builder who filed 40 permits last year and 38 the year before runs a steady operation. A builder who filed 40 last year and 6 the year before either grew fast, took over another builder’s lots, or had one unusual project. None of those is bad on its own. Each changes what you ask next. Steady volume also hints at payment: builders do not keep filing in the same county for years if every job ends in a fight. It is a first filter, not proof.

Work type comes second. Sort the permits: new homes, remodels, additions, commercial build-outs, repairs. A builder whose record is mostly production homes runs a different site, schedule, and pay cycle from one who does six custom remodels a year. Count only the work that touches your trade. If you roof, count their roofing and new-construction permits, not their total.

Geography comes third. Permits cluster where a builder actually works, often a tighter map than their ads claim. Shovels makes this point in its contractor directory: look up contractors by their service area, not their business address, because permits show where the work really happens. If most permits sit an hour from your shop, your travel and punch-list trips change. Price that in, or pass.

Job size comes fourth, where the county records a value. Compare the builder’s typical stated value with the job you are pricing. A sub who crews for $40,000 remodels can get hurt on a $900,000 custom home even if the builder is honest, because the draws stretch longer. Prequalification guides ask this in reverse, comparing a sub’s average and largest sizes with the job on offer. Do the same check in your direction.

Count closings, not just filings

Anyone can file a permit. Finishing is the test. Take a sample — the 20 most recent permits old enough to be done — and check the status of each. Finaled, closed, or certificate of occupancy issued is what you want. Still open two years after issue, expired with no renewal, or withdrawn is what you ask about.

There are innocent reasons for one open permit. Owners pause jobs. Projects change hands. Some counties update a status slowly after the final passes. A pattern is different: if half your sample never closed, this builder leaves jobs unfinished, paperwork unfinished, or both. Either version lands on you, because your final payment often waits on finals above you.

Inspection history sharpens the point. See whether jobs pass and move on or bounce through repeat corrections. Shovels calculates an inspection pass rate and construction speed for contractor profiles wherever that data is available — a sign buyers are expected to check it. You can do a rough version free: pick three completed jobs and count the inspections it took to reach final in the county portal. Then ask about what you found before you bid. A good builder explains an expired permit in one sentence.

Verify the license yourself

Don’t copy the license number off the truck door and call it checked. Look it up in the state board’s own tool and confirm four lines: the status is active, the classification covers the work, the name on the license matches the name on your contract, and there is no discipline on the record.

California’s Contractors State License Board shows how simple this is. You can check a license status online, or by phone at (800) 321-CSLB (2752), searching by license number, business name, or the contractor’s name. The board also tells people to ask to see the pocket license and a photo ID when a contractor bids, and to verify the business address and phone number. That advice was written for homeowners. It protects a sub the same way.

Your state will differ in detail, but the rule travels: verify at the source that issues the license, on the day you research, and save a dated screenshot. Status changes. Note the bond and workers’ compensation entries if your state shows them — California’s board notes that a contractor with employees must carry workers’ compensation.

Check the safety record before you price

A builder’s safety record becomes your crew’s daily risk the day you sign. Two checks cover most of it.

The first is OSHA’s Establishment Search, a public federal database. Enter the company name and you get past inspections, citations, and penalties, including whether an inspection started from a complaint, a referral, or an accident. Read it with judgment. A single old paperwork citation is not repeat fall-protection citations across three years. Patterns matter. And no inspections at all is not proof of safety.

The second check is the EMR, the experience modification rate. It compares a company’s workers’ compensation injury claims with similar companies in its industry. A rating of 1.0 is average. Above 1.0 means a higher-than-average rate of injury claims, and prequalification guides tell GCs to treat a company above that line with caution. Turn the tool around: ask the builder for their EMR letter, the same document they will ask you for. A builder who cannot produce one is telling you how their office runs.

Check money and capacity the way GCs check you

Every sub knows the prequalification packet from the receiving end: insurance certificates, safety numbers, financial questions, project lists. GCs ask because the answers predict whether a company finishes and pays. Procore’s guide lists what a thorough application collects — company details, insurance and limits, surety and bonding capacity, safety including OSHA incident rates and EMR, financial statements and credit references, project history with average and largest sizes, and any litigation, defaults, terminations, or suspended licenses. Read that list as your checklist for the builder.

Start with capacity. You need three answers: how many jobs are running now, how many start in your window, and who funds the draws on your job. A builder who is vague on all three is a risk no matter what the permit record says.

Then insurance. CrewCost’s guidance says coverage should include, at minimum, general liability, workers’ compensation, commercial auto, and inland marine for off-road equipment, and it tells GCs to track renewal dates rather than file a certificate and forget it. Ask for their certificate the same way they ask for yours.

Then get payment terms in writing before your price is final: draw schedule, retainage, and how change orders get approved. Nationwide’s guidance adds public lawsuits, bankruptcies, and Better Business Bureau standing to the check list.

A high-quality prequalification application covers insurance, surety and bonding capacity, safety (OSHA incident rates, EMR, safety policies), finances (statements, credit reports, bank or bonding references), project history (annual volume, average and largest project sizes), and litigation (disputes, defaults, terminations, suspended licenses)

Finish with three phone calls

Paper first, people last — the calls go better once you know what to ask. Call two subs in trades other than yours who work for this builder, and one supplier who delivers to their jobs. Suppliers know payment speed better than anyone, because they see every builder’s 30, 60, and 90-day column.

Ask short questions and listen for specifics. When did you last work for them? How many days after your draw request did the money land? Are change orders priced and signed before the work, or argued after? Would you send your best crew to them again? A reference who answers with dates and numbers is worth more than five who say “they’re great.”

A good builder who pays at 45 days and says so up front can be priced. The dangerous answer is “it depends,” repeated three ways. Slow pay on a clear schedule is a financing cost. Unpredictable pay is a payroll risk, and payroll is the one bill a small sub cannot push back.

If the builder offered the references, still make one call of your own choosing. A handed-over list is a highlight reel.

Red flags and good signs, side by side

Red flags: permits filed in bursts with long silent gaps. Many sampled permits sitting open or expired long past their work. A license that is expired, suspended, in a different name, or in a class that does not cover the job. Repeat serious OSHA citations in the last three years. No EMR letter. Vague answers about who funds the draws. References who only offer general praise. Terms that push every risk downhill — open-ended retainage, pay-if-paid with no dates, change orders at the builder’s sole say.

Good signs: steady filings year over year in a clear territory. Permits that close, with finals passed and few repeat corrections. An active license in the contract name, produced without hesitation. A current EMR letter at or below 1.0. An insurance certificate handed over as fast as they asked for yours. References who give dates, draw timing, and a superintendent’s name they would work for again.

Frequently asked questions

Where do I start if I only have the builder’s name?

Start with the permit record. Search the builder’s exact company name in your county’s permit report or portal and pull two to three years of permits. That list gives you volume, work type, territory, and a sample whose closing status you can check. Then run the license lookup.

How far back should a builder’s permit history go?

Two to three years. One year can be a fluke — a single big project or a slow season. Three years shows whether filings are steady, growing, or fading, and gives you permits old enough that their final status means something.

What if a builder has very few permits under their name?

Ask why before you assume the worst. Some builders file under a land company, a DBA, or the owner’s name, and some repair work needs no permit. But if a builder claims a long track record and no name you try produces permits, treat the claim as unproven and price that risk, or pass.

Is an open permit always a bad sign?

No. One open permit can be a paused owner, a slow county update, or a job still running. It turns into a bad sign as a pattern: many permits sitting open or expired well past when the work should have finished.

Can I check a builder’s safety record for free?

Yes. OSHA’s Establishment Search is free and public: enter the company name to see inspections, citations, and penalties. For the fuller picture, ask for their EMR letter — above 1.0 means more injury claims than average for their industry.

What should I do if the research turns up mixed results?

Price what you found. A strong permit record with slow-but-steady pay might mean a higher number and a clear draw schedule in writing. A thin record with every flag red means the safest bid is no bid.

What does “finaled,” “issued” and “expired” mean on a permit record?

By PermitsByCounty Research Team · Published November 19, 2026 · Guides & Answers

QUICK ANSWER

If you are asking what does finaled mean building permit records are telling you the job is done and signed off. Finaled means the work passed every required inspection and the building department closed the permit. Issued means the permit was approved and paid for, so work may start, but no inspector has signed off the finished work yet. Expired means the time limit ran out before the permit was finaled, so the record stays open in effect and usually needs to be renewed, reissued, or closed before a sale closes cleanly.

TL;DR — the whole post in 20 seconds

  • Finaled is the clean end: all required inspections passed and the permit is closed.
  • Issued is the start, not the finish: work is allowed, but it has not been proved by a final inspection.
  • Expired means time ran out before final: the work may be done, part done, or never started, and the record alone does not tell you which.
  • Most permits go stale if work does not start within 180 days, or if work stops for 180 days, under the model building code many cities adopt.
  • Read the status with the inspection log, the dates, and the scope. One word on its own can mislead you.

The three words, in plain order

A permit record follows a job through its life. First a person applies. The city reviews the plans. If the plans pass and the fees are paid, the city issues the permit. Work starts. Inspectors visit at set stages. If the last inspection passes, the city finals the permit.

The City of Sacramento publishes a plain guide to its own statuses, and it lines up with that order. Sacramento says issued is assigned to records that were approved, all fees were paid, and the project is now under construction. Finaled, in Sacramento, means the project received approval on all required inspections. Expired, in Sacramento, means a building permit or application expired due to inactivity. Three short lines, and they cover most records you will meet in other cities too.

What does finaled mean building permit readers should check first

Finaled means the department closed the loop. The work named on the permit was built, inspectors checked it at the stages the code requires, the final inspection passed, and the city marked the record complete.

That is a strong signal, but it has limits. Finaled covers the scope written on that one permit, for the code in force at that time. It does not bless other work at the house that was done under a different permit, or with no permit at all. A finaled roof permit says the roof job passed. It says nothing about a bathroom added in the same year with no permit on file.

LegalClarity, in its guide to finding building permits for a property, puts the point this way in its own reading of a record: a permit marked closed or finaled means all required inspections passed and the work met code, while an open or expired status means the project was never formally signed off. That split — signed off, or not signed off — is the first question to ask of any record.

There is one close cousin to know. A certificate of occupancy goes further than a finaled permit. A finaled permit closes one project. A certificate of occupancy says a new building, or a space with a new use, may be occupied. Sacramento lists it as its own status: a new commercial building or tenant space is approved for occupancy. Not every job needs one. A re-roof or a water heater swap finals without any occupancy certificate, because nobody’s use of the building changed.

Issued means work may start

Issued means the city said yes to the plans and took the money. The permit is real, the work named on it may legally start, and inspections can be scheduled as the work goes along.

That is all it means. Crest Real Estate, which writes guides on Los Angeles permit searches, warns readers about this exact mix-up. In its words, an issued status merely means the owner paid the fees to start construction; it does not prove that an inspector ever returned to verify the job was done to code. Crest tells readers to picture the status column as a traffic light: finaled is green, issued is yellow, and expired is red.

An issued permit can also be a stale sign. If the issue date is years old and no inspection activity shows after it, the job may have stalled, or the work may have been finished and nobody called for the final inspection. That second case is common. The last step is easy to skip when the room already works and the contractor has moved on.

So do not judge issued by the word alone. Judge it by age and by the inspection log. A permit issued three weeks ago with a framing inspection passed last week is a live job. A permit issued six years ago with no inspections at all is an open question, and you should ask the building department what it would take to close it.

Expired means time ran out

Expired means the permit hit a time limit before it was finaled. Sacramento’s short version is that a permit or application expired due to inactivity. Behind that one line sit two clocks that show up in most cities.

The model building code rule, in section 105.5 of the International Building Code and the matching residential rule, says a permit becomes invalid if work does not start within 180 days after it is issued, or if work starts and then stops or is abandoned for 180 days. The building official may grant written extensions, for periods of no more than 180 days each, if the request is in writing and shows a good reason. Many cities adopt that rule as written. Others set their own dates, so read your city’s rule before you count days.

Los Angeles shows how a local rule can differ. The Los Angeles Department of Building and Safety says an express permit expires in two years from the date it was issued, if no work has started within six months from issuance, or if work started but stopped for over six months. Within 30 days of expiration, the owner may file a request to extend the permit. Same shape, different numbers: a start clock, a stall clock, and a short window to ask for more time.

An expired permit does not tell you what happened on the ground. There are three common stories. In the first, no work ever started: the owner changed plans, and the permit died on its own. In the second, work started and stopped partway, and the job sat. In the third, work was finished in full, but the final inspection was never called, so the permit expired with a completed room behind it. The record alone cannot split those three. The inspection log helps: no inspections points to story one, a passed framing inspection and nothing after points to story two, and a string of passed inspections with only the final missing points to story three.

What expired does mean, in every story, is that the city never signed off the finished work. If you own the house, that usually means you need a renewed, reactivated, or new permit, plus the inspections the department asks for, before the record can be finaled. If you are buying the house, an expired permit is a cost and a delay to price in before you close, not after. LegalClarity’s open-permit guide notes that an expired permit usually has to be renewed or closed before the city will issue new permits, or before a sale can close cleanly, and that in many places an expired permit cannot just be reopened — a new application for the same scope is the route.

Other status words you will see

Applied or submitted means the application is in and the job has not been approved yet. No work may start on an application alone.

In review or plan check means staff are reading the plans. Corrections required means the reviewer found problems and sent the plans back for fixes. That is a normal round in many jobs, not a denial.

Active or open usually means the same stage as issued: the permit is live and the job is not finaled yet. Some cities use open for the whole life of the permit until it closes.

Completed or closed usually means the same end as finaled. Read the inspection log to be sure, because a few offices close old records for housekeeping with no work done. If the log shows a passed final inspection, you have your answer.

Cancelled, void, or withdrawn means the permit was ended on purpose — by the owner, the contractor, or the city — and will not proceed as it stands. It is not a sign-off.

Certificate of occupancy issued means the building or space was approved to occupy, as noted above. A temporary version lets people move in while small items remain, for a set time.

How to read one record, step by step

Start with the scope. Read what work the permit names, in plain words: a 200 amp panel, a kitchen remodel, a new roof. Every other field is about that scope and no other work.

Next, read the status with the dates beside it. Note the date applied, the date issued, and any expiration date. A finaled status with a final inspection date is a closed story. An issued status from last month is a live job. An issued or expired status from years ago is a question to raise.

Then read the inspection log. Look for the stages in order and for the word passed or approved next to the final inspection. A log full of passed stages and no final is a job that stopped one step short. A log with a failed inspection and no re-inspection after it is a job that stopped at a problem.

Last, count what is missing. If the house has a finished room, a deck, or a converted garage and no permit of any status covers it, that gap is its own finding. A clean set of finaled permits for other work does not cover work that was never filed.

What to do with each status

If a record is finaled, keep the record. Save the permit number, the final date, and the scope. You will want that page when you sell, refinance, or file an insurance claim.

If a record is issued and the job is yours and still going, keep it moving. Schedule each inspection as its stage finishes, and do not let 180 days pass with no progress and no inspection. If the job will stall, ask the department in writing for an extension before the clock runs out, not after.

If a record is issued and old, or expired, call the building department and ask three things: what status do you show for this permit today, what steps would final the work, and what will those steps cost in fees? Get the answer in writing if you can. In many places the route is a renewal or a new permit, an inspection of the work as it stands, and fixes for anything that does not meet the current code. In some places the inspector may need finished work opened up to see what is inside a wall. Price that risk before you agree to take it on in a purchase.

If you are selling, close what you can before you list. A finaled record is a selling point you can show. An expired record found by a buyer’s search late in a sale can slow a closing, cut the price, or push the buyer to ask for a holdback until the permit is closed.

Common mistakes

The first mistake is reading issued as finished. It is the most common error on a permit record, and it flips the meaning of the record. Issued starts the clock. Finaled stops it.

The second mistake is reading expired as torn out. Expired says nothing about whether the work exists. It says the paper ran out of time. The work may be sitting finished behind it.

The third mistake is trusting one city’s words in another city. Final in one portal, finaled in a second, and closed in a third can all mean the same signed-off end, while closed in a fourth can mean the file was shut with no work done. Check the inspection log when the word is new to you.

The fourth mistake is stopping at the newest record. A house has a whole history. Read every permit for the address, oldest to newest, and look for the work you can see in the house that no record covers.

Frequently asked questions

What does “finaled” mean on a building permit?

Finaled means the work on that permit passed every required inspection and the building department closed the permit. The City of Sacramento defines it as approval on all required inspections. It covers the scope named on that one permit, under the code in force at the time.

Does “issued” mean a permit was approved and the work is done?

No. Issued means the permit was approved and paid for, so work may start. Sacramento defines issued as approved, fees paid, and the project now under construction. The work is done in the record only when the permit is finaled.

What does “expired” mean on a permit record — was the work torn out?

No. Expired means the time limit ran out before the permit was finaled, often due to inactivity. The work may never have started, may be part done, or may be finished with no final inspection. The record alone does not say which, so check the inspection log and ask the department.

How long does a permit stay issued before it expires?

It depends on the city. Under the model code rule in IBC 105.5, a permit becomes invalid if work does not start within 180 days, or if work stops for 180 days. Los Angeles express permits expire in two years, with a six-month start clock and a six-month stall clock. Read your city’s own rule for your dates.

Can an expired permit be fixed, or do I need a new one?

Often it can be renewed, reactivated, or replaced, but the route is local. Some cities allow an extension if you ask within a short window, such as 30 days of expiration in Los Angeles. Others require a new application for the same scope. Ask the building department what steps would final the work at your address.

Is a certificate of occupancy the same as a finaled permit?

No. A finaled permit closes one project after its inspections pass. A certificate of occupancy approves a new building, or a space with a new use, for people to occupy. Many house jobs, such as a re-roof, final with no occupancy certificate at all.

How much does a building permit cost in Phoenix and Maricopa County?

By PermitsByCounty Research Team · Published November 16, 2026 · Guides & Answers

QUICK ANSWER

It depends on who issues it. Inside the City of Phoenix, the permit fee starts at $195 for work valued up to $1,000 and reaches $303 at a $10,000 valuation. If the job needs required general plan review — and most jobs over $5,000 do — that adds the same amount again, under the city’s fee schedule effective January 20, 2026. Out in unincorporated Maricopa County, that same $10,000 job carries a $181.25 permit fee plus a plan review fee of 65% of that, about $299 in all, under the county’s Table 1-A schedule. A new home in Phoenix also pays development impact fees, from $7,638 in Ahwatukee to $16,824 in Paradise Ridge, and those sit on top of the permit fee.

TL;DR — the whole post in 20 seconds

  • Phoenix and Maricopa County are two different fee schedules. The city issues inside Phoenix city limits. The county issues only in unincorporated areas.
  • City of Phoenix, 2026 schedule: $195 up to $1,000 of value, $303 at $10,000, and $703 at $50,000, before plan review.
  • Required general review in Phoenix adds 100% of the permit fee on jobs up to $50,000 in value, and 80% above that, with a $195 minimum.
  • Maricopa County Table 1-A starts at $23.50 and prices a $10,000 job at $181.25, with plan review at 65% of the permit fee.
  • A new Phoenix home also pays development impact fees by area, from $7,638 to $16,824, due at permit issuance. Budget them as a separate line.
  • Mesa, Chandler, Scottsdale, Tempe, Glendale, and the City of Maricopa each run their own schedule. A Phoenix address on the envelope does not settle which one applies.

Two schedules, one metro

Start with the split, because it decides every number after it.

The City of Phoenix Planning and Development Department — everyone calls it PDD — issues building permits inside the city limits. You’ll file through SHAPE PHX, the city’s portal for residential and commercial work. The department sits at 200 West Washington Street in Phoenix.

Maricopa County Planning and Development only issues building permits in the unincorporated parts of the county — the land that sits outside every city’s limits. You’ll file through the county’s Permit Center, which the county moved to in 2024. If your land is inside Phoenix, Mesa, Chandler, Scottsdale, Tempe, Glendale, Surprise, Peoria, or the City of Maricopa, the county schedule in this post doesn’t apply to you. That city’s schedule does.

It’s easy to mix these up. The mailing address says Phoenix across a wide area, and Maricopa County contains Phoenix, so people assume the city fee applies. It doesn’t work that way. The parcel’s jurisdiction decides it, not the address. Check the parcel first, then price the job. Pricing the wrong schedule is the most common mistake in this metro — and it’s an easy one to make.

One quick honesty note before the tables. We don’t claim any PermitsByCounty Phoenix permit counts in this post. The City of Phoenix permit feed is blocked in our Wave 1 source audit, so there’s no county data brief here. Every figure below comes from a published fee schedule, named and dated in its own block.

What the City of Phoenix charges

Phoenix prices a permit off the value of the work. Valuation means the full value of the job: materials, labor, overhead, and profit. And the city can use a higher assessed valuation than the one you write down, so a low number on the application doesn’t always hold.

Under the schedule that took effect on January 20, 2026, Table A starts at $195 for any valuation up to $1,000. From there it adds $12 for each additional $1,000, or part of $1,000, up to $10,000. So a $3,000 job pays $195 plus two times $12, which is $219. A $5,000 job pays $195 plus four times $12, which is $243. A $10,000 job pays $195 plus nine times $12, which is $303.

Above $10,000, the rate changes. From $10,001 to $50,000, the fee is $303 plus $10 for each additional $1,000 or part of it. A $25,000 job pays $303 plus fifteen times $10, which is $453. A $50,000 job pays $303 plus forty times $10, which is $703.

Then comes the part that catches people off guard. On most jobs over $5,000 in value, Phoenix also charges a required general review fee. That fee is 100% of the permit fee on work up to $50,000 in value, and 80% of the permit fee above that, with a $195 minimum. On a mid-size job, it doubles the permit line.

Here’s the worked example the city guides use. A $10,000 remodel with required general review pays a $303 permit fee and a $303 review fee. That’s $606 before site or plot review, before any separate system charges, before development impact fees, and before any extra inspections. If you budgeted $303 for that permit, you budgeted half of it.

Small jobs have their own lines. A residential water heater valued at $1,000 or less carries a $98 minimum permit fee, and that band allows two inspections, counting reinspections. An extra inspection on that band costs $195 each. A small standalone job valued under $5,000 with a counter review of 15 minutes or less can fall under the no-review exception — that’s why a $3,000 electrical scope is quoted at its $219 permit component with review excluded. Ask which band your job sits in before you assume the review applies.

Fire work is priced by the hour. Fire and life-safety review runs $195 an hour, with a six-hour minimum of $1,170, and the system permit and inspection charges sit on top of that. Commercial and tenant work that touches sprinklers or alarms should carry that line from the start.

The second Phoenix bill: development impact fees

On a new home, the permit fee isn’t the big number. The development impact fee is.

Phoenix charges new development a set of impact fees to pay for the water, sewer, streets, parks, fire, and police capacity the new building needs. The total depends on which impact fee area the land sits in, and the spread across the city is wide. The totals below are for a new single-family home, due at permit issuance, from the city’s published impact fee table as carried in the PermitFlow Phoenix guide.

In Ahwatukee, the net impact fee is $7,538, plus a $100 administrative charge, for $7,638 in all. In Estrella North it is $10,338 in all. In Laveen East it is $10,608. In Deer Valley it is $13,227. In the Northeast area it is $15,092. In the Northwest area it is $15,169. At the top, Paradise Ridge totals $16,824.

That’s a gap of more than $9,000 between two lots in the same city, on the same size house, before the permit fee itself. Two builders quoting the same plan on different sides of Phoenix aren’t looking at the same government bill. If you’re comparing land, compare the impact fee area with the lot price, not after it.

Impact fees only apply to new development. A kitchen remodel or a water heater swap doesn’t pay them. They belong in a new-home or new-subdivision budget, next to water and sewer connection charges, which are also separate from the permit fee.

Pools have a small city line of their own. Phoenix charges a $75 plot plan review fee when you log in a pool or spa permit, separate from the construction permit fee itself, and a pool built close to a property line can trigger added plot review. The permit fee on the pool itself still runs off its valuation under Table A.

What Maricopa County charges in unincorporated areas

The county schedule is older in shape and cheaper in practice. It’s Table 1-A in the county’s Summary of the Fee Schedule, dated June 2024, and it prices the permit off total valuation in bands.

At the bottom, a job valued at $1 to $500 pays $23.50. From $501 to $2,000, the fee is $23.50 plus $3.05 for each $100, or part of $100, over $500. From $2,001 to $25,000, the fee is $69.25 plus $14 for each $1,000, or part of $1,000, over $2,000. From $25,001 to $50,000, it is $391.75 plus $10.10 for each $1,000 over $25,000. From $50,001 to $100,000, it is $643.75 plus $7 for each $1,000 over $50,000. The bands keep stepping down per thousand as the value climbs: $5.60 per $1,000 in the $100,001 to $500,000 band, $4.75 in the $500,001 to $1,000,000 band, and $3.65 per $1,000 above $1,000,000, where the base is $5,608.75.

Run those same jobs through it. A $10,000 job in the unincorporated county pays $69.25 plus eight times $14, which is $181.25. A $50,000 job pays $391.75 plus twenty-five times $10.10, which is $644.25. A $100,000 job pays $643.75 plus fifty times $7, which is $993.75.

County plan review is a flat share, not a second full fee. It’s 65% of the calculated permit fee. So the $10,000 county job pays about $117.81 in plan review on top of its $181.25 permit fee, for about $299 in all. Put that next to the $606 Phoenix subtotal for the same $10,000 of work and you’ll see why the jurisdiction check matters more than any single line in either table.

The county’s small lines are small. A demolition permit is $50. An after-hours inspection is $150 per residential inspection. A county permit is valid for one year from the date it is issued, and it stays active while the project shows continuous progress with inspections. If a permit does expire, ask about renewal before you reapply: a local permit service that works the county daily puts a renewal asked for within 90 days of expiration at 20% of the original permit fee, and a later renewal at the full new application fee. Treat that as a guide to ask from, and get the county’s own number on your permit.

For budgeting, that same county practice gives plain ranges. Simple residential permits, like a water heater, an HVAC swap, or minor electrical work, usually land between $100 and $500. Mid-size residential work, like a room addition or a remodel, usually lands between $500 and $3,000. Commercial tenant improvements usually run $2,000 to $15,000 or more, and new commercial construction starts at $10,000 and goes up. Those are ranges from practice, not schedule lines, but they match what the tables produce.

The other cities are a third answer, and a fourth

Phoenix and the county are only two of the issuers here. Every incorporated city in the metro runs its own building department and its own fee schedule. Mesa has one. Chandler has one. Scottsdale, Tempe, Glendale, Surprise, Peoria, Gilbert, and the City of Maricopa each have one. None of them uses the Phoenix table, and none uses the county table either.

Chandler shows how different the math can look. Its residential remodel fees run at $68 for the first $2,000 plus $12.50 for each additional $1,000, with plan review at 65%. A $10,000 kitchen remodel there works out to about $306 in all — close to the county total, and about half the Phoenix subtotal. Same valley, same size job, three different bills.

So there isn’t one price. There’s a short decision. Name the issuer first, run that issuer’s table on your valuation, add its review fee, and on new construction add impact and connection charges as their own lines. Skip the first step and the rest of the math is wasted.

Common mistakes that raise the bill

The first mistake is pricing the wrong issuer. A job inside Phoenix priced off the county table looks cheap on paper and then doubles at the counter. A county job priced off the Phoenix table looks dear and can scare a customer off a fair quote. Confirm the parcel’s jurisdiction before you quote.

The second mistake is forgetting the review fee. In Phoenix, required general review adds 100% of the permit fee on most mid-size jobs. In the county and in Chandler, review adds 65%. Either way, the permit fee alone isn’t the bill.

The third mistake is leaving impact fees out of a new-home budget in Phoenix. At $7,638 to $16,824 by area, they’re larger than the permit and review fees put together on a typical new home. They’re not hidden. They’re just on a different table, which is why they surprise people.

The fourth mistake is low-balling the valuation. Phoenix can assess a higher valuation than the one declared, and the fee follows the assessed value. Declare the real value of the work, with materials, labor, overhead, and profit in it.

The fifth mistake is letting a county permit sit. A Maricopa County permit is valid for one year and needs continuous progress to stay active. A stalled job should get a renewal question asked early, while the cheaper renewal window is still open.

Frequently asked questions

How much is a building permit for a $10,000 remodel in Phoenix?

Under the city schedule effective January 20, 2026, the permit fee at a $10,000 valuation is $303. If the job needs required general review, the review adds another $303, for a $606 subtotal before site charges, separate system charges, and any extra inspections.

How much is the same $10,000 job in unincorporated Maricopa County?

The county permit fee is $181.25 under Table 1-A. Plan review adds 65% of that, about $117.81, for about $299 in all. That is roughly half the Phoenix subtotal for the same value of work.

Does a Phoenix mailing address mean Phoenix fees?

No. The issuer follows the parcel, not the mailing address. Land inside Phoenix city limits pays the city schedule. Land in unincorporated Maricopa County pays the county schedule. Land inside another city, like Chandler or Mesa, pays that city’s schedule.

How much are Phoenix impact fees on a new home?

By impact fee area, the total due at permit issuance runs from $7,638 in Ahwatukee to $16,824 in Paradise Ridge for a new single-family home. Impact fees are separate from the permit fee and the plan review fee.

How much is a water heater permit in Phoenix?

A residential water heater valued at $1,000 or less carries a $98 minimum permit fee under the 2026 city schedule, and that band includes two inspections. Extra inspections cost $195 each.

How long is a Maricopa County building permit good for?

One year from the date of issuance, per the county guide. It can stay active while the project shows continuous progress with inspections. If it expires, ask the county about renewal before you file a new application.

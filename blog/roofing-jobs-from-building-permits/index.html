How do I find roofing jobs from building permits in my county?

By PermitsByCounty Research Team · Published November 2, 2026 · Guides & Answers

QUICK ANSWER

Start with your county’s own permit portal, filter for roofing work, and work the newest issued permits first. A re-roof permit is the clearest roofing job: the address, the work description, the issue date, and usually the contractor of record are on the public record. New builds and additions with roof work are next, because the roof has not been fitted yet.   • A roofing permit is a job already decided and funded, not a guess about who might need a roof.

What a roofing job looks like in a permit record

A permit record is short. It reads like a work order that has already started, not like a sales lead.

Most records carry the same five things: the property address, the permit type and a short work description such as “reroof,” the date it was filed or issued, the contractor of record where one is published, and sometimes a stated job value. That is enough to sort, to call, and to drive to.

What it does not carry matters too. Outside a few cities, a permit does not attach a verified way to reach the person who awards the work. If a seller says every row comes with a homeowner phone number, ask to see the record it came from. PermitsByCounty publishes business permit facts only, never homeowner names or contact data.

One more warning. Cities do not name roofing work the same way, and some places bury the roof inside a general alteration description. Search for re-roof, reroof, roof replacement, roof repair, and new construction too, then read the description before you add a job to your list.

The four roofing signals, in order

Not every permit that mentions a roof is a job for you. Rank them in this order.

1. A direct re-roof. This is the job itself: the description says the roof is being replaced or repaired. These go first, because the owner has already chosen to spend money on a roof.

2. A new build. A new house or commercial building still needs a roof, and most trade scopes are still open when the building permit lands. A new-build filing is early enough to get in, and late enough that the project will happen. Work it before the framing is done.

3. An addition or remodel with roof work. An addition changes the roof line, and a big remodel often touches the roof, the flashing, and the gutters. Read the description. If the job is inside work only, drop it. If the roof line, deck, or covering changes, keep it.

4. A cluster of re-roofs in one ZIP. One re-roof is one job. Ten in the same ZIP in one month is a patch of work. It may follow a storm, a wave of aging roofs, or a builder working a street. Do not call it storm damage without proof. A cluster still tells you where to put your next week.

A re-roof permit is competitor intelligence too. If another roofer already pulled it, that job is taken. The value is in seeing which firms are busy in your patch, and which streets are moving.

Your weekly routine, step by step

Do this once a week, on the same day, and keep the list short. A list of 200 jobs you never work is worse than 20 you actually call.

Step 1: Pick one county, and one set of ZIPs. Start where your crews already work. Add a second county only when the first list is worked every week.

Step 2: Pull the newest issued permits. Use your city or county open-data portal first. Filter by issue date, newest first, and by permit type or description. Write down the exact filter words you used, so next week’s pull matches this week’s.

Step 3: Filter to roofing, then read the description. Keep direct re-roofs, new builds, and additions with roof scope. Drop interior-only work, finished permits, and duplicates where one project carries two permit rows.

Step 4: Sort by date, then by ZIP. Newest first, because a roof job moves fast. Then group by ZIP, so one drive covers several streets. A roof installs in one to three days, but the full job often takes one to four weeks, so a month-old permit may already be done.

Step 5: Note the contractor of record. If a roofer is already named, that job is taken — mark it as competitor activity, not a prospect. If no roofer is named yet, that is your opening. Do not guess at a name. If the record is blank, write “not named.”

Step 6: Work the list in one block. Call, visit, or mail in the order you sorted it. Note the date you worked each job and what happened. A job with no answer twice is not a fresh job next week — move it to a follow-up list.

Step 7: Check the portal again before you drive. Permits change status. A job active on Monday can be finaled by Friday. A two-minute check saves a wasted drive.

How to read a record in 60 seconds

Open the permit and answer five questions. Address: is it in your patch? Description: does it say roof work, and what kind? Date: was it issued recently? Status: issued or active, or finaled and done? Contractor: is a roofer already named?

If any answer kills the job, drop it. The skill is not collecting permits. It is dropping the wrong ones fast, so the right ones get a call while they are still live.

Watch for two traps. One project can appear more than once, as a building permit and a trade permit, or as a renewal — count it once. And a stated value can be a placeholder: some places publish $1 values, some publish the whole project value on a trade permit. Use a value as a rough size guide only.

Before you pay, trial one county and check three things. Does the feed cover your county at all? Does it arrive while the jobs are still live? Does each row match the portal when you check the permit number? If a row does not match, do not pay. The portal is the source; the feed is only a copy.

A permit is public, so every subscriber sees the same jobs. Speed matters more than size here. The roofer who works the list the day it lands beats the roofer who buys a bigger list and works it on Friday.

What not to do with a permit list

Do not treat a permit as proof of damage, insurance work, or a sale. A re-roof permit proves a roof is being replaced. It does not prove a storm hit that house, or that the owner wants a new quote. Say what the record says, and nothing more.

Do not buy homeowner phone numbers a seller cannot trace to a public record, and check your local do-not-call and solicitation rules before you call or visit. A permit gives you an address and a project. How you reach the owner is a separate question, and the rules change by place.

Do not chase finaled permits as live jobs. A finaled roof is done work. It still shows which firms win work on which streets — but it is not a prospect.

Do not price a job from a permit value. Stated values can be placeholders, estimates, or whole-project figures. Price the roof you walked.

Frequently asked questions

Where do I find roofing permits for my county?

Start with your city or county permit portal, or its open-data dataset, and filter by issue date and roofing descriptions. PermitsByCounty’s county report, where live, shows roofing activity by month, so you can see if the patch is busy before you dig into rows.

What counts as a roofing job in a permit record?

A direct re-roof first, then a new build, then an addition or remodel where the description names roof work. Read the description every time. A general alteration permit with no roof scope is not a roofing job.

How new should a permit be before I work it?

Work the newest issued permits first, and work them weekly. A standard roof installs in one to three days, and the full job often runs one to four weeks, so a month-old permit may already be finished. Check the status before you drive.

Does a permit give me the homeowner’s phone number?

Usually no. A permit normally carries the address, the work, the dates, and the contractor of record, not a verified homeowner phone number. If a seller promises one on every row, ask which record it came from.

Should I pay for a roofing permit feed?

Pay only when it saves you real hours. If you work one county and can pull the list free in under an hour a week, free wins. If you work several counties, or need the list every morning, a feed can be worth it. Trial one county first, and check each row against the portal.

Can I tell from a permit if a roof was storm-damaged?

No. A permit proves roof work was filed, not why. A cluster of re-roofs in one ZIP shows where roof work is moving, but do not call it storm damage, or pitch insurance work, without separate proof.

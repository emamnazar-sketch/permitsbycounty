How do I check if my contractor actually pulled a permit?

By PermitsByCounty Research Team · Published November 17, 2026 · Guides & Answers

QUICK ANSWER

You can check in about five minutes, and it won’t cost you a thing. Go to the permit portal for the city or county that covers your address, search by your street address or parcel number, and open the permit for your job. It should name your contractor as the contractor of record, show a status of issued or active while the work is running, and end as finaled or closed once the last inspection passes. If there’s no record at all — or the permit is sitting in your name when you never agreed to pull it — stop and ask questions before more work gets covered up.

TL;DR — the whole post in 20 seconds

  • Permits are public records. Anyone can look them up by address, and most cities and counties offer a free online search.
  • The record you want names your contractor as the contractor of record. A permit in the homeowner’s name puts the duty to close it on the homeowner.
  • Issued is not finished. The status you want at the end is finaled or closed, which means an inspector signed off.
  • No record can mean three things: no permit was pulled, you searched the wrong city or county system, or the record hasn’t posted yet. Check the other system and call the building department before you accuse anyone.
  • A contractor who asks you to pull the permit in your name so he can start faster is a red flag. A contractor who does clean work pulls his own permits.

Start with the right office

A permit lives with the office that issued it, and that office is local. For one house it might be the city building department. For the next street over, outside the city line, it might be the county. That split is why most failed searches fail. You search the county site, find nothing, and panic — when the permit was sitting in the city system the whole time.

So your first job isn’t the search itself. It’s naming the right office. Search your address plus the words building permits. Open the result from a government site. If you’re still not sure, look your address up on the county assessor or property appraiser site. That page usually names the jurisdiction for the parcel, and it gives you the parcel number too, which is your backup search key.

Write down three things before you open the portal: your full street address, your parcel number if you can find it, and the permit number if your contractor gave you one. A permit number is the fastest key of all. If he gave you one, you’ll know in under a minute whether it’s real.

The five-minute check, step by step

Step one: open the portal and search by address. Most portals label the search permit search, permit lookup, or property information. Type the street number and street name first. If nothing comes back, try it without the suffix — Main instead of Main Street — because some systems store addresses that way. If the address still fails, search by parcel number. The parcel number points at the land itself, so it cuts through unit numbers, spelling changes, and old address formats.

Step two: find your job in the list. A busy house can carry a long list — an old roof, a water heater from years ago, a fence. Match on three things: the date, the work description, and the trade. Your kitchen remodel should read like a kitchen remodel. A record that says electrical only, on a job that also moved plumbing and a wall, is a partial answer, not a full one. Big jobs often carry several permits, one per trade, so look for the set, not a single line.

Step three: open the full record and read the contractor field. That field is called the contractor of record. It should carry your contractor’s business name, and often his license number. That’s the whole point of this check. A permit that exists but names a company you’ve never heard of, or names you, isn’t the same thing as your contractor pulling his permit.

Step four: read the status. While work is running, issued or active is normal. When the job is done, you want finaled or closed. Expired means the clock ran out before a final inspection passed. Open, long after the crew left, means the last step never happened.

Step five: read the inspection history. Most portals show each inspection, its date, and whether it passed. A final inspection marked passed is the strongest line in the whole record. It means the city or county looked at the finished work and signed it.

Step six: save what you found. Note the permit number, the contractor name exactly as spelled, the status, and the date you checked. A screenshot is fine. If you later sell, refinance, or argue about the job, that one page does more work than any text message thread.

What each field on the record is telling you

The permit number is the file’s name. Quote it in every call and email. Staff can pull a file by number in seconds, while an address search can take a conversation.

The description of work is the scope the office approved. Read it word for word. If the crew is building a 300-square-foot addition and the description says interior remodel, no addition, the work in the yard is not covered by that permit. Work beyond the approved description is one of the common ways a permitted job still lands in trouble.

The contractor of record is the party the office holds responsible. Permit guidance for Los Angeles makes the same point in different words: records name a contractor of record, and a pattern tied to that name — expired permits, permits never finaled — is a warning sign worth reading before you hire, not after. The name also lets you run the second check, the license check, below.

The status is the life of the file. Applied means someone filed and nothing has issued yet — no, work shouldn’t have started on an application alone. Issued means the office approved it and work may run, inside its clock. Finaled or closed is the clean end. Expired, void, or withdrawn means there’s no live permission behind the work now, whatever there once was.

The dates matter more than people think. A permit issued after the work started is a late permit. That happens on honest jobs too — a filing delay, a portal lag — but a permit that shows up only after you asked about it tells a story, and you’re allowed to ask why.

The second check: does the license match the permit?

A permit with your contractor’s name on it is a good start. It’s not the whole check. Take the license number from the record and search it on your state licensing board’s site. In California that’s the Contractors State License Board. In Florida it’s the Department of Business and Professional Regulation search at myfloridalicense.com. Most states offer the same free lookup under a plainer name.

You’re matching three things. The name on the license should match the name on the permit. The license should be active, not expired or suspended, on the dates the work ran. And the license class should fit the work — an electrical job under a license that doesn’t cover electrical work is a problem even if the number is real.

Then run the same name search the other way. Search the contractor’s past permits in your city or county, the way you would search your own address, using his name or license number where the portal allows it. You are reading for a pattern, not a single job. A healthy record shows permits across years, in the trades he sells, most of them finaled or closed. A thin record — few permits for a contractor who claims years of big remodels — means his work is running under someone else’s permits, or under none. A record full of expired permits that never finaled means he starts paperwork and does not finish it, and the homeowner is left holding each open file.

Do this before you sign, if you can. It takes about ten minutes and it’s free. Doing it mid-job still helps. It tells you whether to push for inspections now, while walls are open, or later, when opening them again costs real money.

When there is no record at all

No record is a finding, not a verdict. Work through the boring reasons first.

You may have searched the wrong system. City and county records do not cross, and suburbs run their own. If your house sits near a line, search both.

The record may not have posted yet. Portals don’t all update in real time. A permit issued yesterday can take days to show in some systems. If the crew says the permit issued this week, ask for the permit number and call the office with it. Staff can see files the public portal hasn’t posted.

The job may not need a permit. Plenty of honest work needs none — paint, cabinets swapped in place, a like-for-like fixture change in many places. If your job is on that list, no record is the right answer. Our deck, pool, and fence post and our water heater and panel post walk through where those lines usually sit.

Or no permit was pulled. If the office confirms, by phone, that no application and no permit exist for your address and your job, and work that needs a permit is already running, that’s the answer you feared, and the next section is for you.

Red flags that mean stop and ask

The clearest red flag is the permit in your name. If a contractor tells you to pull the permit yourself, as owner-builder, to save money or start faster, understand what changes. The applicant of record is the person the building department deals with until the permit closes. Correction notices come to that person. If the work fails inspection, that person answers for it. Pulling your own permit doesn’t lower the bar — same code, same inspections — it just moves who carries the duty. Angi’s permit guidance puts the hiring point plainly: your contractor should pull the permit in his own name, you pay for it as part of the job, and a contractor who refuses to pull it is a red flag.

The second flag is a permit that does not match the job. Wrong trade, wrong scope, a value far below the real contract price, or a description that leaves out the addition everyone can see from the street.

The third flag is time. Work starts weeks before any permit issues, inspections are always somehow next week, and the final inspection never gets scheduled after the last check clears. The final is the step that protects you. A contractor who avoids it is avoiding the one look at his work he doesn’t control.

The fourth flag is paper that will not sit still. A permit number that turns out to belong to another address. A license number that belongs to a different company. A promise to send the permit copy that never arrives, when the portal would have shown it to you in five minutes for free.

None of these proves bad work. Each one is a reason to pause payment on the next stage until the record matches the story. Put your question in writing, keep it plain — permit number, name on the record, status — and give a date for the answer.

If the permit was never pulled

Stop the work that needs a permit, in writing, and keep the site as it is. Don’t let anyone cover work that an inspector would need to see. Covered work is the expensive kind of unpermitted work, because fixing the paper can mean opening walls.

Call the building department and say exactly what happened: work started at your address, you searched the record, and staff confirmed no permit exists. Ask what the path is in that office. In most places it’s an after-the-fact or retroactive permit — an application now, plans or photos of what was built, inspections of what can still be inspected, and fees that often run higher than the original permit would have. Our retroactive permit post walks through that path step by step.

Talk to your contractor with the record in hand, not with a suspicion. Some honest firms file under a parent company name, or the filing sits in a second portal, and the call clears it in a day. If it doesn’t clear, hold further payment on permitted work until a real permit, in the right name, covers the job. If money is already out and the answers stop coming, that’s the point where a construction attorney or your state licensing board’s complaint process stops being an overreaction.

One more thing, because it surprises people: the risk doesn’t stay with the contractor. Unpermitted work can draw fines, can be denied by a homeowner’s insurer if damage traces back to it, and follows the house into a sale, where the next buyer’s own five-minute check will find exactly what you found — nothing, where a record should be.

Frequently asked questions

How do I check if my contractor actually pulled a permit?

Search your address on the permit portal for the city or county that covers your house, open the permit for your job, and read three fields: the contractor of record should be your contractor, the status should be issued while work runs, and the inspection history should end with a passed final. No record, the wrong name, or your own name on the permit each calls for questions before more work is covered.

Is it free to check if a contractor pulled a permit?

Yes. Permit records are public records, and most building departments offer the search free online. Some older records need a visit, a call, or a written records request, and certified copies can carry a small fee, but the basic check — is there a permit, in whose name, at what status — costs nothing.

What if my contractor pulled the permit in my name?

Then you’re the applicant of record. The department will deal with you on corrections, inspections, and closing the permit, even though a contractor did the work. Ask why it was filed that way, get the answer in writing, and don’t agree to an owner-builder filing on a hired job just to start faster. Same code and same inspections apply either way — only who answers for them changes.

What does it mean if a permit says issued but never finaled?

It means the office approved the work to start, but no inspector ever signed the finished job. The permit stays open, and an open permit can delay a sale or a refinance years later. Call the contractor first and ask him to schedule the final. If he’s gone, call the building department — most offices have a path for the owner to close it with inspections.

Can I check a contractor’s permit history before I hire him?

Yes, and it’s one of the best free checks you can run. Search his name or license number in the portal and on your state licensing board. Look for permits in the trades he sells, across the years he claims, with most of them finaled or closed. Few permits, or many expired ones that never finaled, is a pattern worth a hard question before you sign.

My search shows no permit, but my contractor says he pulled one. Who is right?

Either of you could be, at first. You may have searched the wrong city or county system, or the record may not have posted yet. Ask him for the permit number — a real filing has one — and call the building department with it. Staff can confirm a live file in minutes. If the office says no application and no permit exist for the job, treat that as the answer.

How do I find HVAC jobs from building permits before my competitors do?

By PermitsByCounty Research Team · Published November 18, 2026 · Guides & Answers

QUICK ANSWER

You find HVAC jobs from building permits by watching the permits that come before the HVAC work, not the HVAC permit itself. Check new-home, remodel, and commercial build-out permits in your county each week. Look for a general contractor named with no HVAC company named yet. Rank those builders by how many permits they filed in the last few months, then call the busiest ones with one real job of theirs in mind and ask to get on their bid list for HVAC leads from building permits in your area.

TL;DR — the whole post in 20 seconds

  • Do not wait for a mechanical permit. By then the HVAC company is usually already picked.
  • Watch building permits instead: new homes, additions, remodels, and tenant build-outs that will need heating and cooling.
  • The best sign is a GC named and no HVAC sub named. Paid services sell that exact filter.
  • Rank builders by permits filed in the last 3 months. Small and mid-size builders are the easiest to reach.
  • Call with one of their real jobs in hand, send your license and insurance, and ask for the next bid list.

Why the HVAC permit itself is too late

Most HVAC owners start in the wrong place. They search for mechanical permits, find a furnace or air conditioner permit, and call about it. That job is gone. The company that pulled the mechanical permit is the company doing the work.

The job was open weeks earlier. It was open when the builder filed the building permit for the house, the addition, or the shop fit-out. At that point the plans call for heating and cooling, but no HVAC company has been picked yet. That gap is the whole opportunity.

Think of the order on a normal job. First comes the building permit. Then framing. Then the trades get booked — plumbing, electrical, and HVAC — so rough-in can happen on schedule. If you only show up at the mechanical permit, you missed the booking step. If you show up at the building permit, you are on time.

That is why companies pay for this filter. Helptyfy, a Chicago permit-lead service, sells leads filtered by trade and points to projects with a general contractor named and no HVAC sub yet. HVAC Scout sells commercial HVAC leads sourced from permits and other public records, one company per county. Morning Permits sells a weekday morning digest of new permits by trade, including HVAC, for cities such as Austin, Texas and Seattle, Washington. Three different sellers, same idea: the building permit comes first, and speed matters.

What to look for on the permit

Open a building permit and read five things. Each one takes seconds once you know the order.

First, the type of work. New construction, an addition, a big remodel, and a commercial tenant build-out all need HVAC work. A re-roof or a fence does not. Spend your time on the first group.

Second, the address and the ZIP code. Work you can actually reach beats a long list you cannot. Map the permits for a week and you will see clusters — a new street going up, a shopping strip turning over. Clusters mean one trip can cover several calls.

Third, the declared value. The value on the permit is what the applicant told the city, not a final price. Still, it sorts the list fast. A $400,000 new home and a $15,000 room remodel are different HVAC jobs. Use the value to pick the size of job your crew wants.

Fourth, who pulled the permit. In most offices the record names the builder or general contractor, often with a phone number. That name is your call. TarSignal, which reads North Carolina permit offices, puts it plainly: the permit names the address, the kind of work, the declared cost, and in most offices the contractor and a phone number.

Fifth, and most important, who is not named. If the record names a plumber or an electrician but no HVAC company, the HVAC slot may still be open. If it already names an HVAC company, move on. Do not spend a call on a filled job.

The weekly routine that works for HVAC leads from building permits

You do not need to check permits every hour. You need a steady routine you will keep.

Pick one morning a week. Monday works well because it catches the prior week in one batch. Pull the new building permits for your county or city for the last 7 days. Filter to new homes, additions, remodels, and commercial build-outs. Drop anything that already names an HVAC company.

Now rank what is left by builder. Count how many permits each builder filed in the last 3 months, not just this week. A builder with one permit may be a homeowner or a one-off. A builder with eight permits in a quarter is a repeat buyer of HVAC work. That count is your priority list.

TarSignal gives a real picture of how lopsided that list can be. In Mecklenburg County, North Carolina, in September 2026, 608 new-home permits were filed by 143 different builders. Six builders filed 20 or more homes each, 154 permits between them. The other end of the list matters too: 82 of the 143 builders filed just one home. Big production builders tend to work with trade partners they already have. The small and mid-size builders in the middle are the ones most likely to take your call and try a new HVAC company.

Write down three things for each builder on your short list: the count, one current job address, and the phone number from the permit. That is all you need to make a good call.

How to make the call

Do not open with a pitch about your company. Open with their job.

Say the address. Say the work. For example: you saw their permit for a new home on a named street, you do HVAC rough-in and set work for homes that size, and you want to bid the next one. That one sentence proves you did your homework. A generic call about looking for work does not.

Then ask for the bid list by name. Ask who handles preconstruction or estimating, and ask to be added to their bidders list for your trade and your area. TarSignal describes the same step: ask the estimator or preconstruction manager to add you, and send your license, insurance, and a few similar jobs.

Send that packet the same day. Keep it short: license, insurance certificate, two or three similar jobs with values, and the areas you cover. If a builder asks for safety records or references, send them fast. Speed at this step is part of the test. Builders are watching how you handle paper before they trust you with a schedule.

If the builder has no work open right now, ask when the next job bids. Put that date in your calendar and check that builder’s new permits the week before it. You will know about the job before the call if you keep watching the permits.

Replacement work is a second lane

New construction is not the only HVAC work in the records. Change-out work — a furnace, heat pump, or air conditioner swap — needs a mechanical permit in most places, and those permits tell you two useful things.

First, they show which companies are busy near you. If one shop pulls a lot of change-out permits in your ZIP codes, that is your real competition, named in public. Second, they show the streets and subdivisions where equipment is old enough to fail in batches. Homes built in the same year tend to need new systems in the same few years.

TarSignal counted this in North Carolina: in the 12 months to September 2026, in five offices it reads, 4,335 replacement permits named a heat pump and 3,022 named a gas furnace or gas pack, and 59 percent of the replacement permits named a heat pump. You cannot call a homeowner from a permit record in most places — and you should not expect contact details the record does not carry. Use the pattern instead: pick your service area and your mail and ad timing from where the old equipment actually is.

Common mistakes

The first mistake is waiting for the mechanical permit. Covered above, and it is still the most common one. The permit that names the HVAC company is a record of a decision, not an open job.

The second mistake is calling the biggest builders first and stopping there. The top few builders file a lot of permits, but they also have the longest-standing trade partners. Work the middle of the list: builders filing three to fifteen jobs in a quarter, in your area, at your job size.

The third mistake is a list with no follow-up. One call rarely lands a bid list spot. Check each short-list builder’s new permits each week, and call again when a new job that fits you shows up. The second call is easier because you can name the new job too.

The fourth mistake is trusting a record without a quick check. Permit data can lag, a scope can change, and some offices publish no contractor at all. TarSignal notes that some offices publish no contractor, and a homeowner’s own permit names none. If the record is thin, say so in your notes and move on. Do not fill in gaps you cannot see.

Free way or paid way

You can do all of this free. City and county permit portals are public, and PermitsByCounty county reports are free. The cost is your time: an hour or two a week once you know your portal.

Paid services charge for speed and filtering. Morning Permits charges for a weekday digest by city and trade. HVAC Scout charges a flat monthly subscription and sells each county to one company. Shovels and other data services charge more for bulk permit data you can filter yourself. None of them has secret permits. They sell the sorting. If your hour is worth more than the fee and the filter is the exact one you need — GC named, no HVAC sub yet — paying can make sense. If not, the free routine above gets you the same records.

Frequently asked questions

How do I find HVAC jobs from building permits before my competitors do?

Watch building permits, not mechanical permits. Each week, pull new-home, remodel, addition, and commercial build-out permits in your county. Keep the ones with a general contractor named and no HVAC company named yet. Rank those builders by permits filed in the last 3 months, then call with one real job in hand and ask to get on their bid list.

Where do I look up building permits for HVAC work?

On your city or county permit portal, and in the free PermitsByCounty county report for your county. Search by date and type of work first, not by the word HVAC. The early jobs are filed as building permits. The mechanical permit comes later and usually names the company already picked.

What is the best sign that an HVAC job is still open?

A general contractor named on a new building permit with no HVAC company named yet. Paid lead services sell that exact filter because it marks the gap between a job being filed and the HVAC sub being booked. If an HVAC company is already named, the job is filled.

Should I call big builders or small builders first?

Call the middle first. The biggest builders file the most permits but tend to use trade partners they already have. Builders filing a handful of jobs in a quarter are easier to reach, and the owner or estimator often takes the call. TarSignal found 82 of 143 builders in one county-month filed just one home, so the list is mostly small builders.

Can I get HVAC replacement leads from permits too?

You can learn from them, with a limit. Change-out permits show which companies are busy and which streets have old equipment failing in batches. Most records do not give you a homeowner’s contact details, so use the pattern to pick your service area and timing, not as a call list.

Is it worth paying for a permit lead service?

It can be, if the service filters the way you work and your time costs more than the fee. HVAC Scout sells one county to one company, and Morning Permits sells a weekday digest by trade. Both sell sorting and speed, not secret data. The same permits are public and free if you run the weekly routine yourself.

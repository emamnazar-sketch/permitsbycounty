How long does it take to get a building permit — and how can I speed it up?

By PermitsByCounty Research Team · Published October 27, 2026 · Guides & Answers

QUICK ANSWER

In 2026, a building permit first review takes 22.9 days on average and 14 days at the median, based on published guidelines from 741 U.S. cities in PermitPlace’s 2026 State of Building Permits study, checked October 27, 2026. That is only the first review. PermitPlace says the total time from first filing to permit in hand is typically 2 to 4 times longer for commercial work, once correction rounds are counted. You speed it up mainly before you file: submit a complete set the first time, clear zoning and other pre-approvals first, use any fast review your city sells or offers, and answer corrections in days, not weeks.

TL;DR — the whole post in 20 seconds

  • First review is not the full wait. The 2026 national average is 22.9 days for the first review alone, and the full run is often 2 to 4 times that.
  • City gaps are wide. Denver, Houston and Miami list 2 days for initial review. Chicago lists 92 days and San Francisco lists 60 days, per PermitPlace.
  • The clock does not start until your set is complete. A missing sheet or form can add 1 to 4 weeks before review even begins.
  • Corrections are where most time is lost. Most commercial jobs need 2 to 3 rounds, and each round adds another full review period.
  • Paid speed exists in some cities. Philadelphia charges $2,000 for Accelerated Plan Review and reviews within 5 business days, against a standard 15 to 20 business days.

What does “how long” actually mean?

When a city posts a review time, it usually means one step: the time from a complete filing to the first set of comments or an approval. It does not mean permit in hand. Pre-application meetings, correction rounds and final issuance sit outside that number, per PermitPlace. Ask two questions for your job: how long is the first review in my city, and how many correction rounds should I plan for? The second answer decides your real start date.

How long does it take in major cities?

The spread between cities is wide. Two jobs with the same drawings can start weeks apart for no reason except the city line they sit in.

Denver, Houston and Miami list 2 days for initial review; Austin 15 days, New York City 30 days, San Francisco 60 days, Chicago 92 days, the slowest major metro

Fastest and slowest major metros — United States major metros · 2026 study, checked October 27, 2026

Source: PermitPlace — 2026 State of Building Permits · Date pulled: October 27, 2026

PermitPlace gives reasons for Chicago’s slow place: a multi-department review, high application volume, and aldermanic approval steps. San Francisco is called the most complex process in California in the same table. Texas averages 17.4 days across 81 cities, California 21.1 days, and Florida 21.2 days, per the same study — but inside each state the range runs from 1 to 180 days, so use the city figure for planning.

How long does each kind of job take?

Simple work moves faster than new construction in almost every city. Austin shows the pattern well.

Small residential projects 5 to 10 business days for initial review, residential new construction 10 to 20 business days, commercial 15 to 25 business days, resubmittal about 10 business days; typical commercial total 6 to 12 weeks once corrections are counted

Austin review times by job type — Austin, Texas · Guide last verified February 2026, checked October 27, 2026

Source: PermitPlace — Austin, TX Building Permits · Date pulled: October 27, 2026

The City of Austin’s own residential page lists express work as typically same day, many small items such as fences, interior remodels and pools at 5 business days, and new construction and additions at 10 business days for that review step. Those are targets for that step, not a promise for the whole job.

Seattle shows the other end. Its permit page says staff try to finish an initial review of a simple building, such as a typical new home, in about 2 weeks, and that a large or complex building takes about 8 weeks. In short: a small residential permit can be days to a couple of weeks in a fast city; a new home or commercial build-out is weeks to a few months, and longer when a zoning or other review sits in front.

Why do permits take longer than the posted time?

First, incomplete filings. PermitPlace calls them the number one cause of delay. The clock does not start until the department says the filing is complete. A set rejected at intake for a missing document, a wrong form or a fee error can lose 1 to 4 weeks before any reviewer sees it.

Second, correction rounds. Most commercial jobs get comments and need 2 to 3 resubmittals. Each round adds another full review period. A 15-day first review can become a 60-day total on that math alone.

Third, separate approvals outside plan review. A zoning variance, a conditional use approval, a design board or an environmental review each has its own timeline, and PermitPlace notes those can add months. Philadelphia makes the same point locally: for most work you need a Zoning Permit before you can apply for the Building Permit at all.

Fourth, workload. Posted times are targets, not guarantees. A department that normally turns work in 10 days can stretch to 30 or more in a busy season. You cannot control that part, only avoid adding your own delay on top.

How much does a delay cost?

For a home project, a delay mostly costs time. For a commercial job, it costs money every day the doors stay shut.

Delays cost a commercial project about $500 a day in carrying costs; the gap between a 14-day city and a 92-day city is 78 days, about $39,000 per location, and about $780,000 a year for a retailer opening 20 locations

That $500 figure is a vendor estimate, not a rate for your job. Work your own number — rent or loan cost per day, crew cost if a start slips, and any lost opening date — before you decide what speed is worth paying for.

How can I speed it up? The steps that actually work

No trick moves a correct set ahead of a city’s queue by itself. The gains come from removing the delays you control.

1. Clear what must come first. Check zoning for the lot and the use, and list every pre-approval your city wants first. In Philadelphia that means the Zoning Permit first for most work, plus Historical Commission approval for historic properties before the filing is accepted. Finding a zoning problem after you file is one of the most expensive surprises in the process.

2. Take the pre-application meeting if one is offered. Philadelphia offers a preliminary project meeting for $350 and a preliminary plan review for $442, per the city’s service page. That is where you confirm items that would otherwise come back as first-round comments.

3. File a complete set the first time. Use the city’s own checklist, not memory. A full filing holds application forms, plans for each discipline your job touches, the owner’s authorization, contractor license numbers and proof of insurance, with plan review fees paid at filing, usually 50 to 65 percent of the total fee, per PermitPlace’s guide. Have a second person check the set, sheet by sheet, before it goes in.

4. File so reviews can run together. Some cities review all disciplines at the same time and others one after another, and parallel review is faster, per PermitPlace’s guide. A complete set for every discipline on day one is what lets parallel review work. A missing fire or mechanical set holds the whole job.

5. Use the fast lane your city sells or offers, when your job fits it.

Standard review 15 business days for one-or-two-family work and 20 business days for all other work; Accelerated Plan Review costs $2,000, $350 due at application, reviewed within 5 business days, and is not credited toward the final permit fee

Philadelphia paid fast review — Philadelphia, Pennsylvania · City page checked October 27, 2026

Source: City of Philadelphia — Get a Building Permit · Date pulled: October 27, 2026

Austin has its own versions, from a typically same-day express route to an expedited plan review program for commercial work. Not every job fits. Ask before you file.

6. Answer corrections fast, and answer all of them. Backcheck reviews take about 5 to 15 business days, some jobs go through 2 to 4 correction rounds, and PermitPlace says its clients save time in part by answering in 24 to 48 hours. A partial answer can buy another whole round. Send one clean response that names each comment, says what changed, and gives the sheet where the change sits.

7. Hire an expediter when the math or the city calls for it. PermitPlace’s guide lists the cases where one tends to pay: a tight deadline with rent running, a first filing in an unfamiliar city, a complex job, filings in many cities at once, or a filing already rejected. It puts the usual saving for its clients at 2 to 6 weeks — a vendor claim about its own work, so treat it as a reason to get a quote, not a promise. Our earlier guide on expediter cost covers the price.

8. Keep proof of filing and follow up in the portal. Keep the confirmation and the exact set you sent. If a review sits well past the posted time, a short check with the permit number and filing date is fair. Starting work early does not help — it risks a stop-work order, added fees and removal, as our guide on building without a permit sets out.

What should I put in my schedule?

Plan from the total, not the first review. Take your city’s figure, allow for correction rounds, and add the pre-approvals in front. For a commercial job, PermitPlace’s 2 to 4 times guide is a more honest start than the posted number alone. Do not book a crew or opening date on the best case.

Frequently asked questions

How long does a building permit take on average?

The first review averages 22.9 days, with a median of 14 days, across 741 U.S. cities in PermitPlace’s 2026 study. The total wait is longer, typically 2 to 4 times the first-review figure for commercial work once corrections are counted.

What is the fastest way to get a building permit?

File a complete, correct set the first time, after clearing zoning and pre-approvals, and use a fast review your city offers if your job fits it. Then answer corrections in full, in days.

Why is my building permit taking so long?

The usual reasons are an incomplete first filing, correction rounds, a separate approval outside plan review, or a busy department past its posted target. Check which one applies in your portal.

How long does a permit take in Chicago and San Francisco?

Chicago lists 92 days for initial review and San Francisco lists 60 days, the slowest major metros in PermitPlace’s 2026 study. Those are first-review figures, so a commercial total with corrections will run longer.

Is a paid expedited review worth it?

It can be, when your daily cost of waiting is high. Philadelphia charges $2,000 to review within 5 business days against a standard 15 to 20 business days. Compare the fee with your rent, loan and crew cost for the days saved, and check eligibility first.

Can I start work while my permit is in review?

No. Review is not approval. Starting before the permit is issued risks a stop-work order, added fees and having to undo work. Wait for the issued permit, post it at the site as your city requires, and then start.

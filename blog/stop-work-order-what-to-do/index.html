What should I do if I get a stop-work order?

By PermitsByCounty Research Team · Published November 3, 2026 · Guides & Answers

QUICK ANSWER

Stop all work the order covers, right away, and leave the posted order where it is. Read the order to find the exact violation, the issuing office, and the conditions for resuming. Then fix that violation — get the missing permit, correct the code problem, or make the site safe — pay any penalties, pass a re-inspection, and wait for a written rescission before anyone starts again. In New York City, working against a stop-work order costs $6,000 for a first offense and $12,000 for later offenses, and the city will not rescind the order until those penalties are paid, per the NYC Department of Buildings.

TL;DR — the whole post in 20 seconds

  • Stop first. All covered work stops at once, not at the end of the day. Only work to make the site safe may continue, and only if the order allows it.
  • Read the paper. The order names the violation, the issuer, and what must happen before work can resume. Full and partial orders are different.
  • Fix the cited problem, not a nearby one. A missing permit needs a permit. Bad work needs a correction. A hazard needs to be removed.
  • Pay, get re-inspected, and get the release in writing. A verbal okay is not a release, and fixing the problem alone does not lift the order.
  • Do not work against the order. Penalties stack by the day or by the offense, and they can reach your permits and your license.

Stop all covered work at once — that is the first job

A stop-work order means the work it covers stops now. Not after the pour finishes. Not after the crew wraps up for the day. Now.

Tell every worker, subcontractor, and delivery driver that work is suspended. Secure the site so no one drifts back onto the cited work by habit — cover open work and lock the area if you can. Work truly needed to make the site safe is the one exception, and only where the issuing office allows it. Leave the posted order alone: taking it down is a separate problem on top of the first one.

Under Section 115 of the International Building Code, the building official can issue a stop work order when code-regulated work is done contrary to the code or in a dangerous manner; the order must be in writing, state the reason and the conditions for resuming, and be given to the owner, the owner’s agent, or the person doing the work

If you have not been given the order on paper yet, and it is not an emergency, ask the office for the written order.

Read the order before you touch anything else

The order tells you who issued it, what it cites, how much of the job it stops, and what must happen before work can resume.

A full stop work order prohibits all work except remedial work required to make the site safe, as authorised by the Commissioner; a partial stop work order prohibits only certain work or work in a particular area, with the same safe-site exception

Read the scope line word by word. If it names electrical work, the framing crew may still have a job. If it names the whole site, no one does. Take photos, note the date and time, save every paper tied to the order, and tell the owner or contractor the same day.

Why did I get a stop-work order? The usual reasons

Each order rests on a named violation or safety concern. The same five causes show up again and again.

No permit or an expired permit — starting without the required permit is the single most frequent cause, including work beyond the scope of an existing permit — building code violations, work that deviates from the approved plans, zoning violations, and unsafe conditions posing an immediate danger to workers or the public

Work out which of these caused your order before you plan the fix. A permit problem is a filing problem. A plan problem may need new drawings. A safety problem needs the hazard gone first. For work done with no permit at all, see our guides on building without a permit and on retroactive permits.

Contact the issuing office fast, with a plan in hand

Call or visit the issuing office the same day, or the next morning at the latest. Many places expect a response within 48 hours, and even where no deadline is printed, a fast call shows good faith and starts the clock on getting back to work.

Do not show up only to ask what to do. Bring a short corrective plan: the violation, the fix you propose, who will do it, and how you will prove it. Pick one point of contact and let every update run through that person.

Washington, DC shows the shape this can take, per the District of Columbia Department of Buildings’ How To Resolve a Stop Work Order page, checked October 29, 2026: do not remove the placard until authorised, submit a compliance review request with one point of contact, and within 5 business days the reviewer emails a detailed checklist. When every item is done, you request a follow-up inspection.

Fix the cited violation — the exact one, not a nearby one

The fix has to match the violation on the order. Fixing something else, even something real, does not clear the order.

If the problem is a missing or wrong permit, apply for the corrected permit, pay the fees, and wait for approval. Work already in place may need as-built drawings by a licensed architect or engineer. Wrong code work may need to be opened, torn out, and redone, or certified by an engineer. A hazard comes first; papers come second.

Violation Clinic, a Florida firm that lifts these orders, lays out six steps on its Steps to Lift a Stop Work Order page, checked October 29, 2026: freeze all activity, review the inspector notes, bring in professionals for a plan, submit corrective permits, complete the repairs, then schedule the final inspection and clear fines. Permits before repairs, inspection last.

Keep proof as you go: before-and-after photos, permit numbers, and engineer letters. Track the cost of the stop from day one too, because who pays for the delay often turns on whose violation caused the order.

Pay the penalties, pass re-inspection, and get the release in writing

Three gates stand between a fixed job and a working job. First, pay. Agencies do not release an order while penalties are still owed, even when the fix is done. What you owe depends on the place and the cause.

The penalty added to the regular permit fee is 200% of that fee for work without a required permit, beyond a permit’s scope, or by a person who must be licensed or listed and is not; it is 100% for permitted work that is unsafe or contrary to the Chicago Construction Codes or Zoning Ordinance

Source: Municipal Code of Chicago — Section 14A-12-1204.4, Stop work order penalty table · Date pulled: October 29, 2026

Second, get re-inspected. Fixing the problem does not lift the order on its own. Ask the issuing unit to verify the correction on site.

Third, get the release in writing. The order stays in effect until the building official rescinds it in writing. A friendly word at the fence is not a release, and starting on a verbal okay counts as working against the order. New York City uses the same sequence, and on a no-permit order there, work cannot resume until a permit has been issued and the order rescinded. Both, not either one.

What happens if you keep working anyway?

This is the most expensive choice on the page. Work against a live order draws fresh penalties on top of the original case, and the order will not be lifted until they are paid.

It is a violation of Section 28-207.2.2 for any person with knowledge or notice of a stop work order to allow, authorise, promote, continue, or cause covered work to continue; strict additional civil penalties are $6,000 for a first offense and $12,000 for subsequent offenses, the Department will not rescind the order until those penalties are paid, and inspectors conduct surprise inspections at stop work order sites

Elsewhere the meter often runs by the day. Daily fines in major places commonly run from roughly $1,000 to over $10,000, so two weeks of defiance can reach five figures. Past the money, permits can be revoked, a license can be suspended, and work built during the order can be ordered torn out at your cost.

If a contractor asks you to keep going quietly, that request is your answer about the contractor. If an owner presses you to work through an order, your license is on the line, not theirs — see our guide on who pulls the permit.

Can I appeal a stop-work order?

Yes, in most places, if you believe the order was issued in error — the code was read wrong, the cited rule does not apply, or your method meets the same standard another way. The usual route is a written appeal to the building department or a review board, then a hearing.

Two facts catch people off guard. Deadlines are tight, often around 15 days, so a slow decision can lose the right to appeal. And filing an appeal almost never pauses the order, so you still cannot work while it is pending, unless you win a specific stay, which is rare. Many owners fix the violation and appeal the penalty at the same time. If the department appeal fails, court is a steep next step: you generally have to show the agency acted outside its authority or made a clear error of law.

How long will the job stay stopped, and what will it cost?

There is no single number. The stop lasts as long as your fix takes, plus re-inspection and the written release. A clean paper problem can clear in days. Work that needs new drawings, an after-the-fact permit, or a tear-out can take weeks.

The costs stack in layers. The direct layer is penalties, fees, and the correction work. The quiet layer is usually bigger: loan interest, rentals that do not pause, subcontractors who move to other jobs, and delay charges after a missed completion date. In practice, the quickest honest fix usually ends up costing the least.

Frequently asked questions

What should I do first if I get a stop-work order?

Stop all covered work at once, secure the site, and leave the posted order in place. Then read the order for the violation and the conditions for resuming, and contact the issuing office the same day with a plan.

Can I keep working on parts of the job the order does not name?

Only under a partial order, and only on work the order does not cover. A full order stops everything except work needed to make the site safe, where the issuer allows it. If the scope is unclear, ask the office first.

How do I get a stop-work order lifted?

Fix the exact violation cited, pay any penalties, request a re-inspection from the unit that issued the order, and get the rescission in writing. Fixing the problem alone does not lift the order.

What happens if I ignore a stop-work order and keep working?

Fresh penalties on top of the original case — $6,000 for a first offense and $12,000 for later offenses in New York City, and daily fines of roughly $1,000 to over $10,000 in many major places. Permits can be revoked and licenses suspended.

Do I have to stop work if I appeal the order?

Yes. Filing an appeal almost never pauses the order, so the work stays stopped while the appeal is heard, unless you win a specific stay. Deadlines are tight, often around 15 days.

Who pays for the delay caused by a stop-work order?

It depends on whose violation caused the order and what your contract says. Track every shutdown cost from day one, so the claim rests on records, not memory.

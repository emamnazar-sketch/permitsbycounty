Do I need a permit to replace a water heater, furnace or electrical panel?

By PermitsByCounty Research Team · Published November 14, 2026 · Guides & Answers

QUICK ANSWER

Yes, in most places, for all three jobs. Replacing a water heater needs a plumbing permit in most jurisdictions, replacing a furnace or air conditioner needs a mechanical permit, and replacing or upgrading an electrical panel needs an electrical permit — even when the new unit is the same size and sits in the same spot, per LegalClarity's permit guides checked November 14, 2026. It’s not about the box. It’s about the gas line, the vent, and the wires the box connects to. Fees are small next to the job: a water heater permit commonly runs $25 to $250, a furnace permit about $75 to $300, and a panel permit about $50 to $500 depending on the city.

TL;DR — the whole post in 20 seconds

  • A straight swap still needs a permit in most places. Same size, same spot, same fuel — the connections are what get inspected.
  • Water heater: a plumbing permit in most jurisdictions. Furnace: a mechanical permit. Panel: an electrical permit, every time it is replaced or upsized.
  • The small jobs around them are exempt: a filter, a thermostat, a faucet, a light fixture, or a receptacle in the same spot needs no permit.
  • Fees are modest — roughly $25 to $250 for a water heater, $75 to $300 for a furnace, and $50 to $500 for a panel, by city.
  • If a contractor tells you to skip the permit to save money, that’s the red flag. A licensed contractor pulls the permit as part of the job.

Why does a straight swap need a permit at all?

This is the part that surprises people. The old water heater died on a Tuesday. The plumber brings the same size tank on Wednesday and hooks it to the same pipes. Nothing moved. Why does the city care?

Because the danger in these three jobs was never the equipment itself. It’s what the equipment connects to. A water heater connects to a gas line or a heavy electrical circuit, a vent that carries carbon monoxide out of the house, and a pressure relief valve that keeps the tank from becoming a hazard. A furnace connects to fuel, venting, and wiring. A panel is the point where every circuit in the house meets the power company’s service. Swap the box and you break and remake every one of those connections. The permit is the city’s way of sending a second pair of eyes to check that the remade connections are right.

The model code says the same thing in drier words. The International Residential Code, Section R105.2, exempts ordinary repairs from a permit — but its repair exemption stops where system work starts. Ordinary repairs cannot involve adding to, replacing, or relocating any water supply, drainage, gas, vent, electrical, or mechanical system. A new water heater, furnace, or panel is a replacement of part of one of those systems, so it sits outside the exemption even when it looks like a simple swap.

Under IRC Section R105.2, ordinary repairs are exempt from a permit, but the exemption cannot involve cutting a wall or partition, removing a structural support, or adding to, replacing, or relocating any water supply, drainage, gas, vent, electrical wiring, or mechanical system affecting safety

Do I need a permit to replace a water heater?

In most places, yes. LegalClarity’s permit guide puts it plainly: installing a water heater, gas or electric, requires a permit in most jurisdictions, because the job involves gas lines, venting, or electrical circuits that need inspection. Its replacement-in-kind guide adds the sharper version: even replacing a tank water heater with the same model often requires a permit and an inspection, for the same reason — gas or electrical connections, venting, and pressure relief are all regulated parts of the system.

Three water heater changes always push the job further into permit territory. Moving the heater to a new spot means new pipe and new venting. Switching fuel — gas to electric, or electric to a heat pump water heater — means new circuits or new gas work. And going from a tank to a tankless unit changes the venting and often the gas line size. A straight swap is the lightest version of this permit. Those three are the heavier versions, and no place treats them as exempt repairs.

Do I need a permit to replace a furnace?

Yes, in most places, and the permit has a different name. A furnace replacement takes a mechanical permit, because the work involves fuel lines, electrical connections, and exhaust venting. LegalClarity’s furnace guide is direct: most jurisdictions in the United States require a permit before you install or replace a furnace, local codes rest on the International Mechanical Code or the Uniform Mechanical Code, and those codes require permits for installing, replacing, or relocating heating equipment. Its general permit guide adds that replacing a furnace or air conditioner with a similar unit in the same location typically requires a permit for the same three reasons — combustion, venting, and electrical connections.

Replacing a furnace or air conditioner with a similar unit in the same location typically requires a mechanical permit because of the combustion, venting, and electrical connections involved; portable space heaters and window air conditioners do not require one

What does not need the permit is the care around the furnace, not the furnace itself. Changing a filter, swapping a thermostat, and cleaning or adjusting a burner are routine maintenance, below the permit line in just about every place. The line is crossed when the equipment itself is installed, replaced, or moved — or when the job grows to new ductwork, a fuel conversion, or a new circuit for the unit. If your quote includes any of those, the permit should be on it by name.

Do I need a permit to replace or upgrade an electrical panel?

Yes — this is the clearest of the three. LegalClarity’s permit guide lists the electrical triggers in one line: an electrical permit is required whenever you add new circuits, install a new panel, run wiring to a new location, or upgrade your service capacity. A panel replacement touches at least two of those four. An upgrade from 100 amps to 200 amps touches all of them, and adds the power company, which has to disconnect and reconnect the service and usually wants to see the passed inspection before it does.

An electrical permit is required whenever you add new circuits, install a new panel, run wiring to a new location, or upgrade your service capacity; replacing an existing light fixture or swapping an outlet or switch in the same location does not need a permit

Panel permit fees run a little higher than the other two, and they’re still a small share of the job. Against a panel upgrade that commonly costs a few thousand dollars all in, the permit and inspection are the cheapest safety check on the invoice.

Which small jobs around them need no permit?

The exempt list is real, and it is worth knowing, because it shows where the line actually sits. You can replace a faucet, a toilet, or a showerhead in the same spot. You can swap a light fixture, an outlet, or a switch in the same location. You can change a furnace filter, replace a thermostat, clear a drain, stop a leak, and plug in a portable heater or a window air conditioner. None of that changes a system. You are using the system as it stands, or maintaining the equipment on it.

The moment a small job changes the system, it crosses over. Adding one outlet means new wire back to the panel. Moving a sink means new pipe. The question for any job is the same: does this touch a pipe, a wire, a gas line, a vent, or a duct in a new way? If it does, expect a permit. If it only touches fixtures in place, expect none.

Who pulls the permit, and what does the inspection check?

On all three jobs, the licensed contractor normally pulls the permit and builds the fee into the quote. That’s not a courtesy — in many places only a licensed trade contractor can pull a trade permit at all, and a contractor who offers to skip it to lower the price is telling you something about every other job on their truck. Some places let an owner pull one for a home they live in. Rentals and flips usually do not qualify.

The inspection is short and specific. For a water heater, the inspector checks venting, the pressure relief valve and its drain line, the connections, and, in earthquake country, the strapping. For a furnace: venting, combustion air, fuel connections, and the hookup. For a panel: wire sizes against the breakers, grounding and bonding, labeling, working space, and that outdoor disconnect. Most simple swaps get one final inspection. Keep the signed-off permit with your house papers — it is the record a buyer, a lender, and an insurer will look for later, and our guide to checking a house’s permit history before you buy shows how that search reads a home with clean records against one with gaps.

What happens if the work is done without a permit?

The permit fee is the cheapest version of this story. LegalClarity’s furnace guide states the common penalty pattern: many jurisdictions charge double or triple the original permit fee when work is caught without one, and some add daily fines until the violation is cleared. On a $150 permit, that’s real money spent to save $150.

The quieter costs last longer. Unpermitted equipment leaves a gap in the permit record for that address, and gaps get found — by a buyer’s search before a closing, by an appraiser, or by an insurer after a fire or flood tied to the equipment. Fixing the record later means a permit after the fact, the penalty fee, and sometimes opening finished work so the inspector can see the connections. None of this is a reason to fear a normal replacement — these are the most routine permits a building department issues, and simple ones may be approved the same day.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a permit to replace a water heater with the same size tank in the same spot?

In most jurisdictions, yes. Even a same-model swap remakes the gas or electrical connections, the venting, and the pressure relief setup, and those are the parts the code regulates. A few places exempt a true like-for-like swap, so confirm with your building department — but plan on the permit, which commonly costs $25 to $250.

Do I need a permit to replace my furnace if the new one is the same kind?

Yes, in most places. A furnace replacement takes a mechanical permit because of the fuel line, venting, and electrical connections, even when the new unit is similar and sits in the same location. Changing a filter or a thermostat needs no permit. Fees typically run $75 to $300, and routine permits often issue within a few business days.

Do I need a permit to upgrade my electrical panel from 100 to 200 amps?

Yes, always. Installing a new panel or upgrading service capacity is a named trigger for an electrical permit, and the power company usually coordinates its disconnect and reconnect around the inspection. Expect the permit to run roughly $50 to $500 depending on the city, and expect current code items — like an outdoor emergency disconnect — to be part of the job.

Can I pull the permit myself, or does my contractor have to?

It depends on the place and the trade. Some jurisdictions let an owner pull a permit for a home they own and occupy, but rentals and resale flips usually do not qualify, and some trade permits can only be pulled by a licensed contractor. On gas, furnace, and panel work, the licensed contractor pulling the permit as part of the job is the normal and safer route.

What if my water heater fails at night or on a weekend?

Many jurisdictions allow an emergency replacement first, with the permit pulled right after, because a home cannot sit without hot water while an office is closed. The permit and the inspection are still required — only the order changes. Ask your plumber how your city handles emergency swaps before the work starts.

What happens if a previous owner replaced the water heater or panel without a permit?

The gap sits in the permit record for the address, and it usually surfaces when you sell, refinance, or file an insurance claim tied to the equipment. The usual fix is a permit pulled after the fact, a penalty fee that is often double or triple the normal one, and an inspection of the existing work — sometimes with finished surfaces opened so the inspector can see the connections.

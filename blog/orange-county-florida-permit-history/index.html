How do I find a property’s permit history in Orange County, Florida?

By PermitsByCounty Research Team · Published November 13, 2026 · Guides & Answers

QUICK ANSWER

Start with the parcel, not the portal. Look the property up on the Orange County Property Appraiser site and read the Municipality field: if it says Orlando, search the City of Orlando’s Permit and Planning Project Lookup; if it says Unincorporated, search Orange County’s OC Fast Track. The two governments run separate systems and the records do not cross, per the Orange County expediting guide checked November 10, 2026. In Fast Track, search the property twice — once by address and once by parcel ID — because each search returns only part of the record, per the Fast Track Properties page, checked November 10, 2026.

TL;DR — the whole post in 20 seconds

  • Your mailing address does not pick the system. A lot of addresses that say Orlando sit in unincorporated Orange County.
  • Check the parcel first. The Property Appraiser’s Municipality field names the government that holds the permits.
  • City of Orlando property: search the city’s project lookup by permit number, address, project name, or parcel ID.
  • Unincorporated county property: search OC Fast Track twice, by address and by parcel, and open the Building Permits list too.
  • An empty result usually means the wrong system, not a clean property. Search the other system before you trust it.

How does an Orange County FL permit lookup split between the city and the county?

It splits at the city limit line, and the line is not where most people guess. The City of Orlando sits inside Orange County, but the city permits its own ground. Land outside every city limit — unincorporated Orange County — is permitted by the county. A house can carry an Orlando mailing address and still sit on county ground, because a mailing address follows the post office, not the city boundary.

That split is not a paperwork detail. The county and the city run separate departments on separate systems, and a permit filed with one never shows up in the other.

Orange County’s Division of Building Safety, using OC Fast Track, has jurisdiction only in unincorporated Orange County; the City of Orlando runs its own Permitting Services Division under Economic Development, with ProjectDox for plan review and a separate permit lookup and payment site; records do not cross

Two departments, two systems, no crossing — Orange County, Florida · County guide checked November 10, 2026

Source: Southwest Florida Permit Expediters, Orange County FL permit expediting guide · Date pulled: November 10, 2026

So the first job is not searching. The first job is naming the right government. Every wrong-system search after that wastes time and can leave you thinking a property is clean when you never looked in the drawer where its permits sit. The Florida open-permit directory puts the rule in one line: establish the jurisdiction first, because a city permit will not appear in the county’s system, and if you are not certain, search both.

Our guide to checking a house’s permit history before you buy covers the national habit — match every visible addition and remodel to a closed permit. This post is the Orange County version of the first step: which drawer to open.

How do you tell whether a property sits in Orlando or in unincorporated Orange County?

You read the parcel record. The Orange County Property Appraiser keeps a record for every parcel in the county, city parcels included, and that record names the municipality the parcel sits in. That one field settles the question the mailing address cannot.

Go to the Orange County Property Appraiser website (ocpafl.org), search the property address, and look at the Municipality field in the results: “Orlando” means City of Orlando rules apply, and “Unincorporated” means Orange County rules apply

Write down two things from that parcel page before you move on: the municipality, and the parcel ID. The parcel ID is the second key for every search that follows. Address searches miss permits for ordinary reasons — a street suffix typed differently, a unit number, a corner lot recorded under the side street. A parcel ID does not care how the address is spelled.

If the Municipality field names a city other than Orlando — Winter Park, Apopka, or any other Orange County city — neither the Orlando lookup nor the county system is your first stop. That city keeps its own permits. There is a section on those cities below.

How do you search unincorporated Orange County in OC Fast Track?

You run two different searches, because Fast Track stores two different kinds of history. The county says so on its own Properties page: a parcel and an address are not two spellings of the same search. They pull different records.

Start with the Property search. In Fast Track, click Property (Address/Parcel) in the sidebar and search the property. That is the search the county itself points people to for past permits and expired permits: the Division of Building Safety’s own answer to “How do I find out if a property has expired permits?” sends you to that Property search, by address or parcel ID, where information about the past will be displayed.

The county tracks projects by parcel when the subject of the entitlement is related to the land — variances, zoning exceptions, development plans, rezoning, plats, conservation determinations, flood permits — and tracks projects by address when the record is based on a structure, such as a house, store, or electrical meter; only a subset of records will be returned from each search

Why the address search and the parcel search return different records — Orange County, Florida · Fast Track Properties page checked November 10, 2026

Source: OC FastTrack, Properties (Address or Parcel) · Date pulled: November 10, 2026

Then run the second search, the permit list itself. In the left-hand orange column, click “Permits, All Types,” then “Building Permits,” and enter the permit number, the address, or the parcel ID. The county describes the result as real-time: when the building permit number appears at the bottom of the screen, click it, and every item in blue is a link to more detail.

Permit status can be viewed in real-time on Fast Track Online Services: in the left-hand orange column, click on “Permits, All Types” then click on “Building Permits” and enter the permit number, address or parcel ID; when the building permit number appears at the bottom of the screen, click on it — every item in blue is a hyperlink to more information

Two habits make these searches work better. First, if a search finds nothing, try fewer words — just the street number and the street name. The county gives that tip itself, and adds a quirk worth knowing: the street-name field matches from the start of the name, so “Lake” finds Lake and Lakeside alike. Second, you do not need an account to do any of this research.

Fast Track offers services including searching for permits, investigative reports, addresses and parcels; many tools are available to all visitors, but permit applications and electronic plan submittals require a Fast Track Member Services account

The county’s building permits cover six kinds of work: building, electrical, gas, mechanical, plumbing, and roofing. Read the whole list for the property, not just the building lines. A roof, a panel upgrade, and an air-conditioner swap each leave their own line, and the trade lines are often the ones that tell you when the big systems were last touched — the same reading habit our roof and HVAC age guide teaches.

How do you search a property inside the City of Orlando?

You use the city’s own lookup, and you can search it four ways. The City of Orlando’s Permits & Inspections page says you can check a permit with your permit number, address, project name, or parcel ID in its project lookup tool. For a history search, start with the address, then run the parcel ID from the Property Appraiser page as your second pass — same two-key habit as the county side.

You can use your permit number, address, project name or parcel ID to check the status of a permit in the City of Orlando’s project lookup tool

The four ways into the Orlando lookup — Orlando, Florida · Permits & Inspections page checked November 10, 2026

Source: City of Orlando, Permits & Inspections · Date pulled: November 10, 2026

Keep two city systems straight, because sellers and agents mix them up. ProjectDox is where plans are submitted and reviewed for a new city permit — PermitPlace’s Orlando guide describes the city’s fully digital system that way, with the Permit and Planning Project Lookup kept as the separate tool for searching existing permits. For history, you want the lookup, not the plan portal. The same guide lists one more route for a single known permit: the PROMPT phone system at 407.246.4444 gives automated status updates.

A city history and a county history for the same street can both exist without either being wrong. Orlando has annexed land over the years, and older county permits do not move into the city system. If a property sits near the city line, or the seller remembers a county permit from years back, run both systems and keep both lists. That is not double work — it is the whole check.

What if the property sits in another Orange County city?

Then that city is the source, and neither main system will give you a full history. Orlando is the biggest city in the county, not the only one. Winter Park, Maitland, Edgewood, and Belle Isle each run their own permitting, and the expediting guide for the county warns that large parts of the Orlando postal area are unincorporated county while those neighboring cities are separate again — the parcel record decides which one you are dealing with.

The Central Florida reality is fragmentation. A realty directory for the metro lists dozens of separate city and county permit portals, each with its own search and its own record depth. There is no single Orlando-area permit drawer. So read the Municipality field literally: whatever government it names is where you search first. If that city’s portal looks thin, call its building department and ask how far back its online records go and how older permits are requested. A short portal history is a limit of the portal, not proof the property has no past.

How do you read the history once you find it?

You read it the same way in both systems: one line at a time, newest first, and you sort every line into one of three piles — closed, open, or expired. A closed or finaled line is finished work: the permit was issued, the work was inspected, and the record was closed. That is the line that proves a roof, an addition, or a remodel was done with a permit.

An open line means the record never got its final inspection. The work may be done and fine. The paper is not done. In the county system, the same word carries a second meaning during review: the Division of Building Safety notes that if any required process on a permit is marked Open, that permit is not ready to be issued. Read the line’s own detail page before you decide which kind of open you are looking at — a permit still in review, or old finished work that was never finaled.

An expired line has its own county clock.

An Orange County permit expires 180 days after the date it was issued or 180 days from the last passed inspection; a one-time 90-day extension can be granted on active permits for a fee; expired permits must be replaced with new permits and all remaining inspections must be performed; and in order to close out any permits, a final inspection is required

Write down, for every line that is not closed: the permit number, the date, the work named, and the status in plain words. Then match the list against the house in front of you. Every visible addition, conversion, roof, and system swap should have a closed line. Work with no line at all is a different problem from work with an open line — our guide to telling whether a flip has unpermitted work before you offer walks through that check. And if an open line turns up days before a closing, our guide to open permits and closing dates covers why the timing matters.

What does an empty result actually mean?

Usually it means you searched the wrong drawer. The Florida open-permit directory states the pattern plainly: if a search comes up empty for a property you know has permit history, that usually means you are in the wrong system rather than that the property is clean. In Orange County that warning does double duty, because the county-city split gives every searcher a second drawer to miss.

Portal names and URLs change; if a search comes up empty for a property you know has permit history, that usually means you are in the wrong system rather than that the property is clean; a full check may mean two searches, city and county

Empty usually means wrong system — Florida, United States · Open permit search directory checked November 10, 2026

Source: 1ContractorSolutions, Florida Open Permit Search Directory · Date pulled: November 10, 2026

There is a second, quieter limit. Even in the right system, each Fast Track search returns only part of the record — that is the county’s own warning on its Properties page — and the county also says it is still entering the history of older development projects into the system, so a record you are certain should exist may not be online yet. The county’s advice in that case is to contact the proper agency. Treat an empty online result as “not found online,” never as “no permits exist.” For a purchase, do the careful version. Get into the right system, search by address and by parcel, and write down both piles. Then call the building department about anything the portal cannot settle.

Frequently asked questions

How do I find a property’s permit history in Orange County, Florida?

First find the parcel on the Orange County Property Appraiser site and read its Municipality field. If it says Orlando, search the City of Orlando’s project lookup. If it says Unincorporated, search OC Fast Track. In either system, search by address and by parcel ID, because each search can return records the other misses.

Why does my Orlando mailing address not tell me where to search?

Because a mailing address follows the post office, not the city boundary. A large share of addresses that say Orlando sit in unincorporated Orange County, whose permits live in the county’s Fast Track system, not the city’s. Other addresses sit inside smaller cities with their own systems. The parcel’s Municipality field is the record that settles it.

Do I need an account to search OC Fast Track?

No. Many Fast Track tools, including searching for permits, addresses, and parcels, are available to all visitors. A Fast Track Member Services account is needed to submit permit applications and electronic plans, not to research a property’s history.

What is the difference between searching by address and by parcel in Fast Track?

The county tracks land records by parcel — variances, zoning exceptions, development plans, rezoning, plats, and flood permits — and structure records by address, such as a house, a store, or an electrical meter. Each search returns only a subset of the property’s records, so a full history takes both.

What if my permit search comes up empty?

Do not read it as a clean property. You are most likely in the wrong system — city records do not appear in the county search, and county records do not appear in the city’s. Search the other system, try just the street number and street name, and try the parcel ID. If it still looks empty, call the building department: the county is still entering older project history into Fast Track.

What if the property is in Winter Park or another city, not Orlando?

Search that city’s own permitting system. Cities inside Orange County keep their own permits, so neither the Orlando lookup nor OC Fast Track holds a full history for them. The Municipality field on the parcel record names the city. If its portal only covers recent years, ask its building department how to request the older record.

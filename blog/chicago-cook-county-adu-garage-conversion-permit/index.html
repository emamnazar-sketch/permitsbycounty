Do I need a permit for an ADU or garage conversion in Chicago and Cook County?

By PermitsByCounty Research Team · Published November 17, 2026 · Guides & Answers

QUICK ANSWER

Yes. In Chicago, an ADU or a garage conversion into a home always needs a permit, and the permit comes in two stages. First you get pre-certification from the Chicago Department of Housing, which confirms your address is eligible under the ADU ordinance that took effect citywide in expanded form on April 1, 2026. Then you get a building permit from the Chicago Department of Buildings, with plans drawn by an Illinois-licensed architect. In unincorporated Cook County, outside Chicago and outside every suburb, there’s no standard county ADU permit path listed — the county’s e-permit list covers sheds and detached garages as structures, not as homes — so you’ll need to ask the county Building and Zoning department in writing before you design anything.

TL;DR — the whole post in 20 seconds

  • A Chicago ADU permit is required for every coach house, basement or attic conversion unit, and garage conversion. There is no small-job exemption for creating a new home.
  • Stage one is Department of Housing pre-certification. No building permit is issued without it.
  • Stage two is a Department of Buildings permit. The 2026 minimum is $1,800 for a conversion that adds a dwelling unit and $2,450 for new residential construction such as a new coach house, with a $600 non-refundable deposit at submission.
  • Eligibility expanded on April 1, 2026: ADUs are allowed by right in all RT and RM districts citywide, and in RS single-family districts in 34 wards that have opted in. Over 320,000 parcels are now eligible, up from about 116,000 under the pilot.
  • A garage used as a home in unincorporated Cook County or in a suburb is not covered by Chicago’s ordinance. Each suburb writes its own rule, and the county lists no by-right ADU use.

Yes, you need a permit — and in Chicago it is two permits in sequence

Start with the plain answer, because a lot of garage work in Chicago gets talked about as if it were a remodel. It isn’t a remodel in the city’s eyes. The moment a garage, a basement, or an attic becomes a separate home — its own kitchen, its own bathroom, its own entrance, someone sleeping there full time — you have created a new dwelling unit. Chicago treats a new dwelling unit as new residential work, and new residential work needs a permit.

That was true even before the new ordinance. What the ordinance changed is whether the unit is allowed at all. Chicago banned new ADUs in 1957. A pilot program brought them back in five areas on May 1, 2021. The permanent expansion, ordinance SO2024-0008918, passed the City Council on September 25, 2025 by 46 votes to none and took effect on April 1, 2026. If your address is eligible under that ordinance, the permit path below is open to you. If it isn’t eligible, no permit can fix that — the use itself isn’t allowed there yet.

The two stages trip people up, so name them early. The Department of Housing, called DOH, pre-certifies the project. The Department of Buildings, called DOB, issues the building permit. DOH goes first, always. DOB will not issue an ADU building permit unless DOH has certified the project. Filing your building plans first doesn’t speed anything up. It just puts your plans in a queue they can’t leave until the housing certification arrives.

Who is eligible in Chicago now

Eligibility follows your zoning district and, for single-family areas, your ward.

In RT districts, the two-flat and townhouse districts, and in RM districts, the multi-unit districts, ADUs are now allowed by right across the whole city, apart from downtown districts. Certain business and commercial districts allow them too. If your building sits in RT or RM, the address question is mostly settled in your favor, and the rest of this post is about the permit steps.

In RS districts, the single-family districts, an ADU is allowed only where the area has been added to the ADU-allowed RS areas. Alderpersons opt their wards in, area by area. By the April 2026 launch, RS districts in 34 wards had eligible areas, and two follow-on ordinances have added more since. That means two houses on the same street can have different answers if a ward or area line runs between them. Don’t rely on a neighbor’s approval. Check your own address on the city’s eligibility map at chicago.gov/adu before you pay anyone to draw plans.

There are building rules on top of the zoning rules. A conversion unit — a new unit inside your existing building, in a basement or an attic — is allowed only when the principal building has lawfully existed for at least 20 years. A coach house, the detached unit at the back of the lot, can be added regardless of the main building’s age, as long as the main building is there first. For a property with one to four existing units, you can add one coach house, or one conversion unit if the building is old enough. You can’t build both a coach house and a conversion unit on the same property. For properties with five or more units, coach houses are not permitted, but conversion units can be added up to a set share of the existing count.

One more eligibility point matters for garage owners in particular. Converting an existing detached garage into a coach house is a conversion of that garage, but the home you create is still a coach house in the ordinance’s terms: a detached dwelling unit at the rear of the lot. It must sit behind the main building, within the rear half of the lot. Calling it a garage conversion in conversation does not move it into the cheaper or simpler conversion-unit category.

The size and shape rules for a coach house and a garage conversion

A coach house has to fit a small box, and most failed designs fail here, on paper, before any fee is paid.

The floor area of a coach house is capped at 60 percent of the lot’s required rear yard area. On a standard Chicago lot, that works out to roughly 450 square feet of living space. Deeper lots can allow more. Height is capped at 22 feet, measured to the top of the highest feature. The unit must sit within the rear half of the lot, behind the main building, with setbacks of 2 feet from the rear and side lot lines.

A conversion unit inside the main building is capped at 700 square feet, or the size of the existing unit it sits alongside, whichever is less. Habitable rooms need a ceiling height of at least 7 feet 6 inches. That one line ends a lot of basement plans, because many Chicago basements sit below that height as built, and raising the ceiling means digging down or lifting the house. Price that before you fall in love with the plan.

No extra off-street parking is required when you add an ADU, even if the coach house takes the place of a parking space or a garage bay. That rule is a real change from the old code, where parking is part of why ADUs were banned in 1957.

Every ADU plan must be prepared by an architect licensed in Illinois. That isn’t a suggestion in the ordinance summary pages. It’s a stated requirement, for coach houses and conversions alike. And a new coach house carries one more Chicago-only rule: the contractors building it must take part in a U.S. Department of Labor-approved registered apprenticeship program. Conversion units are exempt from that contractor rule. If you are converting an existing garage with a small local crew, ask early whether the apprenticeship rule reaches your project as the city classifies it, and get the answer from DOH in your pre-certification, not from the crew.

Stage one: Housing pre-certification

Pre-certification is the city confirming two things: your property is eligible, and the unit you propose fits the program’s rules, including any affordability duty.

You apply through the ADU portal at chicago.gov/adu. The portal gives feedback tied to your address and your proposed unit, and the city built an eligibility map into the same site, so start there even before the formal application. Expect to file a neighbor notification affidavit as part of the process. Expect the affordability question to be answered at this stage too, because DOH, not DOB, advises you whether any of your proposed units must be kept affordable.

The affordability rule is narrow, and most single-ADU owners will not hit it. If you add two or more ADUs to a property, half of the added units, rounded down, must be rented at rates affordable to households earning 60 percent of the area median income, and kept that way for 30 years, with a $500 registration charge per affordable unit in the published fee summaries. One ADU on its own does not trigger it. Two or more can, so a plan that grows from one unit to two mid-design needs a fresh DOH answer.

Demand at this stage is real, not theoretical. By March 18, 2026, before the expansion had even taken effect, DOH had already pre-certified 816 ADUs under the program, with roughly 85 percent of them on the North and Northwest sides. That tells you two practical things. The office is processing these at volume, and the early wave is coming from the areas where eligibility arrived first. If your ward has only just opted in, you may be among the first files your reviewers have seen from your area. Clean, complete paperwork matters more, not less, in that spot.

Don’t pay for full architectural drawings before pre-certification clears. That’s the single most repeated piece of advice in the current Chicago guides, and it’s sound. Pre-certification is the cheap gate. Drawings are the expensive step. Run them in that order.

Stage two: the Buildings permit, and what the city charges

With pre-certification in hand, your architect files for the building permit with DOB. The permit is plan-based: the fee is worked out from a formula using construction type, scope, and area, and then a minimum fee applies if the formula lands below it. For ADU work, the minimums are the numbers to budget, because typical ADU sizes usually land below them.

For a conversion that increases the number of dwelling units — the basement or attic case — the 2026 minimum permit fee is $1,800. For new residential construction up to four stories and three units — the new coach house case — the minimum is $2,450. A $600 non-refundable deposit is collected when you submit. Add a Title 17 zoning review charge where it applies, a $500 administrative adjustment fee in the cases that need one, and separate trade work on top: electrical, plumbing, and any new water or sewer connection carry their own charges, and a new 1-inch water tap was priced at $2,000 in the Department of Water Management schedule carried in the 2026 summaries. Treat $1,800 and $2,450 as floors for the main permit, not as your total city bill.

How long does the building stage take? Past Chicago permit records put Standard Plan Review at a median of about 120 days from application to issuance in the recent data summarized this year, and the full run from first application to an approved permit at four to six months in current ADU guides. Those are medians from past records, not promises, and a corrections round adds weeks. Budget the time the way you budget the fee: as a range with room in it, not as a date.

The build itself then has to pass inspection as a home, not as a garage. A legal dwelling unit needs proper ceiling height, light and ventilation, safe stairs and exits, fire separation where the code asks for it, heating, plumbing, and electrical work done to residential code, and rapid, ordinary things a garage never needed. A garage shell that looks solid can still need deep work underneath: many older Chicago garages were built without the footings a home needs, and a conversion that adds a floor above a garage is a structural question first and a finishes question last. Get a structural answer before you price the kitchen.

Short-term rental is off the table, by rule. An ADU permitted under this ordinance can’t be used as a short-term rental on platforms like Airbnb or VRBO. The unit has to be a long-term home, rented on a normal lease or lived in by family. If your whole business case rests on nightly rental, the Chicago ADU permit is the wrong tool, and finding that out now is cheaper than finding it out after the build.

Owner occupancy needs a careful read, because the summaries don’t all state it the same way for every area. The pilot-era rules required owner occupancy in the West, South, and Southeast areas for smaller properties, and current summaries state an owner-occupancy duty for properties with two units or fewer in the expanded program, with larger buildings treated differently. Because this point decides whether an investor-owned two-flat can add a unit at all, confirm it for your exact address inside pre-certification, where DOH gives the binding answer for your file.

The Cook County answer outside Chicago

Now the part of the title that catches people out. Cook County isn’t one permit office. Chicago runs its own. Every suburb runs its own. Cook County’s Department of Building and Zoning runs permits only in unincorporated Cook County — the pockets of land that sit outside Chicago and outside every incorporated suburb. The county says so plainly on its e-permits page, and tells you to check your parcel in CookViewer before you apply, because the county cannot process a permit for a property inside Chicago or inside a suburb.

In that unincorporated area, the county’s online e-permit list is a list of structures and jobs: deck, driveway, fence, flatwork, generator, masonry wall, pool, re-roof, shed or detached garage, signs, sump pump, tent, and window replacement. A detached garage is on that list as a structure. Living in it isn’t. There is no ADU, coach house, or garage-dwelling item in the county’s standard e-permit list, and Illinois has no statewide ADU law that supplies one: ADU rules in Illinois are local, city by city and village by village, as the Illinois Municipal League confirmed in March 2026. So the honest county answer isn’t yes and it isn’t no. It’s this: there’s no clear, by-right county ADU path published, so a garage conversion into a home in unincorporated Cook County needs a direct answer from the county Building and Zoning department, in writing, before design money is spent. The county takes combined or unlisted scopes by email intake, which is the route to use for that question.

The same warning, doubled, applies in the suburbs. Oak Park, Evanston, and a near-dozen other northeastern Illinois communities allow ADUs in some form, each on its own terms. Other suburbs do not allow them at all, and some codes bar using a detached garage as a dwelling in so many words. A Chicago answer never travels across a city line. If your garage sits in a suburb, your suburb’s zoning code is the only document that matters, and its building department is the only office that can clear you.

One cost note belongs in every Cook County plan, whichever office permits you. An ADU is an improvement, and the Cook County Assessor treats it as one. A current builder guide, citing the Assessor’s position, puts the added tax on a typical $200,000 ADU at roughly $3,000 to $5,000 a year, depending on the tax rate and exemptions. That figure is a guide estimate, not a county schedule, but the direction isn’t in doubt: the new home will be assessed, and the bill will rise. Put it in the rental math from the start.

Common mistakes

The first mistake is filing with the wrong office. A Chicago address goes to Chicago. An unincorporated parcel goes to the county. A suburban address goes to the suburb. Each office will turn away a file that belongs to another, and weeks go with it.

The second mistake is skipping pre-certification, or paying for full drawings before it clears. DOH certification is the gate in front of the DOB permit. There’s no way around it, and drawings drawn for a unit the program won’t certify are money spent twice.

The third mistake is treating a garage conversion as the cheap category. In Chicago terms, a home in a detached garage is a coach house, with the coach house size, height, setback, architect, and — for new coach house work — apprenticeship rules. Budget it as a small home, because the code does.

The fourth mistake is planning on short-term rental income. Chicago ADUs can’t be short-term rentals. A plan that only works at nightly rates does not work.

The fifth mistake is assuming a neighbor’s ward answer is yours. RS eligibility is drawn by area, and it has changed three times in a year. Check your own address on the current map, on the day you start.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a permit to convert my Chicago garage into an apartment?

Yes. A garage that becomes a home is a coach house under Chicago’s ADU ordinance. You need Department of Housing pre-certification first, then a Department of Buildings permit, with plans by an Illinois-licensed architect. There is no exemption for using the existing garage shell.

Can I add an ADU to a single-family home in Chicago?

Only if your RS area has been added to the ADU-allowed areas. RS districts in 34 wards had eligible areas at the April 2026 launch, and more areas have been added since. Check your exact address on the eligibility map at chicago.gov/adu. RT and RM districts allow ADUs by right citywide.

How much does a Chicago ADU permit cost?

Budget from the 2026 minimums: $1,800 for a conversion that adds a dwelling unit inside your building, and $2,450 for new residential construction such as a new coach house, plus a $600 non-refundable deposit at submission. Zoning review, any $500 administrative adjustment, trade permits, and water or sewer charges sit on top of the main permit.

How long does a Chicago ADU permit take?

Plan on two stages. Housing pre-certification comes first, then the Buildings permit. Recent Chicago records put Standard Plan Review at a median of about 120 days, and current guides put the full run to an approved permit at roughly four to six months. Corrections rounds add time.

Can I rent my Chicago ADU on Airbnb?

No. Chicago’s ordinance bars short-term rental of ADUs. The unit must be used as a long-term home, on a normal lease or by family. A plan built on nightly rental income will not be permitted on that basis.

Does Chicago’s ADU ordinance cover unincorporated Cook County or the suburbs?

No. Chicago’s ordinance covers Chicago only. Unincorporated Cook County has no ADU item in its standard e-permit list, and each suburb writes its own rule — some allow ADUs, some bar them. Ask the office that governs your parcel, in writing, before you design.

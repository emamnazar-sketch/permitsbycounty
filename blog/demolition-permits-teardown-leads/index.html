A demolition permit names the property, the owner or applicant, and often the firm that filed it, weeks before the building comes down and a new build starts. TL;DR — the whole post in 20 seconds

  • A demolition permit is the earliest public sign that a lot is about to clear. By the time a fence goes up, the job is usually already placed.
  • Read four fields first: the address, the owner or applicant, the type of structure, and any linked new-construction permit on the same lot.
  • A teardown tied to a new build is the best lead. It means an owner or builder has money in the project and a schedule to keep.
  • Call early and plainly. Name the permit you saw, the address, and the week you can walk the site. Do not guess at a price from the permit alone.
  • Work the same counties every week. A short, steady list beats a wide list you only check once a month.

Why a demolition permit is such an early signal

Most trades get called after a project is already moving. Demolition is different. Nothing else can start until the old structure is gone, the utilities are cut, and the lot is clear. That puts the demolition permit near the front of the whole job.

It also puts it in public view early. An owner who wants to tear down a house and build a new one has to file for permission first. The filing shows up in the county record before the excavator shows up on the street. Teardown buying guides for places like Fort Lauderdale, Bergen County, and Dallas all start from the same place for the same reason: demolition permits and records are where a teardown shows itself first.

That timing is the whole value of demolition permits teardown leads. You are not chasing a job that was bid last month. You are reading a public record in the week it was created, while the owner is still lining up the work that has to happen next.

There is a second signal hiding in the same record. A lot of teardowns are not the end of a story. They are the start of one. A small old house comes down and a larger new house goes up on the same lot. When you see a demolition permit and a new-construction permit for the same address, you are looking at an owner or a builder with a real project, real financing, and a real reason to keep the schedule moving. That is a better call than a bare demolition with no plan behind it.

What one demolition permit actually tells you

Start with the address. That sounds obvious, but the address does more than locate the job. It tells you the neighborhood, the lot size you can check from the county record, and whether this is the kind of street where teardowns are already happening. One teardown on a street often means the next one is not far behind, because the math that worked for one owner tends to work for the neighbor too.

Next, read who filed it. The applicant field may name the owner, a builder, or an architect. If a builder filed it, that builder is your buyer — they need a demolition crew they can call again on the next lot. If the owner filed it on their own, the owner is your buyer, and they may not have a crew lined up at all yet. Those are two different calls, and the permit tells you which one to make.

Then read the structure. A single-family house, a garage, a small commercial building, and a full commercial structure are four different jobs. They need different equipment, different haul-off, and different care around neighbors and utilities. The permit type and description usually give you enough to know whether the job fits your crew before you spend time on a site walk.

Last, look for what comes next on the same lot. Search the same address for a new-construction, remodel, or addition permit. A linked build permit tells you the teardown has a purpose and a timeline. No linked permit does not kill the lead — owners often file demolition first — but it does change your question on the call. You ask what is planned for the lot, and you listen for whether there is a schedule at all.

A weekly routine that actually finds jobs

Pick your counties first, and keep the list short. The counties where you can price work fast, get your equipment there without a long haul, and pull the local requirements without learning a new system every time. For most small demolition firms that is two or three counties, not ten. A short list you work every week will beat a long list you work when things are slow.

Then set a fixed day to read new permits. Monday morning works for a lot of crews because filings from the prior week are in the record and owners are back at their phones. Filter for demolition permits only, then sort by date so the newest filings sit on top. You want the permits from the last seven days, not a pile from last quarter.

For each permit that fits, do a two-minute check before you call. Confirm the address is in your service area. Note the structure type. Check the same address for a linked build permit. Note the owner or builder name exactly as it is spelled on the record — names get spelled more than one way, and a clean note now saves a messy search later.

Now call. Keep it short and specific. Say who you are, say you saw the demolition permit for that address, and ask if the teardown work has been placed yet. If it has not, ask to walk the site that week. If it has, ask who is building on the lot next — a placed teardown can still lead you to the builder who will file the next one.

Write down what you learn, even when the answer is no. A builder who said no in November because the job was placed may be the same builder filing three more permits in February. Your notes are what turn a public record into a working list over time. That list, built week by week, is worth more than any single permit.

How to price the first walk, not the permit

Do not quote a teardown from the permit record alone. The record will not tell you what is inside the walls, whether there is asbestos that needs a survey and a separate abatement step, how the utilities are run, or how tight the lot is for equipment and trucks. Those are the things that set the real price, and every one of them needs eyes on the site.

Use the permit to earn the walk. On the walk, check access first: can your equipment get in, can trucks get out loaded, and where does material get staged without blocking the street or the neighbors. Check the structure itself: foundation type, additions that may have been built in different eras, and any part of the building that sits close to a property line or another structure.

Ask about utilities and approvals in plain terms. Gas, water, sewer, and electric service have to be cut or capped the right way, and the rules for that step sit with the city or county and the utility, not with you alone. Ask whether an asbestos survey has been done. In many places a survey, and notice to the state or local air agency, has to happen before demolition starts. If the owner does not know, say so plainly and tell them what step comes first. That honest answer is often what wins you the job over a crew that just threw out a low number.

Then price the full job: the teardown, the haul-off and disposal, the utility coordination, and leaving the lot in the condition the next builder expects. A clear lot, graded and ready, is the product. Name what is included and what is not, in writing, so the owner can compare your bid fairly against another crew’s bid.

The mistakes that cost demolition crews these jobs

The first mistake is waiting for the phone to ring. Teardown work is placed early because it sits at the front of the schedule. A crew that only takes inbound calls is seeing the jobs that nobody else wanted, or the jobs where the first crew fell through. Reading permits each week is how you get in front of the good ones.

The second mistake is calling too late with a generic pitch. If you call three weeks after the permit issued and open with a broad line about your services, the owner hears a sales call. If you call in the first week and name their address and their project, they hear a crew that is paying attention. Same firm, same equipment — a very different call.

The third mistake is chasing every demolition permit in a wide area. A permit two hours away that ties up your crew and your trucks for a small garage teardown can cost you the house teardown twenty minutes away that filed the same week. Fit matters more than volume. Work the ground you can serve well.

The fourth mistake is skipping the rebuild check. A teardown with a new build behind it has an owner who needs the lot clear on a date. A teardown with nothing behind it may sit for months while the owner decides. Both are worth a call, but they are not worth the same amount of your week. Spend your best follow-up time where a schedule already exists.

Frequently asked questions

How do demolition contractors find teardown jobs from demolition permits?

Watch the new demolition permits in the counties you serve, every week. Read the address, the owner or applicant, and the structure type, then check the same address for a linked new-construction permit. Call the owner or builder named on the record while the teardown is still being priced, and ask to walk the site.

What is the best kind of demolition permit lead?

A residential teardown tied to a new build on the same lot. The owner or builder has a project and a schedule, the lot has to be cleared before anything else can start, and the same builder is likely to file more permits on other lots if the first job goes well.

When should I call after I see a demolition permit?

In the first week, if you can. Permits are commonly filed 2 to 6 weeks before ground breaks, per Shovels, so the first days after a filing are when the work is most likely to still be open. A later call can still work, but you are more likely to hear that the job is already placed.

Can I price a teardown from the permit record alone?

No. The record gives you the address, the parties, and the type of work, but it does not show access, foundation type, utility runs, or whether a survey for asbestos is still needed. Use the permit to earn a site walk, then price the full job — teardown, haul-off, disposal, and a clean lot — from what you see there.

Do I need anything besides the demolition permit before work starts?

In most places, yes. Utility services have to be cut or capped, and many areas require an asbestos survey and notice to an air agency before a building comes down. The exact steps sit with your city or county and the utilities. Ask about them on the first call so the schedule you promise includes them.

Whether a paid list beats a free county report for your crew depends on your counties and your volume. Start with a steady weekly check of the records in your own service area, and pay only if you can name what the paid list adds.

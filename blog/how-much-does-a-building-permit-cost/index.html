How much does a building permit cost?

By PermitsByCounty Research Team · Published October 21, 2026 · Guides & Answers

QUICK ANSWER

A building permit costs $1,688 on average in the United States, and most homeowners pay between $525 and $3,114, according to Angi’s 2026 cost data, updated September 15, 2026. A simple permit can cost about $150, while a large or complex project can reach $8,500. Your exact building permit cost depends on where you live, how big the job is, and how many separate trade permits the job needs.

The short version

  • The national average building permit cost is $1,688. Most people pay $525 to $3,114.
  • Small, simple permits can cost about $150. Big, complex projects can reach $8,500.
  • There is no single national price. Each city and county sets its own fee schedule.
  • Fees are usually based on the value of the work, the size of the project, or a flat fee for that kind of job.
  • Plan review, re-inspection, and penalty fees can sit on top of the permit fee itself.

What is the average building permit cost in the United States?

The average is $1,688, and the normal range is wide. Angi’s 2026 guide, based on verified project data, puts most homeowners between $525 and $3,114. That range is wide because a “building permit” is not one product. It covers very different work, from a small plumbing job to a full new house.

A simple permit, for example a basic plumbing or electrical job, can run as low as about $150. A large project that needs several permits at once, like new construction, can reach $8,500. Most home projects land in the middle: a remodel, an addition, or a roof that needs one main permit and one or two trade permits.

Use the national average only as a starting point. It tells you the right order of size — hundreds for small work, low thousands for most home projects. It does not tell you what your city or county will charge. For that, you need your local fee schedule, and this post shows real local numbers below.

Why does the same job cost different amounts in different places?

Because each city and county writes its own prices. There is no federal price list for building permits. Each permit office pays for its own plan reviewers, inspectors, and systems, and covers those costs in its own way.

Angi’s city table shows the gap. For the same kind of project, the typical range is $410 to $2,600 in Dallas and in Houston, $430 to $2,700 in Chicago, and $500 to $3,180 in Phoenix. Same country, same kind of work, different price — because the fee schedule is local.

There is a second trap. The address decides which office you pay. A house inside a city pays the city schedule. A house just outside the city line, in the unincorporated part of the same county, pays the county schedule. Two neighbours on the same street can pay different fees for the same roof. Always check the schedule for the office that covers your exact address.

How are building permit fees calculated?

Most places use one of three methods, and many use a mix of all three.

First, a flat fee. The schedule names the job and names one price. Flat fees are common for clear, repeat jobs like roofing, demolition, and window replacement, because the office does the same review every time.

Second, a fee based on the value of the work. You state what the job will cost, in labour and materials. The office charges a rate on that value — for example a set amount for each $1,000 of value — often with a minimum fee. Bigger work pays more, because it needs more review and more inspections.

Third, a fee based on size, charged by the square foot. Hillsborough County, Florida, for example, charges $1.00 per square foot for new residential construction, additions, and alterations, split evenly between plan review and inspection.

Whichever method your office uses, the permit fee is rarely the only line. Trade work — electrical, plumbing, and mechanical — often needs its own permit and its own fee, even inside one bigger job. That is how one kitchen remodel becomes three or four fees that add up.

How much does a building permit cost in real counties?

National averages hide the real answer, so here are two published local schedules you can check yourself.

In Hillsborough County, Florida, common residential fees are flat and public. A general residential building permit is $130. Roofing is $250. Demolition is $190. An electrical, mechanical, or plumbing permit is $130 each. A pool or spa is $475, and a shed, carport, deck, or screen room is $380. New construction and additions are charged by size, at $1.00 per square foot, and the schedule includes a $50 application fee inside the plan review part. A re-inspection costs $125 per visit.

In Austin, Texas, the city charges by size bands instead. For a single-family home of 1,001 to 2,000 square feet, the city’s schedule lists base fees of $289.53 for building, $166.99 for electrical, $116.07 for mechanical, $200.43 for plumbing, and $65.80 for energy, plus a small amount for each square foot above 1,000. An estimator that runs the city’s own tables puts a 2,000-square-foot new home at about $2,345.83 total, and a 400-square-foot addition at about $1,842.04. The lesson: the trades are not free extras. Read those two schedules side by side and the lesson is plain. Hillsborough prices many home jobs as one flat fee. Austin prices one home as several trade fees that stack. Neither is wrong — they are different systems. That is why no honest answer to this question can give you one number without asking where you live and what you are building.

What extra costs sit on top of the permit fee?

The permit fee is the headline. It is not always the whole bill.

Plan review. Many offices split the fee into a plan review part and an inspection part — Hillsborough splits it 50% and 50%. If your plans need changes and a second review, some offices charge again for the extra round.

Re-inspection fees. If work fails an inspection, the inspector comes back, and the return visit can cost you. In Hillsborough County that fee is $125 per visit.

Work started without a permit. This is the most expensive mistake on this page. In Hillsborough County it adds a penalty of 100% of the permit fee — you pay the fee twice, and you still need the permit and the inspections. Starting early to save time almost never saves money.

An expeditor, if you hire one. Some owners pay a permit runner to file papers and chase the office. Angi puts that extra cost at $200 to $1,000. It is optional — your contractor will often file the permit as part of the job.

Who pays for the permit — you or your contractor?

In most jobs, the contractor pulls the permit and its cost is inside the price. A contractor who will not touch permits is a warning sign. But read your contract: the duty to have a permit can still fall on you, the owner.

If you do the work yourself, you apply and you pay. Either way, ask to see the issued permit before work starts, and keep the permit number. It proves the work was permitted and inspected, which matters when you sell. How can you get your exact price before you apply?

Step 1: Name the full scope. Write down every part of the work — building, electrical, plumbing, mechanical, roof, demo. Each part can be its own permit and fee.

Step 2: Find the right office for your address. City address, city office. Unincorporated county address, county office. Our guide to looking up building permits by address for free walks through finding it.

Step 3: Read that office’s fee schedule. Look for your job by name first (flat fee). If it is not named, use the value-based or size-based table with your project’s value or square feet.

Step 4: Add the extras. Add plan review if it is separate, each trade permit, and a small cushion for one re-inspection.

Step 5: Confirm the total with the office before you file. A short call or email with your address and scope usually gets you the exact lines. Fees change — Austin refreshes its fees each October 1 — so last year’s total can be out of date.

Common mistakes that raise the cost

  • Starting work before the permit is issued. The penalty can double your fee, and the office can order work to stop until the permit is in hand.
  • Under-stating the value of the work. On a value-based schedule, a low value looks cheaper at first. If the office finds the real value, you pay the difference anyway.
  • Forgetting the trade permits. Pricing only the main permit, then finding electrical, plumbing, and mechanical are each a separate fee — five priced lines for one Austin house.
  • Changing the plan after the permit is issued. A bigger addition or added square feet can need a revision, a new review, and a new fee. Finish the design first, then file.
  • Letting the permit expire. Work must start, and inspections must keep happening, inside a set window. An expired permit can mean a renewal fee or a fresh application. Ask about the window when you pick the permit up.

Frequently asked questions

How much does a building permit cost for a house?

For a new house, expect low thousands in most places. Austin’s own fee tables work out to about $2,345.83 for a 2,000-square-foot new home, once building, electrical, mechanical, plumbing, and energy fees are added together. Angi puts most homeowners at $525 to $3,114 across all project types, with complex work reaching $8,500.

How much does a building permit cost for a remodel?

It depends on scope. A small flat-fee job in Hillsborough County can be $130 for a general residential permit, plus $130 for each trade permit the remodel needs. A large remodel that touches structure and several trades moves toward the top of Angi’s $525 to $3,114 typical range.

Do I pay for a building permit all at once?

Often, no. Many offices split the charge: part due when you submit plans, for plan review, and the rest when the permit is issued. Hillsborough County splits its fee evenly — 50% plan review, 50% inspection. Ask what is due at each step.

How much is a permit for a roof, a pool, or a deck?

In Hillsborough County, Florida, the published flat fees are $250 for residential roofing, $475 for a pool or spa, and $380 for a shed, carport, deck, or screen room. Inside a city in the same county, the city schedule applies instead. Check your own office’s schedule for your exact number.

What happens if I do not get a permit to save the fee?

You risk paying far more than you saved. Offices can add a penalty — 100% of the fee in Hillsborough County, so you pay double — order work to stop, and require inspections after the fact. Unpermitted work can also cause trouble when you sell, because a buyer can check the house’s permit history before they buy.

Can my contractor include the permit cost in the price?

Yes, and most do. Ask for the permit cost as its own line in writing, and ask to see the issued permit before work starts. That one habit protects you if there is ever a question about whether the work was permitted.

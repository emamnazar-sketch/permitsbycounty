Can you sell a house with unpermitted work?

By PermitsByCounty Research Team · Published October 24, 2026 · Guides & Answers

QUICK ANSWER

Yes, you can sell a house with unpermitted work. There is no law that says a home with unpermitted work cannot be sold, according to Redfin’s guide checked October 24, 2026. You must tell buyers in writing about work you know was done without a permit, even if a past owner did it. The sale gets harder, not impossible: an appraiser may not count that space, a lender may pause the loan, and most sellers choose one of three paths — retroactive permit, sell as-is with a lower price, or sell to a cash buyer.

TL;DR — the whole post in 20 seconds

  • Yes, you can sell. Disclosure is the part you cannot skip.
  • Unpermitted space often does not count in the appraisal, so the loan can come up short.
  • Work does not become legal because it is old. It was never permitted, so it is not grandfathered.
  • Retroactive permits usually cost 2 to 3 times the normal fee, plus fixes and plans.
  • Your three paths are: permit it first, price it in and sell as-is, or sell to a cash buyer.

What counts as unpermitted work?

Unpermitted work is work that needed a permit but was done without one. It also includes work where a permit was opened but final inspection never passed.

Work that almost always needs a permit includes a room addition, a garage or basement turned into living space, a new bathroom, a deck high off the ground, a new electrical panel, new plumbing, a new heating and cooling system, and taking out a load-bearing wall, per iBuyer’s 2026 guide, checked October 24, 2026. Painting, floors, cabinets that do not move plumbing, and like-for-like swaps usually do not. The exact line is local — your building department gives the final answer for your address.

Do you have to disclose unpermitted work when you sell?

Yes, in most states, if you know about it.

Once you know work was done without a permit, list it on your seller disclosure form — where it is and what it is, for example a finished basement or a panel upgrade. If you are not sure a permit was pulled, write that permit status is unknown. Do not leave it blank.

The duty follows what you know, not who did the work. LegalClarity puts it plainly: once you know about unpermitted construction, you are legally required to disclose it through your state’s disclosure statement — even if a past owner did it.

Hiding it is the risky path. Redfin warns sellers are legally obligated to tell buyers, and trying to hide it is not an option. If a buyer finds it after closing, they can sue for the cost to permit and fix it, and in serious cases ask a court to undo the sale. Full disclosure turns a surprise into a priced fact both sides agreed to. An as-is sale does not remove this duty: as-is means the buyer accepts the condition you described, not that you can describe less than you know.

How does unpermitted work hurt a sale?

It hurts in three places at once: the appraisal, the buyer’s loan, and insurance.

1. The appraisal may not count the space

An appraiser compares the house with the permit record and the tax record. If a room is not in those records, the appraiser must note it and, under Fannie Mae rules cited by iBuyer and LegalClarity, comment on its quality and effect on value. In many cases that space is left out of the official living area.

500 sq ft unpermitted addition × $150 per sq ft = $75,000 excluded from appraised value

If the appraisal is lower than the price, the loan is sized to the lower number. The buyer brings more cash, you cut the price, or the deal fails.

2. The buyer’s loan can pause or fail

Lenders lend against a house they can resell. Work that was never inspected raises a safety question they cannot ignore. Conventional loans leave more room: the appraiser notes the work, excludes that space, and the lender can still approve if there is no safety concern. FHA and VA loans are stricter — iBuyer notes they can be blocked when unpermitted work touches electrical, plumbing, structural, or heating and cooling systems, because those loans require health and safety compliance.

Open permits create a second closing risk, even when the work itself was permitted. A title agency guide warns that a title search that uncovers an open permit can temporarily halt the closing, that lenders may delay or deny financing, and that closing costs can rise.

An open permit found in a title search can halt a closing; lenders may delay or deny financing

Cash buyers avoid the lender step — there is no lender or appraisal condition to clear. You still must disclose to a cash buyer.

3. Insurance can say no

If a fire starts in wiring that was never inspected, or water damage comes from plumbing that was never permitted, an insurer can deny that claim. LegalClarity explains insurers can deny a claim from work that was never inspected, and can cancel or not renew a policy when they find major unpermitted changes. The City of Austin says the same: some insurers will not cover claims for work done without permits.

Unpermitted work can affect insurance coverage and the ability to refinance, obtain a loan, or sell

A buyer who cannot get insurance usually cannot close a financed sale, because lenders require proof of insurance. Put the three risks together and the buyer pool shrinks: FHA and VA buyers may be out, some conventional buyers will walk, and those who stay will price the risk.

Is unpermitted work grandfathered in if it is old?

No.

Grandfathering protects work that was legal when built and later fell out of line when code changed. Unpermitted work was never legal. LegalClarity is clear: it never had legal status, so there is no status to protect, no matter how old it is. Many places treat each day a violation exists as a new violation, so a city can seek a fix years later. Do not write “grandfathered” on a disclosure form for work that was never permitted.

How do you check if your house has unpermitted work?

Do this before you list. It is far cheaper than finding out from a buyer’s appraiser.

Step 1: Pull your permit history. Search your city or county building department for permits at your address, often online for free. Our earlier guide on how to check a house’s permit history before you buy it walks through the same check.

Step 2: Compare the record to the house. List every addition, conversion, and system change. Mark any with no permit, or a permit with no final inspection.

Step 3: Compare the tax record to real size. If the tax record shows far less living space than the house measures, that gap is a strong sign of unpermitted space. iBuyer flags a gap of 200 square feet or more as worth checking.

Step 4: Get a licensed contractor to look. Ask one question: could this work pass inspection today? Their written answer tells you which path below is realistic.

What are your three options?

Option 1: Get a retroactive permit, then sell

A retroactive permit — also called an after-the-fact or as-built permit — is issued after work is already done. You submit plans showing the work as built, and an inspector checks it against current code. If it passes, the work joins the legal record. If not, you fix what the inspector names and it is inspected again. Inspectors may need to see hidden work, which can mean opening small parts of walls, ceilings, or floors, per LegalClarity. The cost is higher than permitting before you start.

Penalty of 2 to 3 times the normal permit fee, plus separate fines; plans and correction work are extra

Retroactive permit cost — United States · 2026 guide, checked October 24, 2026

Source: LegalClarity — What Happens If You Don’t Pull Permits · Date pulled: October 24, 2026

iBuyer adds: filing fees alone are often $50 to $500, and structural work that needs walls opened can reach $5,000 to $50,000 or more in corrections, over 2 weeks to 18 months. Treat that as a vendor range, not a quote. Choose this path when the work is sound, you have time, and the recovered appraisal value is worth it.

Option 2: Sell as-is, disclose fully, and price it in

List the house as it is. Name the unpermitted areas in the disclosure and the listing remarks, so no buyer is surprised after an inspection. iBuyer puts a common adjustment at 10 to 20 percent off the affected value — not off the whole house, off the unpermitted part.

Choose this path when the work is minor, fixing it would cost more than it returns, or you need a normal timeline — iBuyer puts an as-is sale at about 30 to 90 days. Expect a smaller pool, mostly conventional and cash buyers.

Option 3: Sell to a cash buyer

A cash buyer has no lender, no appraisal condition, and no financing deadline to fail. They price the disclosed work into their offer and can close in 7 to 30 days, per iBuyer. Choose this when speed matters most, the work is large, or a financed deal has already failed. You trade price for speed and certainty. Disclosure is still required.

If work cannot be permitted at all — it breaks a setback, zoning, or safety rule that cannot be fixed in place — a city can order it removed at your cost. That is why a contractor who knows local code comes before you pick a path.

What should you do before you list?

In order:

  1. Pull the permit history for your address and save a copy.
  2. Walk the house and list every change with no matching closed permit.
  3. Get a licensed contractor’s written view on whether it can pass today.
  4. Ask your building department, in writing if you can, what a retroactive permit for that work would need.
  5. Pick one path — permit it, price it, or cash — before the first showing.
  6. Fill out the disclosure form completely. Write “permit status unknown” where you truly do not know.
  7. Price with your agent using permitted space only, unless the work is permitted by closing day.

Frequently asked questions

Can you sell a house with unpermitted work?

Yes. Redfin confirms no law bars the sale. You must disclose known work in writing, and it can lower the appraisal, narrow the buyer pool, and make some loans harder.

Do I have to disclose unpermitted work if a previous owner did it?

Yes, if you know about it. The duty follows your knowledge, not who did the work. If a permit check shows a past owner did work without a permit, list it.

Will a buyer be able to get a mortgage on a house with unpermitted work?

Sometimes. A conventional loan can often proceed if the appraiser notes the work, excludes that space, and flags no safety issue. FHA and VA loans are stricter and can be blocked when work touches electrical, plumbing, structural, or heating and cooling systems. Cash buyers have no lender step.

How much does it cost to get a retroactive permit?

Usually 2 to 3 times the normal fee, plus fines, per LegalClarity. Plans and corrections are the larger cost: structural work that needs walls opened can reach $5,000 to $50,000 or more in iBuyer’s range. Get a local quote first.

Does unpermitted work ever become legal with age?

No. Work that was legal when built can be grandfathered. Work that was never permitted was never legal, so age does not change its status.

Should I just lower the price and not mention the work?

No. A lower price is not a substitute for disclosure. Hiding known work can lead to a lawsuit after closing, and in serious cases a court can undo the sale.

How do I find electrical jobs from building permits?

By PermitsByCounty Research Team · Published November 20, 2026 · Guides & Answers

QUICK ANSWER

You find electrical leads from building permits by reading new building permits in your area each week and calling the builder or general contractor named on the filing before the electrical scope is bought out. Electrical work is often hired late, after the GC is already picked, so a fresh permit usually names a real, funded job whose electrician may not be chosen yet. The strongest signal is a building permit with no electrical permit on file at the same address yet. That gap is the opening paid services like BuildLink ATX screen for, and you can screen for it yourself in free county reports and city permit records.

Why electrical leads from building permits work so well

Most trades get picked early. The electrician often does not. Platineer, in its guide on how to find electrical jobs to bid on, puts it plainly: electrical is typically bought out well after the GC is appointed. A project can sit in public permit records, fully visible, with its electrical scope still open.

That timing gap is the whole play. A permit filing is not an ad for bids. It is a public record of a job that is funded and moving. It names the address, the scope, the declared value, the owner, and the applicant. You are not answering a posting that fifty other shops also saw. You are calling while the bid list is still being written.

There is a second reason this fits electrical work. In many cities, electrical work gets its own permit, pulled by the licensed electrician doing the job. TarSignal explains the pattern in its help pages: each permit names the contractor responsible for that permit’s work, and electrical, plumbing, and mechanical permits usually name the trade contractor, not the builder. So you can read the record both ways. A building permit with no matching electrical permit points to open work. An electrical permit that does exist tells you which shop won it, which is useful market knowledge on its own.

Step 1: pick the permits worth your time

Start with new building permits, not electrical permits. By the time an electrical permit is filed, the job is usually taken. Your list is the building permits filed in the last one to two weeks in the counties and cities where your crews actually work.

Sort that list with a short set of filters. Keep jobs with clear electrical scope: new homes, additions, tenant build-outs, restaurants, medical and office space, warehouses, and any project that lists a service upgrade, a new panel, or EV charging. Keep job sizes your shop can staff. A two-person service shop and a forty-person commercial crew should not chase the same list.

Drop the noise. Tiny repairs, sign work with no building scope, and permits where the owner is doing the work alone rarely turn into sub work. Drop addresses outside your service area, even if the job looks good. A lead you cannot staff is not a lead.

Pay close attention to one check that paid services charge for. Its own wording is careful, and you should be too: no trade permit on file does not prove the GC is still hiring. Public records can lag. But as a sorting rule, it puts the most likely open jobs at the top of your call list.

Step 2: read each permit like an estimator

A permit record gives you more than an address. Read it the way you would read a small set of plans.

The scope line tells you the kind of electrical work. New construction means service, rough-in, and trim. A tenant build-out means lighting, panels, and often a service change. A stated job value helps you guess the electrical share and whether the job fits your crew. The applicant and contractor fields tell you who to call. On a building permit, that is usually the GC or the builder. On a home, it may be the owner, and for repeat work the builder is still the better call.

Platineer’s guide makes the same point about residential work: the buyer worth chasing is the builder or remodeler, not the homeowner. One builder who likes your work sends you job after job at known specs. One homeowner sends you one job. Permit filings show you which builders file again and again in your area, and that repeat count tells you who is really building, not just who advertises the most.

Note the date, too. A permit filed this week is a job at the start of its schedule. A permit filed three months ago may already have its walls closed. Fresh filings get your first calls.

Step 3: make the call the right way

Call the GC or builder named on the filing, and ask for the estimator or the person who handles preconstruction. Those are the people who write the bid list. Platineer’s guide gives the same advice for getting onto invitation lists: find the estimator or preconstruction manager by name, because invitations come from people, not from companies.

Keep the call short and specific. Name the job you saw. Say what your shop does, name your license, and name two or three jobs like it that you have finished. Then ask two plain questions: who is handling electrical on this one, and how do I get included when it goes out?

If the scope is already awarded, do not treat the call as wasted. Ask to be added to the list for the next one, and send your details the same day: license, insurance, bonding if you carry it, and a few similar jobs with names the GC can check. Shops that answer fast and send clean paperwork stay on lists. Shops that go quiet fall off them.

Expect some dead ends. Some GCs hire the same electrician on every job. Some permits stall and never start. That is fine. You are building a list of active builders in your market, and that list pays you back for years.

A weekly routine you can keep

Platineer’s guide ends with a weekly routine, and it is a good one to copy. Here is a plain version that fits a small shop.

On Monday, pull last week’s new building permits for your counties. Filter them to your job types and sizes. Check each address for an electrical permit on file. The ones with none go to the top.

On Tuesday, pick the best three or four and call the GC or builder on each one. Keep notes: who you spoke to, what they said, and when to follow up. A notebook works. A spreadsheet works. What matters is that the notes exist next week.

During the week, send your license, insurance, and short job list to every GC who asked for it, the same day they asked. Speed reads as reliability, and reliability is what keeps a sub on a bid list.

Once a month, look at the electrical permits that were filed in your area and note which shops pulled them. You will start to see who your real competition is, which builders they work for, and where your shop is missing. That picture is hard to buy and easy to build from public records.

Common mistakes that waste permit leads

The first mistake is reading only electrical permits. Those records show work that is already awarded. They are good for market knowledge and poor for new work. Your new work sits in building permits that have no electrical permit yet.

The second mistake is calling the homeowner on a new-home permit. The builder hires the electrician. Call the builder.

The third mistake is chasing every permit in the county. A tight list of ten jobs that fit your crew beats a printout of two hundred that fit nobody. Estimators quit routines that take all morning.

The fourth mistake is one call and done. Bid lists are built over months. A GC who has no room for you on this job may have three jobs next quarter. Stay polite, stay on the list, and check the new permits each week so your next call names their next job.

The fifth mistake is treating a missing electrical permit as proof the job is open. Records lag, and some cities fold trade work into the building permit. TarSignal notes that some offices do exactly that, so the record shows only the builder. Always confirm with the GC before you count a job as open.

Frequently asked questions

How do I find electrical jobs from building permits for free?

Read the new building permits in your county each week, sort for jobs with real electrical scope, and check whether an electrical permit is already on file at the address. Call the GC or builder named on the building permit and ask who handles electrical. County reports and city permit portals are free; paid tools only save you the digging time.

What is the best permit signal for an electrical contractor?

A fresh building permit with no electrical permit on file at the same address. Electrical scope is often bought out after the GC is picked, so that gap often means the trade slot is still open. It is a signal, not proof — records can lag — so confirm it with the GC on your call.

Should I call the homeowner or the builder on a new-home permit?

Call the builder. The builder or GC hires the electrician, sets the specs, and sends repeat work. A homeowner gives you one job. A builder who trusts your work can fill a crew’s schedule for a season.

How much do paid electrical permit lead services cost?

Prices checked on November 20, 2026: Electrician Scout charges $150 to $975 a month for commercial electrical leads from public records. BuildLink ATX charges $299 a month for screened Austin permit alerts. PermitVector charges $19.99 a month for one county, plus $9.99 for each added county.

What do I say when I call a GC from a permit filing?

Name the job you saw on the permit, say what your shop does, give your license, and name two or three similar jobs you have finished. Then ask who is handling electrical and how you get included when the scope goes out. If it is already awarded, ask to be added to the list for the next job and send your paperwork the same day.

Do electrical permits themselves make good leads?

Not for new work, most of the time. An electrical permit usually means an electrician is already on the job. Read those records to learn which shops work for which builders and where your competition is busy. Read building permits to find work that is still open.

How can suppliers quote a job before the material order is placed?

By PermitsByCounty Research Team · Published November 13, 2026 · Guides & Answers

QUICK ANSWER

You quote a job before the material order is placed by reading the job one stage at a time, not all at once. A build does not buy everything on the day the first permit issues. Each stage leaves its own permit — site work, then the building permit, then separate electrical, plumbing, and mechanical permits as the work gets close — and each permit is a fresh chance to quote the order that comes next. Shovels, checked November 11, 2026, says building permits are filed 2 to 6 weeks before ground breaks. The SalesJack dealer guide, checked the same day, puts the test plainly: the job you want is the one where the contractor has not committed to a supplier yet. Quote the stage that has not been permitted yet, and you are early. Quote the whole job the week the first permit lands, and half your quote covers orders that will not be placed for months — by which time it is stale, or someone else has it.

TL;DR — the whole post in 20 seconds

  • One job is a string of orders, not one order. Each build stage buys its own materials, in a set order.
  • Each stage also leaves a permit. The permit for the next stage is your signal to quote that stage’s order.
  • Quote early for your stage, not early for the whole job. A framing quote belongs with the building permit. An HVAC quote belongs with the mechanical permit.
  • Speed still matters. A permit you sit on for three weeks can lose the window the permit gave you.
  • The declared value on a permit is a size signal, not a material budget. Size your quote from the work type, not that number alone.

Why is one job really a string of separate orders?

Because nobody orders finish materials the week the lot is cleared. The money moves in the order the work moves. Site work buys pipe, stone, and concrete first. Framing buys lumber and trusses. Roofing buys shingles or metal once the frame is up. The mechanical, electrical, and plumbing trades buy rough-in material after framing, and the finish orders — cabinets, fixtures, flooring, paint — come near the end.

Construction Monitor makes the same point from the supplier side. Suppliers often come into a job during the middle and later stages, after the foundation and framing are done, when materials for interiors and mechanical systems are delivered.

That order is good news. A job you missed at the framing stage is not a lost job. Its roofing, mechanical, and finish orders may still be open. Suppliers who treat a permit as one all-or-nothing moment give up after the first order goes elsewhere. Suppliers who treat it as a string of orders keep a live chance on the same address for months.

How do suppliers quote a job stage by stage, before the material order is placed?

You match each quote to the permit that comes just before that material is bought. The routine has five steps.

Step one: start with the building permit, and place the job on a stage list. Write down the address, the type of work, the builder’s name, and the approval date. A permit record usually carries those fields, and sometimes a project value. That one line opens a file on the job. Our guide to how suppliers find job sites before ground breaks covers building that starting list each week.

Step two: sort the job into the stages your products belong to. If you sell concrete and framing lumber, your stages are early and your clock is short. If you sell HVAC equipment, plumbing fixtures, roofing, or finish goods, your stages come later and your clock is longer — but only if the job sits on a list somebody checks. A later stage is not a reason to wait and hope. It is a date to set.

Step three: watch the same address for the next permit. This is the heart of the method. A house does not run on one permit. Separate permits for electrical, plumbing, and mechanical work are filed as construction moves along, not all at the start. Each new filing at an address you track tells you the job has reached the stage right before yours — or yours.

Step four: quote when your stage’s permit appears, and quote that stage only. Name the job, name the stage, and price the materials that stage needs, with a delivery window that fits the build. A tight quote for one stage beats a thick quote for the whole house. The builder can say yes to a stage today. A whole-job quote asks him to commit to prices for work months away, and most builders will not do that — so the quote sits, and a sharper rival quote for the next stage walks past it.

Step five: if you lose a stage, stay on the address. Losing the framing package does not lose you the roof, and losing the roof does not lose you the finish orders. Cross off the stage that went elsewhere, keep the stages still to come, and be the first quote in when the next permit posts. Builders remember who quoted cleanly and on time — the second order often goes to the supplier who lost the first one well.

What does the stage clock actually look like?

The stages run in a known order, and each takes weeks, not days. A step-by-step build guide puts numbers on the early stages of a house.

Site prep and foundation together usually take 2 to 4 weeks; framing typically takes 2 to 4 weeks; rough-in for electrical, plumbing, and HVAC typically takes 2 to 4 weeks; a house takes 6 to 12 months to build once construction starts

Read those numbers as quote windows. If a building permit issues this week and your product is framing lumber, your window is a few weeks: site work first, then framing. If your product goes in at rough-in, the same permit gives you a longer runway, and the mechanical or electrical permit at that address is the signal that the runway is ending and it is time to send the quote.

Big jobs stretch the pattern over a longer road. TarSignal’s reading of one Charlotte data-center campus shows it: 142 days passed between the first site permit and the shell permit, and the first six data halls were permitted 38 to 41 days after the shell.

The lesson holds at house scale too: permits arrive in stage order, months apart on a big job and weeks apart on a small one, and each arrival is a separate buying moment with a separate winner.

Why does quoting the whole job at once usually fail?

Three reasons, and all three are timing reasons.

First, the early quote goes stale. Prices move, stock moves, and plans change as the job goes through inspections. A finish-materials price sent the week the foundation was poured will be re-quoted by somebody — and not always by you.

Second, the builder is not ready to hear it. The week the building permit issues, his head is on site work and framing. A quote for fixtures lands on a desk with no use for it yet, and early paper gets filed under the newer pile.

Third, a rival only has to beat you once, at the right moment. The SalesJack guide describes the ideal project as one where the contractor has not committed to a supplier yet, and warns what happens once a commitment lands: once framing lumber has been ordered from someone else, you are fighting for scraps on that stage.

The ideal project is one where the contractor has not committed to a supplier yet and has not started buying; once framing lumber has been ordered from someone else, a rival supplier is fighting for scraps on that stage

Quote stage by stage and all three problems shrink. Your price is fresh, since you sent it when the stage came up, and the builder is ready for it — that stage is his next problem. A rival cannot just beat you once, either. He has to beat you at every stage.

How fast do you have to move on a new permit?

Fast enough that your own process does not eat the lead the permit gave you. Shovels gives the outer edge of that lead: permits are filed 2 to 6 weeks before ground breaks, and months before a project shows up in industry reports.

For early-stage products, the quote goes out the same week the building permit posts. For later-stage products, speed means the job goes on your stage list the same week, with a check-back tied to the next permit. A finish supplier rarely loses on a slow quote. He loses on a job noted in month one, never tracked, and found again in month five with the finishes delivered.

One honest limit sits alongside the speed rule. Permit data is a slightly trailing signal. A fast builder may line up key suppliers before the permit posts, and the earliest orders on some jobs are gone before you see the record. You will not win those. Stage quoting keeps you in front of the orders still open — which, on most jobs, is most of them.

What should the quote itself carry?

Keep it short and tied to the stage. The builder should see in under a minute that you know his job and are pricing the part he is about to buy.

Name the job by its address and the stage you are pricing. Builders run several jobs at once, and a quote with no address forces the builder to guess which job you mean. List the materials for that stage, with quantities as far as the permit and the work type let you judge them honestly. Put a delivery window on it that matches the stage clock — the weeks the stage will actually run, not “whenever you are ready.” And say when the price holds until. A clear hold date gives the builder a reason to answer now.

What it should not carry: a full-house price list he did not ask for, or a material total built off the permit’s declared value. That value is the applicant’s figure for the building work, and TarSignal warns readers to treat it as a size signal, not a budget. Use it to judge whether the job is big or small, not to guess what the builder will spend on your line.

One more thing belongs in the file: who buys your stage. On some jobs the general contractor buys the material. On others the trade contractor does — the roofer buys the roofing, the HVAC firm buys the equipment. The trade permit at the address often names that firm, and our guide to which contractors are busiest in your territory shows how to rank those names. Quote the buyer, not just the job.

Frequently asked questions

How can suppliers quote a job before the material order is placed?

Read the job stage by stage. Open a file when the building permit issues, sort the job into the stages your products belong to, and watch the same address for the next permit. When the permit for the stage just before yours — or for your stage itself — appears, send a quote for that stage only, with the address on it and a delivery window that fits the build.

When is it too late to quote a stage?

Once the contractor has committed to a supplier for that stage and started buying, a new quote is fighting for scraps, as the SalesJack guide puts it. If the stage’s material is already on site, that order is placed. The stages after it are still open, so move your quote to the next stage instead of pushing on a closed one.

Should I quote the whole job when the first permit issues?

No. Quote the stage that is coming up, and note the later stages with their check-back points. A whole-job quote goes stale before the later stages are bought, and asks for commitments builders will not make months out. A stage quote can be accepted the week you send it.

How do I know which stage a job has reached?

Watch the permits at the address. Separate electrical, plumbing, and mechanical permits are filed as construction moves along, so a new trade filing means rough-in territory. No new filing for a while usually means a long middle or finish stage — or a stalled job, which a drive past the address will tell you.

Who should get my quote — the builder or the trade contractor?

Whoever buys your material on that job. The general contractor often buys the main packages, while trade contractors buy their own stage material — the HVAC firm its equipment, the roofer its roofing. The trade permit often names that firm. If you are not sure, ask on the first call, and write the answer in the job file.

Can I size a quote from the value on the permit?

Only roughly. The declared value is the applicant’s figure for the building work as a whole, and some offices work it out from square footage. It tells you if a job is large or small, not what any one stage or material line will cost. Size the quote from the work type, the size of the building, and what the buyer says he needs for that stage.

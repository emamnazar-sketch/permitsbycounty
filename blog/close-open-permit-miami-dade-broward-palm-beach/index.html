How do I close an open permit in Miami-Dade, Broward or Palm Beach County?

By PermitsByCounty Research Team · Published October 22, 2026 · Guides & Answers

QUICK ANSWER

To close an open permit in Miami-Dade, Broward or Palm Beach County, find it in the department that issued it, then finish what is missing — usually a final inspection — with the original contractor, a new licensed contractor, or yourself as owner-builder, which Florida Statute 553.79(15) allows. If the permit expired but the work was substantially completed, the department can close it without a new permit, and it may close a permit 6 years after issuance, even without a final inspection, if there is no apparent safety hazard. Start early: open permits often surface only 10 to 14 days before a closing (South Florida Agent Magazine, August 10, 2026).

The short version

  • A permit is open until its final inspection passes — finished work can still be open if nobody called for that inspection.
  • Florida law gives four closing routes: the original contractor, a new contractor, you as owner-builder, or the 6-year agency closure if there is no apparent safety hazard.
  • The three counties share no system: only the office that issued the permit can close it.
  • Fees are modest next to a delayed closing: Broward’s minimum expired-permit fee is $125; Palm Beach County’s certified search is $65.
  • Search at the offer stage, not the title stage.

What does “open,” “expired” and “closed” mean on a permit?

A permit opens when the department issues it, stays open while work is done and inspections pass, and closes only when the final inspection has passed and every condition is met. Florida law says it in legal words: “close” means the requirements of the permit have been satisfied.

An expired permit is an open permit that ran out of time. A permit becomes invalid if work does not start within about six months of issue, and each approved inspection extends it by 180 days. When that clock runs out with no final inspection, the permit does not go away — it sits on the property record, expired, and still has to be closed. That is why sellers get surprised years later: the roof was replaced and everyone was paid, but nobody called for the final inspection. The record follows the house, not the contractor.

Permit invalid if work has not commenced within 6 months of issuance; once started, validity extends 180 days after each approved inspection. For a single-family dwelling, the first window is now 1 year after issuance or the next Florida Building Code edition, whichever is later.

Why does this matter most at a closing?

Because that is when most people find out: a title search near the end of a sale is often the first real permit search a property ever gets. South Florida Agent Magazine reported in August 2026 that open permits surface “10 to 14 days” before closing through the title search, with records scattered across dozens of separate municipal portals. Ten days is a bad time to learn a 2019 permit needs an inspection, a fee, and a contractor nobody can find. Search early, at the offer or listing stage, as our guide on how to check a house’s permit history before you buy it shows.

Step 1: Find every open permit on the property

Start with the office that covers the exact address: inside a city, that city’s building department; in the unincorporated part of the county, the county. A county search will not show a city permit, and a city search will not show a county permit.

In Miami-Dade County, use the county permit search for unincorporated property, by address or folio — a folio starting with 30 means unincorporated Miami-Dade. In Broward County, the county Building Code Division handles unincorporated areas through ePermits, but most homes sit inside a city — Fort Lauderdale, Hollywood, Pompano Beach — with its own department and search. In Palm Beach County, search open permits free through ePZB for unincorporated property; open statuses include active, inactive, expired, in process and stop issue. Inside any city, search that city’s system too.

For a closing in Palm Beach County, the county also sells a certified open-permit search.

For every open permit you find, write down its number, issuing office, type of work, year, and the contractor named on it. Each permit closes separately, with its own office.

Step 2: Pick your closing route — Florida law gives you four

Florida Statute 553.79(15), repeated in the Florida Building Code, sets out how any property owner — named on the original application or not — may close a permit.

Route 1: the original contractor — usually fastest, since they are already the contractor of record and can schedule the missing inspection. Route 2: a different licensed contractor, who can do the work needed to satisfy the permit; the law says this contractor is not liable for defects in the original contractor’s work, only for the work he or she performs. Route 3: you, as owner-builder, under Florida Statutes 489.103(7) and 489.503(6) — a fit for small, finished jobs where what is missing is paperwork and an inspection, not construction. Route 4: the 6-year closure — a department may close a permit 6 years after issuance, even without a final inspection, if it decides no apparent safety hazard exists. Note the word “may”: it is the department’s choice, never automatic.

One more rule helps: if an expired permit’s requirements have been substantially completed, as decided by the local department, it may be closed without a new permit, and the closing work is judged under the code in effect when the original application was received — a 2018 job to the 2018 code, not today’s.

Step 3: Close it, county by county

The route is state law; the paperwork, fees and portal are local.

Miami-Dade County. Work with the county Department of Regulatory and Economic Resources (RER) for unincorporated addresses, or the city’s building department for city addresses. If no work was ever done, ask about cancelling the permit instead — on the request of the qualifier or the property owner, naming the permit number, job address and reason. If work was done, expect the missing inspections, and expect an old expired permit to be reactivated or reissued first. Once reopened, move quickly: each approved inspection restarts the 180-day clock.

Broward County. The county schedule prices expired permits directly: a $125 minimum fee; renewal within 180 days at 50% of the original fee; after 180 days, a new permit at full fees. Changing contractor costs $75, an extension $50, and re-inspections rise per visit — $66, $132, $198, then $264. Inside a city, the city sets its own fees and forms — Fort Lauderdale runs its own system (LauderBuild) — so confirm the schedule with the issuing office first.

Palm Beach County. For unincorporated addresses, the Planning, Zoning & Building Department (PZB) is your office and ePZB holds the record. An inactive permit can be reactivated for $75 within 180 days of inactive status, or 30% of the original fee after 180 days. Once active, schedule the missing inspections and close it the normal way. If you are closing for a sale, the certified search above ($65, or $97 rush) is the county’s own certified answer — the proof a title company wants.

Step 4: Pass the final inspection — and prove the permit is closed

For most open permits, the last step is an inspection of work finished long ago. An inspector can only approve what can be seen, so if the work is covered — behind drywall, under a roof covering — the department may ask for job photos, a letter from a licensed contractor or engineer, or opening a small section. This is normal, not a penalty, and it is why old permits take weeks, not days. When the inspection passes, get proof: print the record showing the permit closed, completed or finaled, and check it again a few days later. The closing counts only when the record changes. Keep that proof — it is what a buyer, lender or title company asks for at the next sale.

What if you just bought the house and the old owner’s permit is open?

Under Florida Statute 553.79(17), a department may not deny you a new permit, issue you a violation notice, or fine or charge you fees solely because a previous owner’s permit was never closed; it keeps its rights against the previous owner and the contractor named on that permit. That is what the statute says, not legal advice: contracts often assign open-permit duties between buyer and seller, so ask your title company or a real-estate attorney who pays.

Common mistakes that delay a closing

Waiting for the title search — by then you have 10 to 14 days and no slack. Searching only the county, or only the city — the wrong portal returns a clean “no records” result for a property with an open permit in the other system. Assuming an old permit closed itself — permits do not age out, and the 6-year route needs the department to act. Hiring a new contractor without the history in writing. Letting a reactivated permit expire again. Paying before the record changes — the changed record is the deliverable.

Frequently asked questions

How do I close an open permit in Miami-Dade County?

Find it in the county search (unincorporated addresses) or the city’s system (city addresses), then close it with the original contractor, a new licensed contractor, or as owner-builder under Florida Statute 553.79(15) — usually by reactivating it if expired, passing the missing final inspection, and checking the record shows closed.

Can I sell a house with an open permit in Florida?

People do, but it is harder and slower: title companies often will not give a clean title picture while a permit is open, and buyers can renegotiate or delay. Safer: close the permit before listing, or disclose it and price the fix into the deal.

How long does it take to close an open permit?

A simple permit with a willing contractor and one missing inspection can close in days to a few weeks. A certified Palm Beach County search alone takes 7 to 10 business days (3 as a $97 rush), and permits where the work cannot be seen may need letters or opening walls, which adds time.

How much does it cost to close an open permit in Broward County?

Under the county schedule: a $125 minimum expired-permit fee; renewal within 180 days at 50% of the original fee; after 180 days, a new permit at full fees. Add $75 to change contractor, and $66 to $264 in re-inspection fees if inspections repeat. City permits follow the city’s own schedule.

What happens after 6 years — does an open permit close itself in Florida?

No. After 6 years from issuance, the department may close the permit even without a final inspection, if it decides no apparent safety hazard exists. “May” is the key word: the department must review and act first.

I did no work at all under the permit. Do I still have to close it?

Ask the department about cancellation instead: where no work was performed, a permit can be cancelled on the request of the qualifier or the property owner, without a full close-out. Keep the cancellation proof.

How much does a permit expediter cost — and when is hiring one worth it?

By PermitsByCounty Research Team · Published October 29, 2026 · Guides & Answers

QUICK ANSWER

A permit expediter typically costs $500 to $7,500, depending on the project. Residential expediting averages $500 to $2,500, a single commercial site runs $1,500 to $5,000, and restaurant work runs $2,000 to $7,500, according to Permit Place’s 2026 price guide, checked October 23, 2026. City permit fees are separate. Hiring one is worth it when a delay costs you more than the fee — on a commercial job, Permit Place’s 2026 study puts delay carrying costs at about $500 a day, so a $2,500 fee breaks even if it saves just five days.

TL;DR — the whole post in 20 seconds

  • Typical expediter fees: $500–$2,500 for a home project, $1,500–$5,000 for one commercial site, $2,000–$7,500 for a restaurant (Permit Place, 2026).
  • The city’s own permit fees are never included — budget another $200 to $5,000 or more for those.
  • Waiting is the real cost: the average first review takes 22.9 days across 741 cities, and commercial delays run about $500 a day.
  • Worth it: a hard opening date, several departments reviewing at once, corrections you cannot answer, or a city you have never filed in.
  • Not worth it: a simple job with clean plans, no deadline, and a city that sells its own faster review for less.

What does a permit expediter actually do?

A permit expediter is a specialist you hire to run your building permit application for you. The work is plain, steady work: find out which permits the job needs, check the plans and forms for missing pieces, submit the package to the right departments, follow up with reviewers, answer correction notes with your designer, and resubmit until the permit is issued.

That sounds simple, and for a simple job it is. The value shows up in two places. First, local knowledge: every city has its own forms and order of steps, and an expediter who files there every week already knows them. Second, clean paperwork: the most common cause of a long wait is not the first review — it is the cycle of corrections after a sloppy first submission. Each round adds a new review, and a new wait.

An expediter is not your contractor and not your architect. They do not design or build the work — they move the paper. And one limit matters before you pay anyone: an expediter cannot override city rules or promise an approval. As one California firm puts it, they can streamline the process, but they cannot override the city’s requirements — their value is accurate documents and fewer revisions, not skipping steps.

How much does a permit expediter cost?

Most expediters charge a flat fee for a defined job, so you know the price before work starts. Permit Place, a national firm that says it has processed more than 30,000 permit projects since 2003, publishes these 2026 ranges:

Residential $500–$2,500 · Commercial single-site $1,500–$5,000 · Restaurant / food service $2,000–$7,500 · Multi-site equipment rollout $1,000–$3,500 per location · Shop-in-shop $1,500–$4,000 per location

How are expediter fees charged?

There are three common shapes. A flat fee covers a clear, defined scope — the usual choice for one home project or one store. An hourly rate covers open-ended work where nobody knows how many correction rounds are coming; JDJ puts California hourly rates at $40 to $300 or more per hour. A hybrid charges a base fee for the main submission, then hourly for corrections — common on ADUs and mid-size commercial jobs.

Whichever shape you choose, get the scope in writing: what is included, what a correction round costs, and what happens if the city asks for something new. Fee arguments in this trade almost always start with a scope that was never written down.

What else will you pay on top of the expediter’s fee?

The city’s fees. Always. An expediter’s invoice never includes the permit fees themselves — those are paid to the building department, and Permit Place puts municipal filing fees at $200 to $5,000 or more on top. Our earlier guide on how much a building permit costs walks through those city fees in full.

Some cities also sell speed directly, and that price belongs in your comparison. Philadelphia is the clearest example in a live PermitsByCounty metro: standard building permit review takes 15 business days for a one-or-two-family home and 20 business days for most other work, but the city offers an Accelerated Plan Review — reviewed within 5 business days — for a $2,000 fee, with $350 due when you apply. If your city’s own fast lane costs less than a private expediter and covers your job, take the city’s lane first.

Accelerated Plan Review fee $2,000 ($350 due at application); accelerated applications reviewed within 5 business days, against a standard 15–20 business days

Why is waiting so expensive?

Because the clock runs on money you are already spending. Permit Place’s 2026 State of Building Permits study, built from 741 cities in 44 states, found the average first review takes 22.9 days, with a median of 14 days — and that is only the first review, before corrections. The same study names Chicago the slowest major metro at 92 days for a first review and San Francisco at 60 days. Both are cities where PermitsByCounty tracks issued permits, and both are cities where a stalled application is normal, not bad luck.

For a commercial job, the study puts the carrying cost of a delay at about $500 a day — loan interest, rent on a space you cannot open, and crews that must be rebooked. Do the plain math on your own job: days you could realistically save, times what each day costs you. If that number is bigger than the expediter’s quote, the fee is the cheaper option.

For a home project the daily cost is softer but real: rent while you wait, a contractor whose schedule fills up, or a sale that cannot close. The math is smaller — which is why the decision rule matters more, not less, for homeowners.

When is hiring an expediter worth it?

Hiring one is usually worth it when at least one of these is true:

  • A date has money attached to it. A store opening, a lease that starts whether you are open or not, a loan draw schedule, a closing date. Delay has a price, and you can name it.
  • Several departments must review the same plans. Building, fire, health, planning, public works — each with its own comments. Restaurants and medical offices top the fee tables for this reason.
  • Your first submission came back with corrections you do not know how to answer. Paying for help on round two is cheaper than guessing through rounds three and four.
  • You are filing in a city you have never filed in. The expediter’s local knowledge is the product. Multi-site rollouts hire one firm for this reason: Permit Place prices repeat locations lower per site ($1,000–$3,500 for an equipment rollout) because the learning is reused.
  • Your own time is the bottleneck. If running the application means days on portals, counters, and phone queues, price those days honestly before calling the fee expensive.

When is an expediter not worth it?

Just as plainly, skip the hire when:

  • The job is simple and your plans are clean. A like-for-like remodel or a small trade permit in a city with a good online portal is work many contractors and homeowners file themselves every day.
  • There is no deadline. If starting three weeks later costs you nothing, the main thing an expediter sells — speed and certainty — has no buyer.
  • The city sells its own faster review for less. Philadelphia’s $2,000 accelerated review, above, is the comparison to make in your own city first.
  • The quote is open-ended with no scope. An hourly engagement with no cap is how a $1,500 job becomes a $6,000 surprise.

One more test: ask what the expediter will do that you cannot. “We know the department” is not an answer. A good answer names the steps — the pre-check of your plans, the departments involved, the follow-up rhythm, and how corrections are handled and priced.

What questions should you ask before you hire one?

Permit Place suggests five questions: How many years have you been in business? Do you have case studies with named clients and measurable results? Have you pulled permits in my specific jurisdiction before? Do you charge flat fees or hourly? What reporting and tracking do you provide?

Add two of ours. First: what is not included — correction rounds, hearings, extra departments? Second: what happens to my fee if the city, not me, causes the delay? The answers tell you whether you are hiring a professional with a system, or a courier with a phone.

Frequently asked questions

How much does a permit expediter cost?

Typically $500 to $7,500, per Permit Place’s 2026 guide: $500–$2,500 residential, $1,500–$5,000 for a single commercial site, and $2,000–$7,500 for restaurant work. City permit fees are separate, at $200 to $5,000 or more.

How much does a permit expediter charge per hour?

When they bill hourly, JDJ Consulting puts California rates at $40 to $300 or more per hour. Many jobs are flat-fee instead — ask which model your quote uses, and what a correction round costs.

Are the city’s permit fees included in the expediter’s price?

No. The expediter’s fee pays for their work only. The building department’s own fees — $200 to $5,000 or more, per Permit Place — are paid separately, on top. Some cities also charge for a faster review, like Philadelphia’s $2,000 Accelerated Plan Review.

Can a permit expediter guarantee my permit will be approved faster?

No. An expediter cannot override city rules or promise an approval date. What they can do is submit a complete package the first time, follow up steadily, and handle corrections quickly — removing the delays that come from your side of the counter.

Do I need an expediter for a small home remodel?

Usually not, if your plans are clean, the city’s process is online, and no deadline is pressing. Simple interior work sits at the low end of the fee tables ($350–$800 in California, per JDJ) because there is little to manage. The case for hiring grows with structural work, several departments, or a date you cannot miss.

What is the difference between a permit expediter and a permit runner?

A runner mostly carries and files paperwork — a courier step. An expediter manages the whole application: which permits are needed, a quality check of the plans, follow-up with reviewers, and coordination of corrections. Runners cost less because they do less; be clear which one a quote is for.

Do I need a permit to replace my roof?

By PermitsByCounty Research Team · Published November 13, 2026 · Guides & Answers

QUICK ANSWER

Yes, in most places. A full roof replacement — tearing off the old covering and putting a new one on — needs a roof replacement permit in roughly 9 out of 10 U.S. jurisdictions, per the Roofing Almanac guide checked November 13, 2026. Small repairs are the usual exception: swapping a few shingles or sealing a pipe boot is normally treated as an ordinary repair with no permit. The permit itself is not the big cost. Across 26 cities tracked by PermitCalculator in 2026, the average roof permit fee was $226 and the median was $163. Your city sets the rule and the fee, so check your own building department before you sign a contract.

TL;DR — the whole post in 20 seconds

  • A full tear-off and replacement needs a permit in most cities and counties. A small repair usually does not.
  • The line is local. Houston exempts roof covering work of 100 square feet or less. San Ramon, California draws its line at 200 square feet.
  • Seattle is a real exception: a house or duplex re-roof needs no permit there if the building envelope does not change beyond insulation.
  • Budget $150 to $500 for the fee in most places. San Francisco starts at $514 for a house or duplex. Houston charges $353.15 for its reference house.
  • Let your contractor pull the permit in their name, get the number before tear-off, and hold final payment until the final inspection passes.

Do I need a permit to replace my roof if I tear the old one off?

Yes, almost everywhere. That is the short answer, and it holds for the job most people mean by a roof replacement: the old covering comes off down to the deck, and a new covering goes on.

Angi’s roof permit guide, updated July 9, 2026, puts the same three jobs at the top of its permit-required list: you replace your entire roof, you change the roof structure or framing, or you install an unconventional roofing material in your area. A plain tear-off and re-roof is the first one.

A roof replacement permit is required when you replace your entire roof, change the roof structure or framing, or install unconventional roofing materials in your area

The model code points the same way. Roofing is not on the list of work the International Residential Code exempts from a permit. The code exempts ordinary repairs but does not define that phrase, so each city and county decides where a repair ends and a re-roof begins. That gap is why there is no single national answer.

Roofing is not on the IRC’s model list of work exempt from permits (section R105.2); the code exempts “ordinary repairs” but does not define them, so each jurisdiction decides where a repair ends and a re-roof begins

A roof-over, where a new layer goes on top of the old one, is still permitted work in most places. It is not a way around the permit, and the code limits when it is allowed at all.

Where do repairs stop and a replacement start?

This question decides most real jobs, because few roofs fail all at once. A storm lifts shingles on two slopes. At some size, your city stops calling that a repair.

The Roofing Almanac guide puts the common line at about 100 to 200 square feet, or about 10 to 25 percent of the total roof area. Under 100 square feet is typically exempt. Structural work of any size — replacing decking, changing rafters — needs a permit, full stop.

Two city handouts show the spread. Houston exempts roof covering work of 100 square feet or less. San Ramon, California requires a permit past 200 square feet, with the work done by a C-39 licensed roofer.

Roof covering work of 100 square feet or less is on the City of Houston list of work that does not need a building permit; roof covering beyond 100 square feet is not exempt and uses a re-roof permit with the Re-Roof Only Worksheet (CE-1109)

A building permit is required when more than 200 square feet of roof covering is installed, repaired or replaced, and all roofing work must be performed by a contractor with a valid C-39 (Roofing) license

Add up the whole job, not each patch on its own. If hail damage covers two slopes and the total passes your city’s line, treat it as a re-roof and call the building department before the crew starts. Your contractor’s view is an opinion. The building official’s view is the one that can stop the job.

Emergency tarping after a storm is temporary work and sits outside the line. The permanent repair that follows still counts toward your city’s threshold.

Is there any place a house re-roof needs no permit?

Yes, and Seattle is the clearest example, which is why this post will not tell you the answer is yes in every city.

Seattle’s own permit page says a permit is not required for re-roof work at one- and two-family homes and townhouses, as long as no changes are made to the building envelope other than adding or replacing insulation, and the insulation value is as good as or better than what was there. Change the envelope in any other way, and you need a construction permit. The city’s re-roof permit is aimed at commercial and multifamily work, and at repairs over 500 square feet on those buildings.

A permit is not required for re-roof work at one- and two-family homes and townhouses if no changes are made to the building envelope other than adding or replacing insulation; a re-roof permit is required for commercial and multifamily projects, including repairs over 500 square feet, and the city issues re-roof permits the same day you apply online

Seattle’s house and duplex exception — City of Seattle, Washington · SDCI Re-Roof Permit page, checked November 13, 2026

Source: City of Seattle, Seattle Department of Construction & Inspections — Re-Roof Permit · Date pulled: November 13, 2026

A few rural counties with no building department sit in the same camp. No permit office means no permit to pull. Your insurer and a future buyer can still ask how the roof was put on, though, and a permit record is normally how you would prove it.

So read any national guide, this one included, as the default, not the decision. The default is: full replacement, get a permit. Then check the one page that can overrule it — your own city’s permit page, or a five-minute call to the building department — before you sign.

What does Florida’s 25 percent rule do to a roof repair?

Florida adds a second test, about code, not just paperwork.

The Florida Building Code rule says no more than 25 percent of a roof or roof section may be repaired, replaced, or recovered in a 12-month period unless the entire section is brought up to the current code. Two small repairs in the same year add up, and the clock is any rolling 12 months.

In May 2022, Florida changed who that rule bites. Under Florida Statute 553.844(5), if your roof was built, repaired, or replaced in compliance with the 2007 Florida Building Code or later, only the part you are working on has to meet the current code — even past 25 percent. Older roofs still fall under the full rule. In practice, the line people use is the permit date: on or after March 1, 2009 sits on the newer side.

If more than 25 percent of a roof or roof section is repaired, replaced, or recovered within a 12-month period, the entire section can be required to meet the current Florida Building Code (Existing Building section 706.1.1); since May 2022, Florida Statute 553.844(5) limits that to the repaired portion alone when the existing roof complies with the 2007 Florida Building Code or later, and local governments may not amend that exception by ordinance

Florida’s 25 percent rule and its 2022 exception — State of Florida · Statute effective May 2022; guides checked November 13, 2026

Source: SPRE Construction — Florida’s 25 Percent Roof Rule and K. Simon Construction — Florida Roof Rules · Date pulled: November 13, 2026

That is why your roof’s permit history matters before you price a Florida repair: the record shows which code edition the roof went on under. Our guide to telling a roof’s age from its permit history shows how to pull it. A full replacement in Florida is permitted work everywhere in the state.

Can I put a new layer over the old roof instead?

Sometimes, and the permit still applies in most places. The code word for it is a recover: a new covering installed over the old one, with nothing torn off.

The model code bars a recover in three cases. The existing roof or covering is water-soaked or has worn out so far that it is not a sound base. The existing covering is slate, clay, cement, or asbestos-cement tile. Or the roof already has two or more applications of any type of covering. That third case is where the familiar two-layer limit comes from: if two layers are already up there, the next job is a tear-off down to the deck.

Under IRC section R908, a roof recover is not allowed when the existing roof or covering is water-soaked or has deteriorated so it is not an adequate base, when the existing covering is slate, clay, cement or asbestos-cement tile, or when the roof already has two or more applications of any type of covering

A recover also hides the deck. Nobody sees rotted sheathing under an old layer. If a contractor pushes a roof-over mainly to skip the tear-off cost, ask what is under the old roof and how anyone will know.

How much does a roof permit cost?

Less than most people fear, and far less than the roof itself. PermitCalculator tracked roof replacement permit fees in 26 U.S. cities across 17 states in 2026. The average fee was $226 and the median was $163. The low end was $27 in San Antonio, Texas. The high end was $688 in Portland, Oregon. The guide built from that data puts most homeowners between $150 and $500.

Fees are set by the city or county, not the state, so two houses ten miles apart can pay different amounts for the same roof. Some cities also scale the fee to the value of the work. Ask for the permit fee as its own line in the bid, so you can check it against the city’s schedule.

How does the permit process run, and who pulls it?

For a standard re-roof, the process is short. Your contractor applies, usually online, with the scope of work, a license number, and proof of insurance. The permit is posted on site before work begins. Then come the inspections: commonly a dry-in look while the underlayment is down, and a final inspection after the covering is on.

The Roofing Almanac guide puts standard issuance at 24 to 48 hours in many places, with plan review adding 3 to 10 days where it is required, and most permits good for six to twelve months. Busy large cities can run a week or two. Do not let tear-off start before the permit is posted. A roof with no covering on it is the worst moment to learn the paperwork is not done.

Let the contractor pull the permit, in the contractor’s name. Our guide to who is responsible for pulling a permit covers that choice in full. When a licensed contractor’s name sits on the permit, the record ties that firm to that roof. If the work fails inspection or the crew walks off, you have a paper trail with the city and the license board. If you pull an owner-builder permit for a contractor’s job, that trail points at you instead.

Get the permit number in writing before work starts, and check it on your city’s portal. The name should match the firm on your contract. Hold final payment until the final inspection passes.

A city permit is not always the only approval. A homeowners association may need to approve a color or material change first, and a historic district may need its own review. San Francisco’s application asks about street-visible changes and landmark buildings for that reason. Clear those approvals before you apply, not after the shingles are ordered.

What happens if you replace a roof without a permit?

The trouble comes in three kinds, and Angi names all of them. There are serious fines. The city can force you to take the new roof off. And a house with an unpermitted roof is harder to sell.

Skipping a required roof permit can bring serious fines and penalties, forced removal of the newly installed roof so the project can be redone to code, liability if the work damages the structure or nearby property, and difficulty selling the house

What skipping the permit can cost you — United States · Guide updated July 9, 2026

Source: Angi — Do You Need a Permit to Replace a Roof? · Date pulled: November 13, 2026

Enforcement often starts small. A neighbor calls, or an inspector drives past a tear-off with no permit posted. The city issues a stop-work order, and the job sits open while you apply after the fact. The inspector may also ask for part of the new roof to be opened up so the layers underneath can be checked. Our guide to building without a permit walks through that fix-it path.

Insurance is the quieter risk. A storm claim is much harder to argue when the roof went on with no permit and no inspection. And at sale time, a buyer who finds a newer roof with no permit behind it will price that gap, or walk.

If the work is already done with no permit, call the building department and ask what it needs to bring the roof onto the record. Get the answer in writing. Cities see this every week, and a roof that was put on well can usually be documented.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a permit to replace my roof if I do the work myself?

Yes, if your city requires one for the job. Doing the work yourself does not remove the permit. Some places offer an owner-builder permit, and the roof still gets inspected. San Ramon’s line is drawn by the size of the work, over 200 square feet, not by who holds the hammer.

Do I need a permit to repair a roof, or only to replace it?

Small repairs usually need no permit. Swapping a few shingles or sealing a pipe boot is an ordinary repair in most places. The line is local: Houston exempts roof covering work of 100 square feet or less, and San Ramon requires a permit past 200 square feet. Structural work, like replacing decking, needs a permit at any size.

How much does a permit for roof replacement cost?

Most homeowners pay between $150 and $500. PermitCalculator’s 2026 tracking of 26 cities put the average fee at $226 and the median at $163, from $27 in San Antonio to $688 in Portland. Your city sets the fee, so confirm it on your city’s fee schedule before you budget.

Does a roof-over with a second layer need a permit?

In most places, yes. A recover is still roofing work. The code also bars a recover when the old roof is water-soaked or worn out, when the covering is slate, clay, cement, or asbestos-cement tile, or when two layers are already on the roof.

How long does it take to get a roof permit?

A standard re-roof often issues in 24 to 48 hours where no plan review is needed, per the Roofing Almanac guide. Plan review adds about 3 to 10 days, and busy large cities can take a week or two. Seattle issues its re-roof permits the same day you apply online.

Who should pull the roof permit — me or my roofer?

Your roofer, in the roofer’s name. The permit ties the firm’s license to your roof. Get the permit number before tear-off, check that the name matches your contract, and hold final payment until the final inspection passes.

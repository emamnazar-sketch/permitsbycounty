How do I get a retroactive permit for work that is already done?

By PermitsByCounty Research Team · Published October 24, 2026 · Guides & Answers

QUICK ANSWER

Yes, in most places you can get a retroactive permit for work that is already done. It is also called an after-the-fact permit or an as-built permit. You contact your building department, hire a licensed pro to draw plans of the work as it stands, file the application, and let an inspector check the work — which often means opening small parts of walls so hidden wiring and plumbing can be seen. LegalClarity, checked October 24, 2026, says the permit fee is typically two to three times the normal cost, and corrections are extra. If the work meets code, you get a final sign-off and the work goes on the record.

TL;DR — the whole post in 20 seconds

  • Start with the building department. Ask what they need for work that is already done.
  • You will need as-built plans — drawings of what was actually built, not what was planned.
  • Expect to pay more than a normal permit, and expect the inspector to need to see behind walls.
  • Work that breaks a setback, height, or zoning rule may not be legalized at all.
  • A simple, sound job can close in weeks. A large job with corrections can take 2 to 6 months.

What is a retroactive permit?

A retroactive permit is a permit you apply for after the work is finished. A normal permit is approved before anyone starts. A retroactive permit asks the city to approve work that already exists.

The names change by city. Your department may call it an after-the-fact permit, an as-built permit, a legalization permit, or a permit correction. LegalClarity uses all of those names for the same goal: legalize the work so it meets current building codes.

This is not the same as closing an open permit. An open permit was pulled, but the final inspection never happened. Unpermitted work was never filed at all. Our guide on how to close an open permit in Miami-Dade, Broward or Palm Beach County covers the first case. This post covers the second.

You need this path if you finished a basement, converted a garage, added a bathroom, or moved a wall with no permit pulled — or if you bought a house and the permit history does not match the house. The duty sits with the property: if a past owner did the work, the department still looks to you as the current owner to make it legal, though some places give a buyer who did not do the work more time or a waived penalty.

How do I get a retroactive permit, step by step?

Most departments use this order. Your city may swap a step or add a form, but the shape is steady.

Step 1: Gather what you have. Pull together photos from when the work was done, receipts, and contractor invoices. LegalClarity makes this the first step: proof of how the work was built makes inspection easier. Also pull your permit history for the address. Our guide on how to check a house’s permit history before you buy it walks through that search.

Step 2: Call the building department and say it plainly. Tell them you have work done without a permit and you want to legalize it. Ask what application they use, whether they offer a first consultation, and whether a setback, height, or use rule would stop this work before it starts. That last question can save you the cost of plans for work a city cannot approve in that spot. LegalClarity says some places reduce penalties for work reported by the owner.

Step 3: Hire a licensed pro to make as-built plans. A licensed architect, engineer, or qualified drafter measures the space and draws what is there — walls, doors, windows, and the systems that changed. Do not reuse old design plans if the built work differs from them.

Several hundred to several thousand dollars for as-built drawings, depending on scope; a separate guide puts drawing fees at 5 to 20 percent of the total project cost

For structural work, ask early if you also need an engineer’s letter for hidden parts like a beam or footing.

Step 4: File the application and pay the fee, with the penalty. File with the as-built plans. LegalClarity notes some departments look closer at after-the-fact files. Get the penalty number in writing before you file if you can.

Permit fee typically 2 to 3 times the normal cost; some places charge double, others charge more, and some add a flat penalty or a separate fine on top

Step 5: Open what the inspector needs to see. An inspector cannot approve wiring, plumbing, framing, or insulation they cannot see. If that work is behind drywall, you may have to open small sections. Violation Clinic calls this destructive testing. Keep openings as small as the inspector allows, and do not patch them until you are told to. Visible work usually needs less of this.

Step 6: Fix what does not pass, then get re-inspected. A licensed contractor fixes what the inspector flags — undersized wiring, poor ventilation, a missing egress window are common — and you book another inspection. One round of fixes is normal.

Step 7: Get the final sign-off in writing, and keep it. When inspections pass and fees are paid, the department issues the final approval, and for a change of use an updated certificate of occupancy or completion. Keep that paper with your house records for a sale, refinance, or insurance claim.

What does a retroactive permit cost in total?

The permit fee is only the first line. The full cost is usually four lines: the plans; the fee with its penalty; opening and patching walls, paid whether any defect is found or not; and corrections, small if the work was built well and large if it was not. That is why the total often ends up higher than permitting the job in the first place. A Portland builder’s guide puts the permit itself at about $300 to $2,000 or more in Portland, before drawings and fixes.

About $300–$2,000+ for the retroactive permit in Portland, at 2–3 times a standard permit, plus drawings, inspections, and corrections

Get a licensed contractor’s written view before you file if you can. It tells you whether this is a fee-and-plans job or a rebuild job — very different budgets.

How long does it take?

A small, sound job can move in a few weeks. A larger job — a basement, a garage conversion, an added bathroom — takes longer. The Portland guide puts the full process at 2 to 6 months, depending on complexity and corrections. Treat that as a planning range, not a promise. If you have a closing date, say so at Step 2.

Typically 2 to 6 months for a retroactive permit, depending on project complexity and whether code corrections are needed

Can a retroactive permit be denied?

Yes, and this is the risk to check before you spend on plans. A permit can be denied if the work breaks zoning rules, creates a safety problem that cannot be fixed in place, or would need to be rebuilt to meet code. LegalClarity says zoning is the most common block: an addition into a setback, over lot coverage, or over a height limit is a land-use problem, not just a building problem.

You can ask for a variance, but LegalClarity warns they are very hard to get for work already built. If a permit is denied and no variance is granted, a department can order the work removed at the owner’s cost. Work in the right place and built soundly, missing only paper and inspections, is what usually gets legalized.

What does this look like on a real job?

Violation Clinic describes a Miami garage conversion. A past owner had turned a garage into a bedroom with no permits. The wiring had not been certified, and the windows did not meet egress rules. The current owner hired a structural engineer, who drew as-built plans, filed for the after-the-fact permit, and designed the fixes: a dedicated circuit, proper insulation, and a compliant egress window. After two rounds of inspections, the department gave final approval and the violation was dismissed.

Should you get one if you are about to sell?

If you have time before you list, usually yes — or at least get the department’s answer first. A legalized job can be counted, appraised, and insured. Unpermitted work often cannot. Our guide on whether you can sell a house with unpermitted work covers the other paths: sell as-is with full disclosure, or sell to a cash buyer. Disclosure is not optional either way. If you do nothing, the violation does not fade with age. LegalClarity notes there is no general time limit that makes unpermitted work legal just because years passed, and insurers can deny a claim from work that was never inspected.

Frequently asked questions

How do I get a retroactive permit for work that is already done?

Contact your building department and tell them you want to legalize work done without a permit. Gather photos and receipts, hire a licensed pro to draw as-built plans, file the application, pay the fee plus any penalty, open small sections if the inspector needs to see hidden work, fix anything that does not pass, and get the final sign-off in writing.

How much does a retroactive permit cost?

The permit fee alone is typically two to three times the normal fee, per LegalClarity, and some places charge more or add a separate fine. The larger costs are usually the as-built plans at several hundred to several thousand dollars, opening and patching walls, and any corrections.

Do I have to tear open walls for a retroactive permit?

Often, yes, for hidden work. Inspectors have to see wiring, plumbing, framing, and insulation to approve them. If that work is behind drywall or a ceiling, small sections are opened, checked, and patched after approval. Visible work usually needs less of this.

How long does a retroactive permit take?

A small, sound job can take a few weeks. A larger job can take 2 to 6 months, per a Portland builder’s guide, depending on complexity and correction rounds. Your city’s review queue sets the real pace.

Can a retroactive permit be denied?

Yes. Work that breaks a setback, lot-coverage, height, or use rule, or work with a safety problem that cannot be fixed in place, can be denied. A variance is possible but hard to get for work already built. If it is denied, a city can order the work removed at the owner’s cost.

What if a past owner did the unpermitted work, not me?

You still have to deal with it as the current owner — the problem follows the property. Some departments are more flexible with a buyer who did not do the work, with more time or a waived penalty. Ask at your first visit, and bring your purchase papers if you have them.

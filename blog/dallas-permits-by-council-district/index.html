How can I track building permits by council district in Dallas?

By PermitsByCounty Research Team · Published November 8, 2026 · Guides & Answers

QUICK ANSWER

You track Dallas permits by council district inside DallasNow, the city's own portal. Go to dallas.gov/dallasnow, open Building, then Reports, and run a Search by Council report — issued, submitted, or active — for one of the city's 14 council districts, or for all of them at once. You don't need to sign in, and the report lands on your device as an Excel spreadsheet you can filter by record type and status. The City of Dallas guide for these reports was checked on November 7, 2026.

TL;DR — the whole post in 20 seconds

  • DallasNow is where district tracking lives now. The old open-data feed ends in 2020, so there's no live Dallas permit list to pull from anywhere else.
  • Three reports do the work: issued permits in a date range, submitted permits in a date range, and every active permit in progress right now.
  • Pick one district or All. Dallas has 14 council districts, and a report for All 14 is the closest thing to a citywide Dallas permit list that exists today.
  • Reports download as Excel spreadsheets. Filter by record type, then by record status, and a raw city list turns into a short call list.
  • The city also posts monthly permit tables by council district on its Reports page — a slower, read-only cross-check next to your own weekly run.

Why track Dallas by council district at all?

A council district is a working territory. It's the area one council member represents, and the city sorts its permit reports by that map. For a contractor, the district is a plain way to cut a big city into pieces a crew can actually cover.

Dallas is too large to chase whole. A GC who bids tenant work in two districts and a sub who roofs in three others don't need the same list. Running the same report for your districts every week gives you a short, steady feed of new work in the ground you already serve, instead of a citywide pile you will never finish reading.

The district is also how the city itself talks about building activity. Its published monthly tables count new construction permits by council district. If you track the same map the city publishes on, your numbers and the city's numbers line up when a customer or a council office asks what is happening in a neighborhood.

One warning before the steps. A council district is not a ZIP code, and it is not Dallas County. District lines are political lines inside the City of Dallas. A job in Irving or Garland sits in a different city with a different permit office, and it will never show up in a DallasNow report, whatever district map you are holding.

Where did the old Dallas permit feed go?

If you used to pull Dallas permits from the city's open-data portal, that road is closed. We counted the old Socrata set record by record for our source audit: 126,840 rows, and every one of them lands in 2020 or earlier. The set holds 52,037 permits from 2019 and 27,459 from 2020, and not one record from 2021 on.

That is why paid trackers handle Dallas the way they do. Tradewind, a Texas permit service, leaves Dallas out of its coverage and says the county feed stopped refreshing. Its year is off — the records end in 2020, not later — but the direction is right. There is no live machine feed of Dallas permits to buy or to scrape.

What replaced the feed is DallasNow. The city launched it on May 5, 2025, on Accela's platform, replacing the old Posse system and joining about 15 separate systems — permitting, planning, inspections, engineering — in one portal. The public side of that portal is where the district reports live.

How do I run a DallasNow report by council district?

The city's own guide walks through it, and the whole run takes a few minutes. No account, no sign-in.

One, go to dallas.gov/dallasnow and select DallasNow. Two, select Building on the navigation bar, so the portal knows you want building records. Three, select Reports, then pick the report you want — the three Search by Council reports are listed below. Four, if you picked the issued or submitted report, enter a start date and an end date. Five, select the council district you want, or select All to include every district. Six, select Submit. The portal builds the report as a spreadsheet and saves it to the Downloads folder on your device.

Three Search by Council reports — Building Active, Building Issued Permit, and Building Submitted — covering permits in progress, permits issued in a date range, and permits submitted in a date range

Two details in the guide are worth knowing before your first run. The active report has no date range — it returns the permits in progress at the moment you run it, all of them. And for the dated reports, a larger date range can take longer to generate. A one-week window comes back fast. A one-year window is a coffee break, and it is also more rows than anyone will work through by hand.

The last step trips people up in a small way. The file doesn't open on its own. After you submit, open it from the browser's download panel — select Downloads if the panel is not showing, then select the report name. If you run three reports back to back, three spreadsheets land in that folder with similar names. Rename each one with its report type, district, and date range before you run the next, or sort out later which file was the issued run and which was submitted. Five seconds of naming saves a real mix-up when a bid depends on whether a permit was filed or approved.

Which permits do the district reports cover?

The by-council reports cover four kinds of building permits: commercial alteration and addition permits, commercial new construction permits, residential alteration and addition permits, and residential new construction permits. Alterations and additions include remodels.

That scope fits territory tracking well. New construction tells you where ground is about to break. Alterations and additions tell you where owners are spending on buildings that already stand — the tenant finish-outs, remodels, and expansions that feed most small and mid-size crews.

Read the scope with clear eyes, though. This report family is a building-permit report. It's the front door of a project, not the whole house. The trade records, inspections, and certificates that follow a building permit live in their own parts of DallasNow, and our Dallas tenant improvement post walks through how those pieces file. Use the district report to find the job early. Use the rest of the portal to follow it.

There is a timing reason to look at submitted permits, not only issued ones. Permit work starts on paper weeks before it starts on site — Shovels, a permit data firm, puts permits at 2 to 6 weeks before ground breaks. A submitted report is the earlier signal. An issued report is the surer one. Running both is how you hear about a job while there is still time to bid it, then confirm it once the city has approved it.

How do I turn a district spreadsheet into a call list?

The download is an Excel file, and the city's guide spends half its pages on working in it — that's a hint about where the value sits. A raw report for all 14 districts is a phone book. Filtered for your work, it is a morning's calls.

If the file opens in Protected View, select Enable Editing first, or none of Excel's tools will run. Then turn on filters from the Data ribbon. The guide's own examples are the two filters that matter most: filter by record type to see only commercial new construction, then filter by record status to see only the permits at the stage you want — its example is commercial new construction permits that are ready to issue. Stacking those two filters on a district report takes you from every permit in a district to the handful that match your trade and your timing.

A few habits make the weekly run pay. Keep the same date window every week — say, the last seven days for issued and submitted — so no permit falls in a crack between runs. Save each week's file with the date in its name, so you can see a project move from submitted to issued to active across your own files. And use Find in Excel, as the guide shows, to jump to a record number or an address when a GC mentions a job by name.

What you do with the short list is the part no portal can do for you. Match it against the builders you already know — our builder research post covers how to check a builder's record before you bid their job — and call the ones doing work in your districts while the job is still new. A permit in your district this week is a conversation. The same permit three months from now is history.

What does the city publish by district on its own?

You don't have to run every number yourself. The Planning and Development Reports page posts monthly tables for the city's fiscal year, and the headline set is residential and commercial new construction permits by council district, month by month. October's table for fiscal 2026-2027 is posted, and the rest of the year fills in as the months close.

Those tables are a cross-check, not a work list. They are counts, posted after the month ends, and they cover new construction only. Use them to see which districts are heating up over a quarter and to check that your own weekly runs are catching the work. Use your DallasNow runs for the names, addresses, and timing you can act on this week.

The two views work best as a pair. The monthly table tells you where to point next month's effort — a district whose new construction count keeps climbing is a district worth a second crew conversation. Your weekly runs tell you who to call inside that district while the work is still fresh. One view picks the ground. The other picks the names. A shop that only reads the monthly table is always a month late, and a shop that only runs weekly reports can miss the slow shift of work from one side of the city to another until the quarter is gone.

The same page is a quiet reminder of what district tracking cannot do. The city splits its own reporting into separate sets — new construction by district in one, certificates of occupancy in another, demolition and solar in others. No single Dallas report shows the whole life of a project. Anyone who promises you one complete Dallas list is selling you a picture the city itself does not publish.

What are the limits of tracking Dallas this way?

Name them plainly, because they shape how you should work.

It's manual. Somebody has to run the report each week, because there is no feed to connect and no alert to set. The run is short, but it is a habit, not a system, and it stops the week nobody does it.

It's Dallas city only. The report knows nothing about the rest of Dallas County, and nothing about Fort Worth, Arlington, or any other DFW city. Our Fort Worth report next door shows the other pattern — a city feed we can pull and check every night. Dallas offers no such feed today.

It's a snapshot, not a history. The active report shows what is in progress when you run it. The dated reports show the window you asked for. Anything filed before DallasNow, back in the Posse years, is not in these reports, and the dead open-data set is the only public pile that still holds the old years — ending in 2020.

And the spreadsheet is only as good as your filters. Run All districts with a wide date range and no filters, and you have homework, not leads. Run your two or three districts, one week at a time, filtered to your kind of work, and you have a list worth calling.

Frequently asked questions

How can I track building permits by council district in Dallas?

Run a Search by Council report in DallasNow. Go to dallas.gov/dallasnow, select Building, then Reports, pick issued, submitted, or active, choose your district or All, and submit. The report downloads as an Excel spreadsheet, and no sign-in is required.

Do I need an account to run a DallasNow permit report?

No. The city's guide states the report steps need no sign-in. Accounts matter when you apply for permits or manage your own records. Reading the public district reports is open.

What is the difference between the issued, submitted, and active reports in Dallas?

Issued shows permits the city approved in your date range. Submitted shows permits filed in your date range — the earlier signal, before approval. Active shows every building permit in progress at the moment you run it, with no date range.

How many council districts does Dallas have?

Fourteen. Council members for Places 1 through 14 are each elected by one district, and the mayor is elected citywide, under Chapter III, Section 1 of the City Charter. A report run for All covers all 14 districts at once.

Can I get a live feed or API of Dallas building permits instead?

Not today. The city's old open-data set ends in 2020 — 126,840 rows, none after that year — and DallasNow reports are generated by hand in the portal. That's why this guide is built around a short weekly run instead of a data feed.

Does a Dallas council district report cover all of Dallas County?

No. It covers the City of Dallas only, split by its 14 council districts. Other DFW cities run their own permit offices, and Dallas County itself is not the issuer on these reports. A job in Garland or Irving will not appear.

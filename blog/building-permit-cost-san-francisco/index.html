How much does a building permit cost in San Francisco?

By PermitsByCounty Research Team · Published November 27, 2026 · Guides & Answers

QUICK ANSWER

The building permit cost San Francisco projects pay is worked out from your project’s valuation, not from a flat price list. The Department of Building Inspection, called DBI, charges a plan review fee plus a permit issuance fee under its Table 1A-A schedule. A bathroom remodel valued at about $30,000 pays about $1,101 in DBI fees on the no-plans path, or about $2,205 if DBI requires plan review. A kitchen remodel valued at about $50,000 pays about $1,707 with no plans, or about $3,378 with plan review. Trade permits are separate flat fees on top: plumbing is $290, mechanical work is $290, and a simple electrical job is $305. Planning Department fees and other city charges sit on top of the DBI bill for bigger jobs.

TL;DR — the whole post in 20 seconds

  • San Francisco prices a building permit off your project’s valuation. There is no single flat fee for most work.
  • DBI charges two parts under Table 1A-A: a plan review fee and a permit issuance fee. No-plans jobs pay the issuance fee only.
  • A $30,000 bathroom pays about $1,101 with no plans and about $2,205 with plan review, counting plumbing and electrical.
  • A $50,000 kitchen pays about $1,707 with no plans and about $3,378 with plan review, counting plumbing, mechanical and electrical.
  • Flat fees cover some small jobs: a single-family roof replacement is $320, solar up to 10 kW is $320, plumbing is $290, and a simple electrical circuit is $305.

One city, one counter: San Francisco is its own county

San Francisco is a city and a county in one, so there is no second county counter to check. If your address is in San Francisco, your building permit comes from the city’s Department of Building Inspection, DBI, at 49 South Van Ness Avenue. DBI sets the fee, runs the review, and sends the inspector.

That sounds simple, and the counter part is. The bill is not one bill, though. DBI’s building permit fee is only the first line. Trade permits for plumbing, electrical and mechanical work are priced on their own tables and added on top. For additions, new homes and bigger projects, the Planning Department charges its own review fee, and school and impact fees can apply too. The city itself gives a plain rule for the whole package on a new unit: city fees across all departments usually run about 6% to 9% of construction cost.

So when someone quotes you one San Francisco permit number, ask which lines it counts. A DBI-only number and an all-departments number for the same job can be thousands of dollars apart, and both can be honest.

How DBI prices the building permit

DBI’s schedule is Table 1A-A of the San Francisco Building Code. It has two columns, and which columns you pay turns on one question: does your job need plans?

If DBI takes your job over the counter with no plans — in-kind work, nothing structural, nothing moved — you pay the permit issuance fee only. That column starts at $195 for the first $500 of valuation or less, then adds $6.47 for each extra $100 up to $2,000, then $9.40 for each extra $1,000 up to $50,000 on the no-plans table in force for permits issued on or after September 1, 2025.

If your job needs plans and a review, you pay both columns: the plan review fee plus the permit issuance fee. On the alteration table, plan review starts at $182 for the first $500 plus $9.47 per extra $100 up to $2,000, then $324 for the first $2,000 plus $27.83 per extra $1,000 up to $50,000. Issuance on that table starts at $169 for the first $500 plus $3.67 per extra $100, then $224 for the first $2,000 plus $6.83 per extra $1,000.

Two things follow from that math. First, the same job costs about twice as much to permit when it needs plan review, because you are paying for the review itself. Second, your stated valuation drives the fee, and DBI does not have to accept a valuation that looks low for the work described. For kitchens and bathrooms, DBI’s own guidance sets minimum valuations — $24,663 for a kitchen and $20,552 for a bathroom — so you cannot shrink the fee by writing down a small number for a full remodel.

Building permit cost San Francisco: two worked remodels

Numbers land better as whole jobs, so here are the two remodels people ask about most, priced from DBI’s July 2026 tables.

Take a bathroom remodel valued at about $30,000, with plumbing and electrical work, nothing structural. On the no-plans path, the issuance fee is about $506. Add the flat plumbing permit at $290 and a simple electrical permit at $305, and the DBI total is about $1,101. If DBI requires plans for that same bathroom, the building part rises to about $1,610 for plan review plus issuance, and the total with the same two trade permits is about $2,205.

Now a kitchen remodel valued at about $50,000, with plumbing, a gas or venting line, and electrical work. With no plans, the issuance fee is about $677. Add plumbing at $290, mechanical at $290, and electrical at $450 for a job with 11 to 20 outlets, and the total is about $1,707. With plan review, the building part is about $2,348, and the total is about $3,378.

A second check on the same rooms gives the same picture. A fee registry that applied the July 2026 schedules to one standard $65,000 kitchen put the San Francisco total at $3,351.90 — the highest of the 15 large cities it priced — and a standard $30,000 bathroom at $2,210.38. Kukun’s permit database, which tracks real permits, lists a San Francisco kitchen permit at $1,850, a bathroom at $1,200, and a room addition at $3,500, with average processing of about 28 days. The sources use different job sizes, so the numbers differ. The order does not: San Francisco sits at the top of the permit-cost table in each one.

The flat fees: small jobs with a fixed price

Not every San Francisco permit runs through the valuation math. DBI lists flat fees for a set of small, standard jobs, and those are knowable to the dollar before you file.

A roof replacement on a single-family house or a duplex is $320 flat. The same job on any other building is $556. A solar system of 10 kW or less is $320 flat. A simple electrical circuit, up to 10 outlets or devices with one inspection, is $305 flat, and a job with 11 to 20 outlets is $450.

Trade permits for a single home follow the same flat pattern. A plumbing permit for a single kitchen or bathroom remodel in one home is $290 under Category 1P. A mechanical permit for gas appliance work in one home is $290 under Category 1M. A like-for-like water heater swap is priced in parts rather than as one line: the registry example put a gas water heater permit at $291.33 all in, while the PermitCompass breakdown puts a swap at about $435 to $801 once the plumbing permit, the boiler program permit, and a mechanical permit for changed venting are counted. Ask DBI which parts your swap needs before you budget the low number.

Since September 14, 2026, no-plan kitchen and bathroom jobs that qualify — in-kind, nonstructural work in a home that is already legally permitted — are filed through PermitSF, the city’s online permit system. If your remodel fits that lane, you skip the counter trip, but you do not skip the fees.

Bigger jobs: additions, ADUs and whole-home work

Once a job adds space or a unit, the DBI fee is only the start of the city bill.

The valuation math keeps running. A whole-home remodel valued at about $500,000 carries a building permit fee of about $10,669 on its own, before any trade permits. At the top brackets of Table 1A-A, a $500,000 valuation sits at $8,165 in plan review for the first $500,000 plus $2,798 in issuance on the new-construction table — the alteration table lands in the same range. Trade permits for each system you touch are added on top.

The Planning Department then charges its own review. For new construction, Planning’s schedule runs $3,502 for work under $100,000 of cost, then $3,504 plus 3.855% of the cost above $100,000 up to $499,999. For an alteration or a conversion, it runs $504 under $10,000, then $516 plus 5.274% of the cost above $10,000, and $3,838 plus 3.524% above $50,000. A back-check is $366.

Added living space can also carry the school fee: San Francisco Unified charges $3.79 per square foot of added habitable area. Impact fees were cut by about two-thirds in August 2026 for many projects, and they are $0 for an ADU under 750 square feet or on a lot with three or fewer units — but they are project-specific, so price them with Planning’s own calculator for your address, not from a general guide.

Put together, this is where the city’s 6% to 9% rule comes from. On a $300,000 detached ADU, one worked example in the sources lands at about $18,400 in Planning and DBI fees before trade permits and school fees. Nobody can give you an honest single ADU permit price without your valuation, your square footage, and your lot. Anyone who does is guessing.

How to get your real number before you start

Start with the scope, in writing. Rooms, valuation, what moves and what stays. In San Francisco the fee turns on two facts — your valuation and whether plans are required — so a clear scope settles both.

Next, decide the lane. In-kind kitchen or bathroom work with nothing structural goes through PermitSF as a no-plan job, and you budget the issuance fee plus your trade permits. Work that moves walls, adds space, or changes a unit goes to plan review, and you budget both DBI columns, plus Planning’s review if your job touches the Planning schedule.

Then add the flat lines your job touches: $290 plumbing, $290 mechanical, $305 or $450 electrical, and the school fee at $3.79 a square foot if you are adding living space. Check the total against the city’s 6% to 9% rule for all-department fees. If your number lands far below that band on a big job, a line is missing.

Last, write the permit total next to your contractor’s quote. If the contract carries a permit allowance, you now have DBI’s own tables to check it against, line by line, instead of a round number you have to take on trust.

Frequently asked questions

How much does a building permit cost in San Francisco?

It turns on your project’s valuation and whether plans are required. A bathroom valued at about $30,000 pays about $1,101 in DBI fees with no plans, or about $2,205 with plan review, counting plumbing and electrical. A kitchen valued at about $50,000 pays about $1,707 with no plans, or about $3,378 with plan review. Trade permits are extra flat fees, and Planning fees apply to bigger jobs.

Who issues building permits in San Francisco?

The San Francisco Department of Building Inspection, DBI. San Francisco is a combined city and county, so there is no separate county permit office. DBI runs the review and inspections, while the Planning Department charges its own review fee on projects that fall under its schedule.

Why does plan review raise the San Francisco permit cost so much?

Because the building permit fee is two fees: a plan review fee plus a permit issuance fee. A no-plans job pays the issuance fee only. A job with plans pays both, so the building part of the bill roughly doubles before any trade permits are added.

What does a kitchen or bathroom permit cost in San Francisco?

From DBI’s July 2026 tables, a kitchen valued at about $50,000 totals about $1,707 with no plans and about $3,378 with plan review, counting plumbing, mechanical and electrical. A bathroom valued at about $30,000 totals about $1,101 with no plans and about $2,205 with plan review. Kukun’s database of real permits lists $1,850 for a kitchen and $1,200 for a bathroom.

Are plumbing and electrical permits included in the building permit fee?

No. DBI prices trade permits on separate tables, and they are added on top of the building permit fee. For one home, plumbing is $290, mechanical work is $290, and a simple electrical job up to 10 outlets is $305. The Table 1A-A building fee also does not include Planning, Fire, Public Works or Public Health fees.

What extra fees apply to an addition or an ADU in San Francisco?

On top of the DBI building and trade fees, the Planning Department charges its own review — for new construction, $3,502 under $100,000 of cost, then a percentage of cost above that — and added living space can carry a school fee of $3.79 per square foot. Impact fees depend on the project; many were cut in August 2026, and an ADU under 750 square feet pays none. The city puts total city fees at about 6% to 9% of construction cost.

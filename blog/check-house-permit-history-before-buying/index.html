How do I check a house’s permit history before I buy it?

By PermitsByCounty Research Team · Published October 20, 2026 · Guides & Answers

QUICK ANSWER

Before you buy a house, search its address in the city or county permit portal and match every visible addition, remodel, roof, and system upgrade to a permit marked closed or finaled. Do this early in your due diligence period, before or alongside the home inspection. If work you can see has no matching permit, or a permit is still open, ask questions before you are locked in — unpermitted or unfinished work can become your cost after closing.

The short version

  • Check early. Run the check during your due diligence period — the window in your contract when you can still ask questions — not the week of closing.
  • Match what you see to what is on record. Every addition, finished basement, and new roof should have a permit marked closed or finaled.
  • Open permits and unpermitted work are questions, not answers. Settle them with the seller before your contingency period ends.
  • The records are public and usually free. Austin and Chicago both let you search by address online — both are worked through below.

Why does a house’s permit history matter before you buy?

It matters because a permit is the paper trail that shows work on the house was planned, approved, and checked by an inspector. When you buy a house, you buy its paper trail too.

A closed permit tells a clean story. The city or county approved the work, an inspector looked at it, and the file was finished. An open permit tells an unfinished story: the work was approved and started, but it never passed its final inspection. After closing, finishing that file can become your job — and your cost.

Work with no permit at all is the biggest question mark. No inspector ever looked at it. It may be solid work. It may not be. You cannot tell from the paint. The permit check gives you facts to ask about while you can still act on them — before your money is locked in.

There is one more reason. Your lender and the title company — the firm that handles the legal transfer of the house — often check for open permits too, and flag them. Finding them early, on your own schedule, is easier than finding them days before closing.

What should you gather before you search?

Have these four things in front of you before you open the permit search. Together they make the check faster, and the match much more accurate:

  • The full street address — with the unit number and the ZIP code. Permit systems are picky, and one small difference can hide a real record.
  • The parcel number, also called the APN — the county’s ID number for the piece of land. Find it through the county assessor — the office that values property for taxes — or on tax records. When an address search finds nothing, a parcel number search often does.
  • The seller disclosure — the form where the seller lists what they know about the house, including work that was done on it. You will compare it against the permit record, line by line.
  • The listing claims — what the ad says was done: “new roof,” “finished basement,” “updated kitchen.” Write each claim down. Each one is a claim you can check against a permit.

How do you check a permit history by address, step by step?

Here is the whole check, in order. Most buyers finish it in under an hour, and it costs nothing.

  1. Find the right office — the city or the county. A house inside a city gets its permits from the city’s building department, and its records live in the city portal. A house outside city limits — in an unincorporated area, which means land that is not part of any city — gets its permits from the county. Search the wrong office and you find nothing, even when the records exist.
  2. Search by the address first. Type the full street address into the portal. If nothing comes back, search by the parcel number instead. If it is still empty, drop the unit number and the ZIP code, search the street name alone, and scan the list — small differences in how an address is written hide real records.
  1. List the work you can see, and match each item to a permit. Walk the house, and look at the listing photos again. Write down every finished basement, addition, garage conversion, new roof, electrical panel upgrade, deck, and water heater or heating and cooling replacement. Then find a permit for each one. Every item with a matching permit is one question answered.
  2. Read the status of each permit. Closed or finaled is the good end state — the work passed its final inspection and the file is done. Open means the file was never finished. Expired means the permit ran out of time before it was closed. An open or expired permit is not a deal-breaker by itself, but each one needs a question before you go further.
  3. Share your list with your home inspector. Your permit list tells your inspector where to look hardest. Work with no permit behind it deserves the longest look. A short talk before the inspection makes the inspection itself worth more.
  4. Ask plain questions — of the seller and of the building department. Put the questions in writing, so the answers are in writing too. Two questions cover most of it:

    “Are there any open permits on this property?”

    “Are there records for additions, remodels, electrical, plumbing, HVAC, or roofing?”

  5. Use what you find before your contingency ends. Your contingency period is the set window in your contract when you can still ask for changes, or walk away. You can ask the seller for a price cut, a repair credit, or to close the open permit before closing. And know this going in: a large addition with no permit at all can be a fair reason to walk away.

What does this look like in Austin, in Travis County?

Say the house is in Austin. Austin addresses sit inside the city, so their records live with the City of Austin. The city posts its issued permits in a free public dataset that anyone can search by address. Each record shows the permit type, the issue date, where the work is, a short description of the work, the square footage, and the project value — enough to match the work you saw to a real line in the record.

5 permit types in one public dataset

The City of Austin’s Issued Construction Permits dataset covers building, electrical, mechanical, plumbing, and driveway/sidewalk permits issued by the City of Austin. Each record carries the issue date, the location, a description of the work, the square footage, and the valuation.

And in Chicago, in Cook County?

For a house inside Chicago, start with the City of Chicago Data Portal, which lets anyone search the city’s building permits by permit number or by address. For a house in an unincorporated part of Cook County, the records sit with the county, not the city. Once again, picking the right office decides whether you find anything at all.

What can permit records miss?

Permit records are strong, but they are not complete. Very old work — done before an office kept records on a computer — may live only in paper files, or may not be recorded at all.

Some smaller towns and rural counties have no online portal at all. There, the steps stay the same and only the tools change: call the building department and ask for the permit history for the address, or file a public records request — a short written request for government records, usually free or cheap.

And some small jobs never needed a permit in the first place. Like-for-like swaps and minor repairs are exempt in many places. So treat a missing record as a question to ask — never as a verdict by itself.

What mistakes do buyers make when they check?

Five mistakes show up again and again. Every one of them is easy to avoid:

  • Searching one office and stopping. City and county keep separate record books. An empty search in one office says nothing at all about the other.
  • Checking too late. Buyers who run this check the week of closing have no time left to use what they find. Run it early, during due diligence.
  • Checking only the big work. Roofs, electrical panels, and water heaters need permits in most places too — and they are the very items a seller may forget to mention.
  • Reading “no record” as an answer. No record can mean the wrong office, an old paper file, or work that was never permitted. It is the start of a question, not the end of one.
  • Keeping the list to themselves. The check pays off when your inspector, your agent, and the seller all see the same list — in writing.

Frequently asked questions

Can I look up a house’s permits by address for free?

Yes, in most places. City and county permit records are public, and their online searches are free to use — you search by the street address. The only cost that can show up is a small copy fee if an office has to pull and copy an old paper file for you.

What does it mean when a permit is closed or finaled?

It means the work was finished, passed its final inspection, and the office closed the file. Closed or finaled is the clean end state you want to see on every permit for the house. It tells you an inspector signed off on the job.

What if a finished basement or addition has no permit on record?

It may mean the work was done without a permit, or the record may be old and kept only on paper at the building department. Ask the seller about it in writing, tell your home inspector, and call the building department before your contingency period ends. Do not guess — fixing unpermitted work can become your cost after closing.

Can an open permit delay or stop a closing?

It can cause real problems at closing. Title companies and lenders often flag open permits when they review a property, because the unfinished file can follow the house to its new owner. If you find an open permit, tell your closing team early, so there is time to sort it out before your closing date.

How far back do permit records go?

It depends on the city or county. Many offices have years of permits searchable online, and older records are often kept on paper or in scanned files at the building department. If the online search shows nothing old, call the office and ask how far back its records go.

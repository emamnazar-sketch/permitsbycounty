How do I close an open permit before I sell my house?

By PermitsByCounty Research Team · Published October 25, 2026 · Guides & Answers

QUICK ANSWER

To close an open permit before you sell your house, search your address with your building department, ask the permit office in writing what is still missing, and book the final inspection. If the work passes, the department marks the permit closed. If it does not pass, a licensed contractor fixes what the inspector names and you book again. First Financial Title Agency, checked October 25, 2026, warns that a title search that finds an open permit can halt a closing, and lenders may delay or deny financing. Start before you list.

TL;DR — the whole post in 20 seconds

  • An open permit means a permit was pulled, but the final inspection never happened.
  • Find it early. Search by address before you list, not when the title company finds it.
  • Most simple cases close with one final inspection. Cases that fail need fixes and a second visit.
  • An expired permit is harder. You may have to reactivate it or apply for a new one.
  • Get the closed record in writing.

What is an open permit?

An open permit is a permit that was opened and never finished on paper. Someone applied, paid, and got permission to do work. The work may even be done well. But the last step never happened: no final inspection, no final sign-off, or a failed inspection that was never fixed and checked again. In the city’s system, the job still looks unfinished. LegalClarity puts it the same way: an open permit is work that has not received final approval or inspection from the local building department.

This is not the same as unpermitted work. Unpermitted work was never filed at all. An open permit was filed. It just was never closed. Our guide on how to get a retroactive permit for work that is already done covers the never-filed case. If your open permit is in South Florida, our Florida guide on closing an open permit in Miami-Dade, Broward or Palm Beach County has the local fees for those counties.

Why does an open permit matter so much in a sale?

Because the problem moves with the house. If you sell with the permit still open, the buyer takes on the duty to close it after closing. LegalClarity warns that buyers who buy a house with open permits assume responsibility for closing them, with the future cost to finish the work or bring it up to code. Most buyers do not want a problem they did not create.

A title search that uncovers an open permit can temporarily halt the closing; lenders may delay or deny financing, and closing costs can rise

It bites in three places at once. The buyer hesitates, because they do not know what closing it will cost. The lender pauses, because uninspected work raises a safety and value question. And price follows: a buyer who stays will often ask for a lower price, a credit, or money held back until the permit is closed. HomeLight adds that an open permit does not always show up on a title report the way a lien does — finding it takes a separate look-up through the local government agency. That is why your own search in Step 1 matters.

How do I close an open permit before I sell my house, step by step?

Most cities use this same order. Forms and fees change by place.

Step 1: Search for open permits at your address. Start online. Many cities and counties let you search permits by address for free. Our guide on how to look up building permits by address for free walks through that search. If the online search is thin, call the building department and ask for a permit search by address. Write down every permit number that shows as open or expired, and what work it was for. Do this before you list.

Typically 2 to 4 weeks added to a traditional sale to close an open permit if the work passes inspection on the first try; 4 to 6 weeks on average if it does not pass the first time

Step 2: Ask the permit office what is missing, and get it in writing. For each open permit, ask three plain questions. What inspection or paper is still missing? Has the permit expired, or is it still active? Who has to call for the inspection — you, or the contractor named on the permit? First Financial Title Agency gives the same first move: contact the permit office and ask what is needed to close the permit; sometimes a simple inspection is enough.

Step 3: Call the original contractor first. HomeLight notes that contractors are usually responsible for opening and closing the permits for their work, and closing one out can be a quick fix with no added cost to you if it was only a recording miss. If that contractor is gone, you will need a new licensed contractor in the same trade, who may have to take over the permit or sign a change-of-contractor form. Ask about that step in Step 2.

Step 4: Check whether the permit has expired. An open permit and an expired permit are not the same job. Quick Home Offers notes most California departments set a time limit of about 6 to 12 months from the date the permit was issued. Georgetown, Texas, states the common rule in plain terms: a permit becomes invalid if work does not start within 180 days, or if work is stopped or abandoned for 180 days.

A permit becomes invalid if work is not started within 180 days of issuance, or if work is suspended or abandoned for 180 days after it started

If yours has expired, ask what reactivation needs. Violation Clinic says expired permits usually need an expiration fee and a re-inspection fee, and some places require a new permit application with the old permit number referenced. Pasco County, Florida, gives a concrete price on its reopen form: a $45 base fee plus $45 for each trade added to the permit, with the reopened permit running for 90 days. Your city will have its own number.

Step 5: Fix what will fail, before the inspector comes. Walk the work with your contractor before you book. Look for unfinished electrical or plumbing ends, no access to valves or panels, and work that was changed from the approved plans. If hidden work will need to be seen, your contractor can tell you where a small opening may be needed.

Step 6: Book and pass the final inspection. Book the inspection the way your city requires — online, by phone, or through your contractor. Make sure an adult can let the inspector in and the work is easy to reach. If the work meets code, the inspector signs off and the permit moves to closed. If it does not, get the correction list in writing, fix what is on that list, and book again.

Step 7: Get the closed record, and keep it with your sale papers. Do not stop at a verbal yes. Ask for the record that shows the permit as finaled, closed, or completed — a permit card copy, a printout, or a portal screen that shows the closed status and the date. Violation Clinic makes this its last step for a reason: that closed document is what you need for a sale, a refinance, or an insurance claim. Give a copy to your agent and to the title company.

What does it cost to close an open permit?

There are three honest cases. Any single national price would be made up.

The simple case can cost very little. If the work is done, the permit is still active, and it passes on the first visit, your cost may be only a re-inspection fee if your city charges one. HomeLight reports that often there are no added city costs, and the permit simply did not get recorded as closed.

The expired case adds city fees. Expect a reactivation fee and a re-inspection fee, as in the Pasco County example above at $45 plus $45 per trade. A permit expired for a long time can require a new application at full current fees.

The repair case costs real money. If the work fails, you pay a licensed contractor to fix it, and you may pay to open and patch a small part of a wall or floor so hidden work can be seen. No honest guide can price that before an inspector and a contractor have seen your house. Get a written quote after the correction list exists. Our guide on how much a building permit does cost shows normal permit fees, which helps you judge a quote.

What if you cannot close it before you list?

Sometimes you cannot. The contractor is gone, the fix is large, or your sale date will not wait. You still have honest paths, but none of them is hiding it.

First, disclose it in writing. LegalClarity is plain: most places require sellers to disclose all known material defects, including open permits, in writing, and failing to disclose can lead to legal action from the buyer. HomeLight says the same: do not conceal open permits and hope a buyer does not find them.

Second, price the problem or hold money back for it. LegalClarity lists the two normal tools: offer a price reduction that covers the buyer’s expected cost to close the permit, or use an escrow holdback, where part of the sale money is held until the buyer closes the permit after the sale. Put who does the work and the deadline in writing.

Third, know what this does to your buyer pool. LegalClarity warns that selling with a permit still open can limit your pool. A cash buyer has no lender to pause the deal, so a cash sale can still close with an open permit disclosed. A financed buyer may not have that choice if their lender requires the permit closed first. Say so early, in the listing papers, not at the closing table.

Frequently asked questions

How do I close an open permit before I sell my house?

Search your address with your building department, ask what inspection or paper is still missing, call the original contractor if you can, fix anything that will fail, book the final inspection, and after it passes, get the record that shows the permit as closed.

How long does it take to close an open permit?

Plan on 2 to 4 weeks if the work passes on the first inspection, and 4 to 6 weeks if it does not, based on a California seller guide’s experience. Your city’s queue and any repairs set the real pace, so start before you list.

How much does it cost to close an open permit?

A simple, active permit that passes can cost very little beyond any re-inspection fee your city charges. An expired permit adds a reopen fee — Pasco County, Florida, for example, charges a $45 base fee plus $45 per trade. A permit that fails costs what a licensed contractor charges to fix the work.

Can I sell a house if an open permit will not close in time?

Yes, if you disclose it in writing. You can offer a price reduction for the buyer’s cost to close it, or hold part of the sale money in escrow until the buyer closes it after the sale. Expect a smaller buyer pool, and know that some lenders will require the permit closed before they fund a loan.

What is the difference between an open permit and an expired permit?

An open permit was pulled and is still active, but its final inspection never happened. An expired permit has passed its time limit — often when work did not start, or stopped, for about 180 days — and usually must be reactivated or replaced before it can be closed.

Who is responsible for closing an open permit — me or my contractor?

Usually the contractor who pulled the permit is responsible for calling the final inspection, which is why you call them first. As the owner, though, the duty lands on you if they do not do it, and it passes to your buyer if you sell with it still open.

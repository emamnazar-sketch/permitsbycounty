How do I look up Chicago building permits and violations by address?

By PermitsByCounty Research Team · Published November 9, 2026 · Guides & Answers

QUICK ANSWER

Use the City of Chicago Department of Buildings search first. Its Building Permit and Inspection Records tool looks up one address for free, with no account, and returns the building permits, inspections, enforcement cases, and alleged violations tied to that address. For bulk or older records, use the Chicago Data Portal, where the Building Permits and Building Violations datasets both run from 2006 to the present and update daily. If the property sits outside the city in unincorporated Cook County, search Cook County instead — the county system does not hold City of Chicago records, and the city system does not hold county ones. Sources checked November 9, 2026.

TL;DR — the whole post in 20 seconds

  • Start with the city’s address search. One address in, and you get permits, inspections, cases, and violations in one result — free, no login.
  • Type the address the city’s way: short postal forms, no periods. A spelled-out street name can return nothing and look like a clean record.
  • Use the Data Portal for depth. Permits and violations both go back to 2006 and update daily, but the city warns the violation data is historical and is not a substitute for a title search at closing.
  • Check which government holds the record before you trust a blank result. Chicago is the city. Unincorporated Cook County is the county. A search in the wrong system proves nothing.
  • A permit on the record does not prove the work was done, or done right. The city says so itself, in plain words, on the search page.

Which search do you start with for a Chicago address?

The Department of Buildings one. Go to the Department of Buildings homepage, open Records and Data, and pick Building Permit and Inspection Records. Accept the user agreement, and the search opens. No account or login is required for public searches.

Type one address. If the city holds a record for it, the result comes back in sections: the input address and, for larger buildings, a range address; building facts like stories, porch, basement, and lot size; then building permits with number, date issued, and description of work; enforcement case activity; inspections with number, date, status, and type; and alleged violations with type, code citation, and details.

That one page is your whole first check, so read all of it, not just the top. The permit list shows what work got approved. Keep going: the inspection lines tell you whether the city ever came out to look, and the violation lines tell you what an inspector found wrong on the day. A buyer who stops at the permits has read a third of the story.

How do you type the address so the search actually finds it?

The city’s way, not the postal way you use on an envelope. The Department of Buildings guide is specific: use standard postal abbreviations, without periods, for directionals and street types. Its own example is “1234 S Building Ave” — not “1234 South State Street,” and not “1234 S. State St.”

Enter addresses using standard postal abbreviations without periods for directionals and street types — for example, “1234 S Building Ave,” not “South” spelled out and not “S.” with a period

This sounds small. It is the most common way this search goes wrong. A blank result can mean a clean property. It can also mean you typed the address in a form the system did not match. Before you call a property clean, run the address a second way — short directional, no periods — and check the range address. Big buildings file under ranges, and one unit’s work can sit under the range, not the exact door you typed.

Try the parcel number too. The city’s violation lookup accepts an address or a PIN, and a PIN search cuts through spelling and range problems in one step.

What does each part of the result actually tell you?

Start with the permits. Each one carries a number, an issue date, and a short description of the work. Match that list against what you can see in the building. A finished basement, a new deck, a converted attic, a replaced roof — each of those should have a permit line that sounds like it. Work you can see with no matching permit is the first flag, and our guide to checking a house’s permit history before you buy walks through how to work that flag.

Then the inspections. Inspection numbers are links — open them. Each carries a date, a status, and a type, and the detail page lists the violations found on that visit, with the code citation for each. A permit with no inspection after it is an open thread: work was approved, but the city may never have come back to pass it.

Then the violations. Each code starts with letters naming the bureau that wrote it — EV elevator, EL electrical, PL plumbing, RF refrigeration, VT ventilation, ES signs, IR iron, BR boiler, FP fire prevention, routed to the Fire Department. The letters tell you the kind of problem before you read the detail. A page of CN conservation items on an old building is wear and upkeep. An open EL or PL item is a system that failed an inspector and was never shown fixed.

Read the status, not just the charge. Every violation carries a status and a status date — OPEN means exactly what it says, COMPLIED means the city recorded the fix. An old complied violation is history. An old open one is a bill and a job that belong to whoever owns the building. That is you, the day after closing.

Where do you check on a permit that has not been issued yet?

That’s a different city tool. The Building Permit Application Status site tracks applications while they’re still in review. To use it fully, you search by the application number — for most applications, 9 digits beginning with 1. Some run 10 characters and begin with B2, and applications in the Traditional or Assisted Developer Services program begin with D and contain a hyphen.

The application status site holds applications started during the last 3 years (36 months); information about older applications and issued permits may not appear, and only limited information is shown for a search by street address

The status site’s limits — City of Chicago, Illinois · Application status help page, checked November 9, 2026

The two tools split the timeline. Issued permits, inspections, and violations live in the records search. A pending application lives on the status site, and only for three years back. If a seller says a permit is “in process,” ask for the application number and check it there. If it is older than three years and nothing shows, ask the buildings department directly — silence there is not proof either way.

What does the Chicago Data Portal add?

Depth — and a way to check more than one address at once. The Data Portal carries the same records as open datasets. Building Permits holds permits issued by the Department of Buildings from 2006 to the present, updated daily, and it leaves out permits that were voided or revoked after issuance. Each year of it holds more than 65,000 records, which is why the city warns it will not open in full in a spreadsheet.

Building permits issued by the Department of Buildings in the City of Chicago from January 1, 2006 to the present, updated daily, at more than 65,000 records per year

Building Violations is the matching set. It holds violations issued by the Department of Buildings from 2006 to the present, also updated daily. Every violation is tied to an inspection, and one inspection can carry several violation records — so count inspections when you judge how troubled a building is, not raw violation lines, or one bad visit can look like five separate problems.

Violations issued by the Department of Buildings from 2006 to the present, updated daily; violations are always associated with an inspection, and multiple violation records can attach to one inspection

The Portal also lets you filter before you download — by address, street, permit type, or date. For an investor screening a block, that is the working tool. For a buyer checking one house before an offer, the address search is faster and reads easier.

One paid supplement exists too. Chicago Cityscape offers address lookups on the same public records, with permits since 2006 and violations since 2001 — five extra years of violations past the Portal’s start. Use it only when the free record leaves a gap you need filled.

What does the city itself warn you not to assume?

Read the disclaimer on the records search before you rely on it — the city spells out three warnings there, and they’re worth taking seriously. First, what’s on the page is for information only, and it may not match the building as it stands today. Second, an issued permit doesn’t confirm the work was done, or done to code. And third, no violations on the site doesn’t mean the building complies with the Municipal Code. A violation entry only shows what an inspector found on that one day, and things may have changed since.

“The fact that a permit was issued does not confirm that work was performed, or that work was performed in accordance with that permit and the requirements of the Municipal Code”

A permit is not proof the work happened — City of Chicago, Illinois · Records search disclaimer, checked November 9, 2026

Source: City of Chicago Department of Buildings, Building Permit and Inspection Records · Date pulled: November 9, 2026

The violation dataset adds a warning aimed at closings. The city tells lenders and title companies the data is historical and should not be relied on for a transaction — for a closing, check the title commitment for enforcement actions in the Circuit Court of Cook County or the Department of Administrative Hearings. Some violations get referred into court or hearings, and those proceedings live in those records, not in the address search.

So use the free search as a fast first pass that finds most problems early, while you can still walk away or price them in. Then let the title search and your attorney confirm the legal state of what you found.

When is the record not Chicago’s at all?

More often than buyers expect. Cook County is not one permit office. It holds more than 130 municipalities plus unincorporated land, and each incorporated city or village keeps its own permits. Chicago’s system is fully separate from the county’s.

Cook County Building and Zoning covers unincorporated Cook County only. Its e-permits page says so at the top: verify the property is in unincorporated Cook County first, because permits cannot be processed for properties in the City of Chicago or inside suburban villages. Its CookViewer map shows which side of the line an address falls on.

Cook County e-permits are for residents and businesses in unincorporated Cook County only; permits cannot be processed for properties located in the City of Chicago or within the incorporated boundaries of suburban cities and villages

The county’s own boundary — Cook County, Illinois · E-permits service page, checked November 9, 2026

Source: Cook County Department of Building and Zoning, Building and Zoning E-Permits · Date pulled: November 9, 2026

For an unincorporated property, the county runs on different keys. Its status search asks for the Property Index Number and the permit number, and its e-permits cover a set list — deck, driveway, fence, flatwork, generator, masonry wall, pool, re-roof, shed or detached garage, signs, sump pump, tent, and window replacement. A job mixing two or more types goes in by email to county intake instead.

Build one habit. Before any Chicago-area lookup, settle the jurisdiction in one minute: city, suburb, or unincorporated county. A blank result from the wrong system is worse than no search at all, because it feels like an answer. Search the office that actually issued the permit.

What if the record you need is older, or on paper?

Go the FOIA route. The Freedom of Information Act is the state law behind public access to these records, and each city department answers for its own files — so a buildings record goes to the Department of Buildings FOIA officer. The city’s 311 FOIA page links the three free online lookups first and takes the formal request for anything they do not cover.

Older permits sit elsewhere. The University of Illinois Chicago Library holds the city’s permit collection from 1872 to 1954 — ledger books and a street index, no building plans — and points you to the Data Portal for 2006 forward. The years between usually take the buildings department and a FOIA request, and longer than an online search. Price that time into a purchase on an older building instead of assuming the online record is the whole record.

A plain routine for checking one Chicago property

Settle the jurisdiction first. Then run the city’s records search with the address typed the short way, no periods, and note the range address it returns.

List the permits and match them to the building. Every visible remodel, addition, deck, porch, and system replacement should have a line. Open the inspection links on the permits that matter, then read the violations whole — code letters, detail, and status — and write down anything still open.

Cross-check in the Data Portal if anything looks thin, and use the status site, with the application number, for any permit the seller claims is pending. Ask for anything open to be priced, fixed, or held in escrow before closing — never after. Keep the city’s warning on top of your notes: a found permit is not proof of finished work, and a clean search is a first pass, not a guarantee. Frequently asked questions

How do I look up Chicago building permits and violations by address?

Start on the Department of Buildings site: Records and Data, then Building Permit and Inspection Records. Accept the agreement and enter the address in short postal form, with no periods. One free search, no account, returns the permits, inspections, enforcement cases, and alleged violations for that address. For bulk or older records, use the Chicago Data Portal datasets.

Why does my Chicago permit search return nothing?

Usually the address form. The city wants standard postal abbreviations without periods — “S” not “South,” and no period after it. Run it again the short way, check the range address for larger buildings, and try the PIN. Also confirm the property is actually in Chicago: unincorporated Cook County and suburban records live in other systems, and a search in the wrong one returns nothing even for a busy property.

How far back do Chicago permit and violation records go online?

On the Chicago Data Portal, both the Building Permits and Building Violations datasets run from 2006 to the present and update daily. The city’s permit application status site only holds applications started in the last 3 years. For much older permits, the UIC Library holds the city’s permit collection from 1872 to 1954, and the years between usually take a Department of Buildings FOIA request.

Does Cook County hold Chicago building permits?

No. Chicago’s Department of Buildings holds city records in its own system. Cook County Building and Zoning covers unincorporated Cook County only — its e-permits page states that permits cannot be processed for City of Chicago properties or for properties inside suburban cities and villages. Check the jurisdiction, with a tool like the county’s CookViewer map, before you search.

If a Chicago search shows no violations, is the building in the clear?

Not on that evidence alone. The city states that the absence of violations on the site does not mean a building complies with the Municipal Code, and that violation records show what an inspector found on that inspection day. The violation dataset also warns that it is historical and should not be relied on for a real estate transaction — closings should check the title commitment for court and administrative-hearing actions.

What should I do if I find an open violation on a house I want to buy?

Treat it as a cost that transfers with the building. Get the violation detail and status, ask the seller to fix it, credit it, or hold funds in escrow before closing, and have your attorney confirm the enforcement state through the title search. An open violation does not have to kill a deal, but it should change the price or the paperwork — before you own it, not after.

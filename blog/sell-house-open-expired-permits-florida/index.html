Can I sell a house with open or expired permits in Florida?

By PermitsByCounty Research Team · Published November 8, 2026 · Guides & Answers

QUICK ANSWER

Yes. Florida law does not stop you from selling a house because a permit is still open or has expired. The sale itself is legal. What stops deals is the machinery around it: most title companies will not issue a clean policy over an open permit, most lenders will not fund over one, and the standard Florida contract makes the seller close open and expired permits before closing, up to a set dollar limit. So you can sell — the real question is whether you close the permit first, hand it to the buyer at a lower price, or lose the buyer when it surfaces late. Sources checked November 8, 2026.

TL;DR — the whole post in 20 seconds

  • The law allows the sale. No Florida rule bars you from selling a house with an open or expired permit on it.
  • Title, lender, and contract decide what happens. All three push toward closing the permit first.
  • On the standard FAR/BAR contract the seller closes open and expired permits up to the Permit Limit, due 5 days before closing. On the AS IS form, that job shifts to the buyer.
  • Most sellers find out late — open permits usually surface in the title search, about 10 to 14 days before closing. Checking before you list turns a crisis into a scheduled job.
  • Florida gives you real ways out: the original or a new contractor can finish the file, a substantially completed expired permit can close under its original code, and a department may close a permit 6 years after issuance if it sees no apparent safety hazard.

What is the difference between an open permit and an expired permit?

An open permit was issued, and the work may even be finished, but nobody ever got the final inspection that closes it. A roof can be sound and a water heater can run for years while the permit behind the job still sits open in the building department's records.

An expired permit ran out of time. Under the Florida Building Code, a permit goes invalid if work does not start within 180 days of issuance, or if work stops for 180 days after it began. An expired permit is usually open too, because it was never closed out either.

A permit becomes invalid if work does not commence within 180 days of issuance, or is suspended or abandoned for 180 days after work has begun

Neither kind clears itself. Open permits stay in the public database for anyone who searches the address, including permits from work done decades ago — which is why a past owner's permit can still land on your closing table.

An open permit is not unpermitted work, either. Unpermitted work never had a permit at all, and fixing it can mean drawings and opening finished walls. An open permit has a file and an approved scope — and the cheaper fix.

Does Florida law stop the sale?

No. Nothing in Florida law says a house with an open or expired permit cannot be sold — and the state has made the position easier in the last few years.

In 2019, Florida changed section 553.79 of the statutes. A local enforcement agency may not deny a building permit to, issue a notice of violation to, or fine or penalize a buyer who bought the property in a normal arm's-length sale, solely because a permit the previous owner applied for was never closed. Before that change, an old open permit could block the new owner from pulling any new permit at all.

Since the 2019 change to Florida Statute 553.79, a local agency may not deny a permit to, or fine or penalize, an arm's-length purchaser solely because a previous owner's permit was never closed

The arm's-length buyer protection — Florida · Law in effect from 2019, checked November 8, 2026

Read the limit of that protection carefully. It stops the building department from punishing the new buyer for the old file. It does not declare the work safe, and it does not bind a lender, an insurer, or an appraiser. The law clears one obstacle out of four.

So why do deals still stall?

Because a sale is not just you and a buyer. A title company has to insure the title, and often a lender has to fund the loan. Both read an open permit as unfinished business.

A title company or closing attorney searching the property will find the open permit — sometimes one from work done decades earlier — and has to decide whether it can issue a clear title commitment. An open permit works like a title defect in practice: the work behind it was never confirmed to meet code, so most title companies will not issue a clean policy over one. Some will issue a policy with the permit written in as an exception. Many will not, and most lenders will not approve financing over an unresolved title issue like that.

For the buyer, the risk is real: if the work was never inspected and later fails, the buyer owns the correction bill. For you as the seller, the problem is mostly time. Closing an old permit can mean finding the original documents, hiring a contractor or engineer, scheduling an inspection, and waiting in the building department's queue. None of that fits well into the last two weeks of a contract.

What does your Florida contract say about it?

In Florida, the contract often answers the question first. It depends which form you signed.

On the standard Florida Realtors / Florida Bar contract, open and expired permits are the seller's job. The seller closes them, up to a dollar cap called the Permit Limit, by a deadline 5 days before closing. Left blank, that limit is 1.5 percent of the price. A government delay can extend closing up to 10 days; after that, either side may cancel.

On the standard FAR/BAR contract (Rev. 2/26), the seller closes open and expired permits up to the Permit Limit, due 5 days before the Closing Date; a government delay extends closing up to 10 days, after which either side may cancel

On the AS IS form, the same item flips. Open permits become the buyer's problem; the seller cooperates but does not have to spend money closing them. The buyer still has an inspection period to cancel in and a lender who may refuse — but the duty to fix sits in a different chair.

When do sellers usually find out?

Late — and that timing is the problem. Open permits typically appear during the title search, about 10 to 14 days before closing. South Florida Agent Magazine put it flatly in August 2026: eleven days before closing, the title company sends an email with the subject line "issue found" — a water heater replaced in 2019, permit pulled, never finaled, nobody knew it was there.

Open permits typically appear during the title search, 10 to 14 days before closing

Found at listing, the same permit is a few phone calls and an inspection appointment. Found 11 days out, it is a rushed close-out in front of a buyer who can walk and a lender who can pause. Run the address before you list, and you get weeks instead of days.

One more trap: a normal title search can come back clean while the building department still shows unfinished work, because an open permit is generally not a recorded document. The search that catches permits is the municipal lien search — ask your closing agent early whether one is included in your sale.

What do you have to tell the buyer?

What you know. Florida is not a buyer-beware state for home sales. In Johnson v. Davis, the Florida Supreme Court held that a seller who knows of facts that materially affect a home's value, and knows the buyer cannot readily see them, has to disclose those facts. A known open permit on an addition, a roof, or a system fits that description in many cases. Brokers carry their own duty under Florida Statute 475.278 to disclose known facts that materially affect value.

The duty covers what you know. A seller who honestly never knew a past owner left a permit open is in a different spot from one who got the title company's email and stayed quiet. An AS IS contract does not erase the duty either.

Once you run your own pre-listing search, you know, and what you know goes on the disclosure. Sellers worry that disclosing scares buyers off. What scares buyers off is finding a permit themselves, late, with no plan and no price attached. A known permit with a quote and a closing plan reads as one scheduled job. A discovered permit reads as a house with secrets.

What are your real options as a seller?

You have four. They are cheaper the earlier you pick one.

First, close the permit before closing — before listing, if you can. This is the cleanest path when the work is done and sound. The original contractor, if still licensed and in business, can often schedule the final inspection. If that contractor is gone, Florida law lets you hire a different licensed contractor to take the file over, responsible only for their own work.

Second, give a credit or cut the price, with the fix priced by someone who does this work. A credit built on a guess usually falls short and gets argued about twice.

Third, use an escrow holdback. Money from the sale sits with the closing agent to pay for the closure or correction work, with the amount, scope, and release terms written down. This keeps a deal moving when the inspection calendar will not fit before closing.

Fourth, sell to a buyer who will take it as it stands — often a cash buyer — at a price that admits the problem, disclosed in writing. What doesn't work is hoping nobody searches. Somebody searches.

How do you close an open or expired permit?

Florida law gives owners more routes than most states do, in section 553.79 and section 105.5 of the Florida Building Code. The original contractor can finish the file: schedule the missing inspections, pass them, and close the permit. A new contractor can take over in their place. And if an expired permit's work was substantially completed, it may be closed without a new permit, under the code in effect when it was first applied for — not today's code. On older work, that can be the difference between an inspection and a redesign.

If a building permit is expired and its requirements have been substantially completed, as determined by the local enforcement agency, the permit may be closed without obtaining a new building permit, under the code in effect when the application was received

Closing a substantially completed permit — Florida · 2023 Florida Building Code, Residential, section 105.5, checked November 8, 2026

There is also a route for genuinely old files. A local agency may close a permit 6 years after it was issued, even without a final inspection, if it decides no apparent safety hazard exists. That's the agency's choice, not a right you can demand, and higher-risk work can still need a real inspection. Ask — the worst answer is that you close it the regular way.

A local enforcement agency may close a building permit 6 years after issuance, even in the absence of a final inspection, if it determines that no apparent safety hazard exists

How do you check your own house before you list?

Start with the building department that actually issued permits for your address — usually the city for homes inside city limits. A mailing address can point you at the wrong counter: a house with a city address can sit in unincorporated county territory, and each side keeps separate records. Confirm the jurisdiction first, then search that portal by address and read the status of each permit, hunting for anything open, active, expired, or missing a final inspection.

If you want the county itself to certify the answer, Palm Beach County's building department runs certified searches for unincorporated county properties, priced below. A house inside a city goes to that city's counter instead.

Then compare the permit list against the house. Every addition, enclosure, pool, and converted space should match a permit that ended closed or finaled. A room with no permit at all is unpermitted work — a bigger conversation, covered in our post on selling a house with unpermitted work. For an open permit, write down the permit number, what it covered, and who pulled it. Everyone you call will ask for that page.

What happens if the buyer takes the house with the permit still open?

It can be done, and sometimes it's the right deal for both sides. Just be clear about what transfers. The permit stays attached to the property, not to you, so the buyer takes over closing it — inspections, corrections, and the cost if the work fails. The buyer gets the 2019 protection, but their lender, insurer, and appraiser were never bound by it. Neither is their own future buyer.

Get the transfer in writing: the permit disclosed, the price or credit stated, the responsibility named. A handshake on an open permit is how a closed sale turns into a dispute a year later.

Frequently asked questions

Can I sell a house with open or expired permits in Florida?

Yes. Florida law does not bar the sale. The problems are practical: most title companies will not issue a clean policy over an open permit, most lenders will not fund over one, and the standard contract makes the seller close open and expired permits before closing, up to the Permit Limit.

Will an open permit stop my closing?

It can delay it, and it can kill it if it surfaces late and cannot be fixed inside the contract's deadlines. On the standard FAR/BAR contract the seller must close open and expired permits 5 days before closing, with up to 10 extra days for a government delay before either side may cancel. Found before listing, it is usually just a scheduled inspection.

Who has to close an open permit when a Florida house is sold — the seller or the buyer?

The contract decides. On the standard form, the seller closes them up to the Permit Limit. On the AS IS form, the buyer takes them and the seller only cooperates. The department's record points at whoever pulled the permit, which is why a seller often ends up hiring a contractor to finish the file either way.

Does an open permit mean the work was done badly?

No. It means the work never got its final inspection. Plenty of open permits sit behind sound work and a missed appointment. Until an inspector passes it, though, nobody official has confirmed it meets code — and that gap is what title companies react to.

My permit expired years ago. Can it still be closed?

Usually, yes. Florida law lets the original or a new licensed contractor finish the file, lets a substantially completed expired permit close under the code from when it was applied for, and lets a department close a permit 6 years after issuance if it finds no apparent safety hazard. The department decides which route fits.

If I disclose the open permit and the buyer agrees, is that the end of it?

For you, mostly — if the agreement is written into the contract with the price, credit, or holdback stated. The permit still lives with the property until somebody closes it, so the buyer inherits the job, with the 2019 protection behind them.

What permits do I need for a commercial tenant improvement in Dallas?

By PermitsByCounty Research Team · Published November 6, 2026 · Guides & Answers

QUICK ANSWER

For Dallas tenant improvement permits, plan on three filings that work as one job: a master building permit for the finish-out itself, separate trade permits for electrical, plumbing, and mechanical work, and a certificate of occupancy for the tenant who will use the space. You file in DallasNow, the city's portal since May 5, 2025. The city aims for a first commercial review in about 15 business days, and the finish-out permit itself runs about $500 to $5,000 or more by project value, before the $375 certificate of occupancy fee.

TL;DR — the whole post in 20 seconds

  • A tenant improvement in Dallas is filed as a commercial finish-out, remodel, or renovation. One master permit covers the build.
  • Trade work needs its own permits. Electrical, plumbing, and mechanical are each pulled by a licensed contractor registered with the city.
  • A certificate of occupancy is separate. It doesn’t let you build anything, and a new tenant or a change of use needs one.
  • File in DallasNow. Budget about 15 business days for a first review, 7 for a resubmittal, and 8 to 16 weeks in total if corrections pile up.
  • The checklist is where jobs stall: full plans, an asbestos survey note, accessibility proof, and energy and green building papers belong in the first filing.

What counts as a tenant improvement in Dallas?

A tenant improvement is the work that turns a leased shell or an old suite into your space. New walls, moved walls, a new ceiling, new lighting, added plumbing, a bigger panel, ductwork that moves — that is a finish-out in Dallas terms.

The city files this work under its commercial building finish-out, remodel, and renovations checklist. Use that checklist when major sections of structural walls are replaced, or when electrical or plumbing work is part of the job. Paint and floor finishes alone, with no wall, system, or layout change, may not need a building permit. Ask before you assume. A call to the permit center at (214) 948-4480 costs less than tearing out work the city never approved.

All new work has to meet the current Dallas construction codes, even inside an old building. The age of the shell doesn’t cover the new work inside it.

What permits do I need for a commercial tenant improvement in Dallas?

Start with the master permit, then add the pieces your scope touches.

The master building permit is the main record. A contractor, an owner, or their agent can apply, but a registered contractor has to be named on the application before the city will issue it. Name the contractor early. That one rule stops a lot of tenant-filed jobs at the counter.

Trade permits sit under the master permit. Electrical, plumbing, and mechanical work each get their own record and their own inspections. In the city's guide, a commercial electrical permit is pulled by a registered master electrician, a plumbing permit by a registered master plumber, and a mechanical permit by a registered air conditioning contractor. Each trade record has to link to the primary record number exactly, character for character. Get that number from the general contractor in writing. If the link is wrong, the inspection does not count toward the main job.

Fire work can add more records. If your layout changes sprinkler heads, alarms, or a kitchen hood system, expect a fire review and, for a hood, a separate fire-extinguishing system permit. Restaurants, retail, and medical spaces pull in added reviewers because the use itself carries health and life-safety rules on top of the building rules.

Tenant finish-outs need plan review for structural, electrical, plumbing, and mechanical changes inside an existing commercial space, and commercial projects are reviewed by building, fire, planning, public works, and health reviewers at the same time

Permit Guru, a Dallas expediter that handles tenant finish-outs every week, puts it the same way: finishing a leased space in Dallas usually needs a permit that covers interior construction plus the mechanical, electrical, and plumbing work, managed from plan coordination through issuance.

The certificate of occupancy is a separate filing

Don’t treat the certificate of occupancy, called a CO, as the last stamp on your building permit. It is its own application, its own fee, and its own review.

A CO says the space is approved for the use listed on it. Dallas needs one before a new tenant occupies a space, and again when the use changes — retail to restaurant, office to medical, and so on. The city is clear on one limit: a certificate of occupancy does not authorize construction of any type. You can’t build on a CO, and you can’t open on a building permit alone if your CO is still pending.

For a change of use, the CO filing asks for more than a form. The city's checklist calls for a site plan of the whole property, drawn to scale, showing every tenant space and its suite number, a floor plan of your space with each room named by use, and a full parking count for every tenant on the property. If there is no record that a permit was ever issued to create your tenant space, the city can require a building permit for that reason alone. Settle who chases that old record before the lease clock starts.

Same use, new tenant is the lighter path. Change of use is the heavier one. Name which path you are on in the first week, because the plan set is not the same.

How do you file in DallasNow?

Dallas moved permitting to DallasNow on May 5, 2025. The new system replaced the old Posse system and pulled about 15 separate systems — permitting, planning, inspections, engineering, and others — into one portal. You apply there, upload plans, pay fees, track the review, and schedule inspections in the same account.

File the finish-out under Commercial Permits as a commercial alteration or finish-out, and attach the building permit application, the fee estimate worksheet, the commercial remodel checklist, and the CO checklist and application where your job needs them. The permit center at the Oak Cliff Municipal Center, 320 E. Jefferson Boulevard, Room 118, still helps in person.

One field is worth a second look before anyone hits submit. When a trade contractor adds a record to your primary permit, marking it as related and typing the primary number exactly is what links its inspections to your job.

What belongs in the plan set?

The finish-out checklist is long because the city checks more than walls. Read it as four bundles.

First, the drawings. Clear, fully dimensioned plans, drawn to a standard scale: a site plan, a floor plan, and the building plans for the work itself, with fire sprinkler status and what you plan to do about it, plus parking counts where the use needs them. For a change of use, the site plan covers the entire property, not just your suite.

Second, the outside approvals the city will not do for you. An asbestos survey for the area being renovated or demolished, done under the Texas Asbestos Health Protection Rules and the federal NESHAP rules. The permit cannot issue before the city gets notice that the survey was done. Proof that your plans went to the Texas Department of Licensing and Regulation for Texas Accessibility Standards review by a Registered Accessibility Specialist, with the TDLR project number on the application.

No permit issues before notice of an asbestos survey for the renovated area, and the application needs proof of Texas Accessibility Standards filing with a TDLR project number

Source: City of Dallas, Commercial Building Finish-Out, Remodel and Renovations Checklist · Date pulled: November 6, 2026

Third, energy and green building. For a first-time finish-out, the checklist asks for a green building compliance affidavit from a registered third-party provider, and an Energy Code Compliance Path form, filed with the application. An Energy Code Compliance Certificate is due before the final inspection for every permit.

Fourth, the small print. Any recorded restriction on the property, filed where one exists, and a statement that the work follows the federal lead renovation rule, 40 CFR Part 745, where it applies.

Missing one paper doesn’t cost a day. It costs a correction round.

How long does a Dallas tenant improvement take?

Use two numbers. The first review is the city's first answer. The total is the day the permit issues.

The city's posted goal for a commercial alteration is a first review in about 15 business days and a resubmittal review in 7. PermitPlace's Dallas guide is wider — 10 to 25 business days for a first commercial review, up to 10 business days of processing before that review starts, and 5 to 25 business days for each correction round. Most commercial jobs go through two or three correction rounds, for a total of 8 to 16 weeks from application to an issued permit.

If the date matters, Dallas sells a faster lane. The Q-Team gives expedited plan review by appointment, with an intake fee of $500 for a project under 10,000 square feet and review billed at $1,000 an hour on top of the standard fees. Book it at the start. It does not rescue a file that already sat through a slow first round.

Food jobs carry a second clock. A food establishment plan review by the health team carries a $200 fee and the same 15-business-day first-review goal. A restaurant tenant who files only the building permit has filed half the job.

How much does a Dallas tenant improvement permit cost?

The city prices the finish-out by the value of the work, so there is no single sticker price. PermitPlace puts a commercial tenant improvement at about $500 to $5,000 or more by valuation, with a $33.10 administrative fee on any permit over $50. A fee summary built on the city's current schedule gives the shape under Ordinance 33080, effective July 1, 2025: a commercial remodel carries a $175 minimum, and plan review runs $0.046 per square foot with a $577 minimum, plus a $15 technology fee and a $25 document handling fee.

Add the CO at $375. Add trade inspection fees where they apply — the city's trade guide puts the inspection fee at $125 per trade and a reinspection fee at $75 per trade if work fails. Add any fire permit your scope triggers, and the Q-Team only if you choose it. Then treat the number in DallasNow as the final one. The portal shows the live fee due before you pay, and a 10 percent surcharge can apply in planned development, specific use permit, and deed-restricted areas.

Don’t mix up permit fees with the build-out itself. One Dallas builder put typical retail work at $150 to $185 per square foot in January 2026, with tenant improvement allowances in Class A space at $30 to $50 per square foot. The permit is the small number in that budget. The delay is the expensive one.

The order that keeps a Dallas finish-out moving

One, confirm the use is allowed before you draw anything. Dallas is a fully zoned city. Two, decide the CO path — same use, new tenant, or a change of use — and start the site plan and parking work it needs. Three, line up the registered contractor and the licensed trade contractors. Four, order the asbestos survey and start the accessibility filing early. Five, file the master permit and all checklist papers together in DallasNow, then have each trade add its record with the exact primary number. Six, answer corrections as one package, build only what the issued permit covers, and close with final inspections, the energy certificate, and the CO.

Mistakes that cost Dallas tenants weeks

Filing a CO and calling it a build-out approval. It’s not. Starting walls before the master permit issues because the last tenant did the same thing. That history isn’t your permit. Filing thin — no asbestos note, no accessibility proof, no green building affidavit — so each gap comes back as its own correction round. Typing a wrong primary record number, so a trade inspection lands nowhere. And forgetting the use-specific reviewers: a medical suite, a restaurant, and a plain office in the same shell do not face the same review table.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a permit for a commercial tenant improvement in Dallas?

Yes, for almost any real finish-out. If the work touches walls, structure, electrical, plumbing, mechanical systems, or the layout, Dallas wants a master building permit plus trade permits. Paint and floor finishes alone may not need one. Confirm your scope with the permit center first.

Is a certificate of occupancy the same as a building permit in Dallas?

No. They are separate filings with separate fees. The building permit lets you build. The certificate of occupancy, $375 for a general CO, says the finished space is approved for the tenant and use named on it. A CO does not authorize construction of any type.

Who can apply for a Dallas tenant finish-out permit?

A contractor, the owner, or their agent can apply. The permit will not issue unless a registered contractor is named on the application. Only a licensed contractor registered with the city for that trade can pull each trade record.

How long does a Dallas tenant improvement permit take?

Plan on about 15 business days for a first review, and 7 for a resubmittal. With two or three correction rounds, PermitPlace puts the full run at 8 to 16 weeks to an issued permit. A food use adds a health review on its own clock.

How much does a tenant improvement permit cost in Dallas?

By project value. PermitPlace puts a tenant improvement at about $500 to $5,000 or more, and the city schedule carries a $175 minimum with plan review at $0.046 per square foot and a $577 minimum. Add the $375 CO and any fire permits your scope triggers.

Can I track Dallas tenant permits in a live public list?

Not in the old open-data feed. That set ends in 2020. Dallas work is now tracked inside DallasNow, and the city publishes hand-run reports by council district. PermitsByCounty publishes no Dallas counts from the dead feed as if they were current.

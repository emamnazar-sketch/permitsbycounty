Why did Dallas County's permit feed stop updating — and where can DFW subs track Dallas permits instead?

By PermitsByCounty Research Team · Published November 5, 2026 · Guides & Answers

QUICK ANSWER

The Dallas building permits search feed on the city's open-data portal stopped adding records after 2020. We checked the dataset ourselves on October 18, 2026: it holds 126,840 rows, and every year from 2021 through 2026 counts zero. The city never gave a reason in the sources we checked, so we won't guess at one. Dallas didn't stop issuing permits — only the feed stopped. What works today is DallasNow, the city's permit system that launched May 5, 2025. Its public portal lets anyone pull reports of issued, submitted, or active building permits by council district — no sign-in — and download them as a spreadsheet.

TL;DR — the whole post in 20 seconds

  • The old Dallas open-data feed is an archive, not a live feed: 126,840 rows, none after 2020.
  • You may have seen 2024 given as the year the feed stopped. It's wrong. We counted the records ourselves: 2020 was the last year with any.
  • None of the public sources we checked says why it stopped. If someone gives you a firm reason, they're guessing.
  • Today you track Dallas in DallasNow: no sign-in, pick a council district, pull issued, submitted, or active permits, and download the Excel file.
  • The rest of DFW is separate homework: Fort Worth permits are live on PermitsByCounty, and every other Dallas County city runs its own system.

What actually stopped — the feed, not the work

Start with the proof. There's a lot of bad advice floating around on this one. The dataset is the City of Dallas "Building Permits" set on its Socrata open-data portal. It was a good feed while it lived. It had the fields a sub actually wants: issue date, job value, permit type, ZIP code, and the contractor's name.

On October 18, 2026, we pulled that dataset ourselves and counted the rows by year. The total was 126,840 rows. The year 2019 held 52,037 of them, and 2020 held 27,459. Then there's nothing: 2021, zero. 2022, zero. Same for 2023, 2024, 2025, and 2026. A companion city dataset of permit points was older still.

So open that feed today and sort by date: the newest Dallas permit you'll ever see is from 2020. Your search isn't broken. There's just nothing newer in there to find.

Dallas itself kept building through all of those years. The city kept issuing permits, inspecting work, and publishing its own building statistics. What ended was one pipe: the automatic flow of permit records into the public open-data set that subs, lead sellers, and sites like ours used to read every morning.

That difference matters, and it can cost you. A stopped feed can make a busy market look dead. If your lead routine still starts with that dataset, you've been reading a five-year-old market and calling it this week.

Why did it stop? What is known, and what is not

Honest answer: we don't know why, and nobody else writing about this in public knows either. We checked our audit record, the city's own guides, and the permit services that cover Texas. Not one of them points to a city statement with a reason. So we won't invent one for you. Feeds die for dull reasons — a system change, a staffer leaving, a contract ending, a quiet decision nobody announced. Picking one would be a story, not a fact.

One claim needs correcting, though, because you'll run into it. Tradewind, a paid service that sells Texas permit leads, says it leaves Dallas out because the county's open-data permits stopped refreshing in 2024. The thrust of it is right: the feed is dead, and Tradewind really does leave Dallas out. But the date is wrong. The direct year counts above show records ending after 2020, years before 2024. If a seller's public facts are off by four years on the one thing you can check yourself, check everything else they tell you too.

There's a second date people mix into this story. On May 5, 2025, Dallas launched DallasNow, a new permit system built on Accela that replaced the old Posse system. That launch is real — it's why the tracking route below works at all. But it came five years after the feed stopped, so it can't be why the feed stopped. Keep the two events separate: the feed died around 2020 with no published reason, and the city's permit office later rebuilt its systems for its own work.

How to run a Dallas building permits search today in DallasNow

The city's replacement lives inside DallasNow, and it's a report tool, not a feed. The steps below follow the city's own guide. You don't need an account to run one.

Start at the address the city publishes, dallas.gov/dallasnow, and open the DallasNow Citizen Portal. Pick Building on the navigation bar — building permits are the record you want. Then open Reports and pick the report that fits your question: all active building permits in progress, permits issued during a time frame, or permits submitted during a time frame. Narrow it down if you want: you can run one council district or all districts at once. Set your dates — a permit only shows up if it was issued or submitted inside that range, and wider ranges take longer to build. Then download the result. You get it as an Excel spreadsheet.

The reports cover four kinds of building permits: commercial alteration and addition, commercial new construction, residential alteration and addition, and residential new construction. For a sub hunting work, the issued report is the one that matters most. A submitted permit is a plan. An issued permit is a job that cleared the counter and can start.

Work the spreadsheet like a call list. Sort by issue date, newest first. Keep the districts where you already work. Flag the new-construction rows first, since they carry the longest chain of trade work, then the larger alteration jobs. That sorted file is your Monday list.

What DallasNow gives you — and what it does not

Know what you're trading, because it shapes your week.

What you get back is a live source. The records behind DallasNow are the city's working records, the same system staff use to take applications, track reviews, and schedule inspections. A report you pull this morning reflects permits the city has actually issued, not a copy that stopped updating years ago.

What you lose is the automatic feed. There's no public data pipe behind these reports that a tool can read on its own each night — our audit didn't find one, and the report portal is the only public route the city documents. So nobody can honestly sell you a hands-off daily Dallas list built the old way. If a service shows you fresh Dallas permits, ask it one question: where exactly do the records come from? DallasNow reports pulled by hand are a fair answer. The old open-data feed is not.

You also go one city at a time. DallasNow covers the City of Dallas. It says nothing about Irving, Garland, Mesquite, or any other city in Dallas County, and there is no single county database that holds them all. Each city runs its own permit system and its own search. That's slower than one feed. It's also how DFW actually works.

And watch the gap years. Nothing in DallasNow's public reports rebuilds the missing 2021–2024 history in bulk. For a single address, the portal can still answer questions. For market history, treat those years as a hole and say so, the way this post does.

A weekly routine for DFW subs

Here's a routine built on the tools that actually exist now. It takes about an hour a week.

On Monday, run the DallasNow issued-permits report for the last seven days, for your districts or for all districts if you cover the whole city. Save the spreadsheet with the date in the file name, so you can see what's new instead of re-reading the same jobs. Sort it, flag your trade's rows, and make your calls while the jobs are still staffing up. Shovels puts it plainly: permits are filed weeks before ground breaks, which is why a fresh issued list is worth an hour.

Midweek, cover Fort Worth, where the old way still works. Fort Worth permits sit in a live feed, and PermitsByCounty publishes them in a free report you can read the day they are issued. Put the two lists side by side and you have most of the metro's issued work in front of you. Our Fort Worth guide shows how local subs use that same-day list.

Once a month, spend twenty minutes on the cities in between. Pick the two or three Dallas County cities where you would actually drive for work, learn each one's permit search, and run it. Don't try to cover two dozen city systems.

And keep the old feed in its place. Bookmark it as history, not as a lead source.

Use the old feed for the one job it can still do

A dead feed isn't useless, though. It's a clean archive of Dallas permits from before 2021, with contractor names, values, and ZIP codes. That makes it good for exactly one kind of question: history.

Say you're checking a house built or remodeled in 2019, or looking into how busy a builder was five years ago. The archive can answer that, and our guide to researching a builder before you bid shows how to read a record like that. What it can't do is tell you anything about this year's work. The date is the line. Anything you pull from it is 2020 or older, every time, no matter how new the page looks.

Watch out for sites that blur that line. A page can dress up old feed records with a fresh date on top and look current. Before you trust any Dallas permit list, find the newest issue date in the records themselves. If the newest record is years old, you're reading the archive, whatever the page claims.

Frequently asked questions

Did Dallas stop issuing building permits?

No. Dallas kept issuing permits, running inspections, and publishing building statistics the whole time. Only the public open-data feed stopped adding records, after 2020. The work kept going. The pipe that carried the records to the public went quiet.

What is the newest permit in the Dallas open-data feed?

Nothing newer than 2020. We counted the dataset by year ourselves on October 18, 2026: 126,840 rows in total, 27,459 of them from 2020, and zero from every year after that.

I read the Dallas feed stopped in 2024. Which date is right?

2020 — we counted it directly. The 2024 date comes from Tradewind, a paid Texas permit service that leaves Dallas out of its coverage. Its reason for staying out — the feed is dead — checks out. Its date doesn't: the year-by-year counts show no records at all after 2020.

How do I search Dallas building permits now?

Use DallasNow, the city's system that launched May 5, 2025. In its Citizen Portal you can run reports with no sign-in: pick Building, then Reports, choose issued, submitted, or active permits, pick one council district or all of them, set your dates, and download the spreadsheet.

Can a tool pull Dallas permits automatically, like the old feed?

Not through any public feed we could find. Our audit turned up no live machine-readable Dallas permit feed, and the public route the city documents is the DallasNow report portal, which you run by hand. Be wary of any daily Dallas list that can't name its source.

Where can DFW subs track permits outside the City of Dallas?

You go city by city. Fort Worth permits are live in PermitsByCounty's free Tarrant County report. Every other Dallas County city — Irving, Garland, Mesquite, and the rest — keeps its records in its own system, so learn the two or three searches for the cities where you actually work.

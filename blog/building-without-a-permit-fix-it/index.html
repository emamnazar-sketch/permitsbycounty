What happens if you build without a permit — and how do you fix it?

By PermitsByCounty Research Team · Published October 25, 2026 · Guides & Answers

QUICK ANSWER

If you build without a permit, the city can order all work to stop, charge you double or triple the normal permit fee, and fine you for each day the problem stays open. LegalClarity, checked October 25, 2026, says most places charge double to triple for a retroactive permit, on top of separate fines. Work that is unsafe or breaks zoning rules can be ordered torn down at your cost. To fix it, stop, call the building department, get as-built drawings, apply for a retroactive permit, open the work for inspection, fix what fails, and pass a final check.

TL;DR — the whole post in 20 seconds

  • The first thing most people get is a stop-work order. All work stops until you have a permit.
  • The permit after the fact costs more. Double or triple the normal fee is common.
  • Fines can run by the day, so waiting makes the bill bigger.
  • You fix it with a retroactive permit, also called an as-built permit.
  • Work that breaks zoning rules or is unsafe may not be fixable. That work can be removed.

What counts as building without a permit?

It means work that needed a permit was started, or finished, with no permit on file. The model building code used by most U.S. places requires a permit before you build, enlarge, change, or repair a building, and before you replace electrical, gas, mechanical, or plumbing systems. LegalClarity lists the usual cases: adding a room, taking out a load-bearing wall, running new circuits, moving plumbing, or replacing a roof.

Some small jobs usually do not need one. Painting, flooring, countertops, and cabinets are often exempt, and small sheds under a city size limit often are too. If you are not sure, call the building department before you start. Our guide on how much a building permit does cost shows the normal fee.

This is not the same as an open permit, which was filed and never closed. Unpermitted work was never filed at all.

How does the city find out?

Most people think no one will notice. That rarely holds for good.

A neighbor complaint is the most common trigger LegalClarity names. One call can send an inspector to your door.

A sale is the other big moment. The appraiser compares the house with tax and permit records, and an extra bedroom that is not in the records stands out fast. LegalClarity says unpermitted work usually surfaces when the stakes are highest — during a sale, an insurance claim, or an injury.

Cities can also spot changes in aerial photos.

What happens first if you build without a permit?

The first thing that happens, in most places, is a stop-work order: all construction must stop at once, until you apply for and get the right permits. Do not keep working after you get one. LegalClarity warns that towns treat that as a separate, more serious problem, with civil sanctions starting around $500, or criminal charges.

Chicago shows how a real city handles this.

Stop-work penalty of $1,000 or more; in most cases the fee for a permit issued after a stop-work order is tripled

Chicago stop-work penalty — Chicago, IL · City FAQ, checked October 25, 2026

Source: City of Chicago — What happens if I start work without a required building permit? · Date pulled: October 25, 2026

Chicago also says the city may start an enforcement case or a lawsuit, and that work which cannot be fixed may have to be removed at your cost. That pattern — stop, pay more, fix or remove — is the pattern in most places.

How much can building without a permit cost you?

There is no single national fine. Each city sets its own. The shape of the cost is the same almost everywhere, in three parts.

First, the permit itself costs more after the fact.

Most municipalities charge double to triple the standard permit fee for a retroactive permit, on top of separate code violation fines

Retroactive permit fee penalty — United States · Guide checked October 25, 2026

Source: LegalClarity — What Happens If You Don’t Pull Permits: Fines and Risks · Date pulled: October 25, 2026

A second LegalClarity guide puts the wider range at two to four times the normal fee.

Second, there can be a fine for the violation itself. LegalClarity says those fines commonly range from several hundred to over a thousand dollars, and each day the violation stays open can count as a separate offense.

Third, in a big city the stop-work penalties alone can be large. New York City states its own numbers for working against a stop-work order.

$6,000 for a first offense of violating a stop-work order; $12,000 for later offenses; work cannot resume until a permit is issued and the order is lifted

New York City stop-work violation penalties — New York City, NY · City page checked October 25, 2026

Source: NYC Department of Buildings — Stop Work Order · Date pulled: October 25, 2026

In many places it can also be a misdemeanor, with added fines or even jail time for repeat cases. For most homeowners the money is the real hit.

What happens later, at a sale or an insurance claim?

The building department is only the first problem.

At a sale, you have to tell the buyer what you know. LegalClarity says you are legally required to disclose known unpermitted work in your state’s disclosure statement. Hiding it is risky and, in practice, hard.

Appraisers have to note additions that lack permits, and that space often does not count in the home’s official measurement. A finished basement that cost real money can add little appraised value, and a low value can sink the buyer’s loan. Our guide on whether you can sell a house with unpermitted work walks through the seller’s choices.

Insurance is the other quiet risk. If damage starts in work that was never inspected — wiring that starts a fire, plumbing that floods a room — LegalClarity says the insurer can deny the claim. An insurer that finds unpermitted work can also cancel the policy or refuse to renew it.

Old work does not fix itself with time. Unpermitted work does not get grandfathered in. Grandfathering covers work that was legal when built and later fell out of step with a new code. Work that was never legal was never legal, and a city can enforce five, ten, or twenty years later.

Can the city make you tear the work down?

Yes, in the worst cases. LegalClarity calls a demolition order the most severe result. If work breaks structural codes or creates unsafe conditions, the city can make you tear it down at your own cost.

It names the cases where this happens most often: unpermitted additions, decks, and converted garages that do not meet rules for exits or structure, plus work that breaks zoning in ways that cannot be fixed. Demolition is not the usual result — most work built well and in the right place can be legalized — but you do not get to pick the outcome. The inspector and the code do.

How do you fix building without a permit, step by step?

The fix is a retroactive permit, also called an as-built permit. Our full guide on how to get a retroactive permit for work that is already done goes deeper. Here is the short order most places use.

Step 1: Stop work, and do not cover anything up. If you have a stop-work order, obey it. Covering hidden work makes inspection harder.

Step 2: Call the building department and say what happened. LegalClarity advises this as the first step to legalize work. Coming forward tends to go better than being caught, and some places reduce penalty fees for self-reported violations. Ask what form they use for after-the-fact work.

Step 3: Gather what you have. Collect photos from during the work, receipts, contractor invoices, and notes on what was done. LegalClarity’s after-the-fact guide says the more proof you have, the smoother the inspection goes.

Step 4: Get as-built drawings. Most departments require plans that show the work as it stands now. A licensed architect, engineer, or drafter makes them.

As-built drawing fees generally run 5 to 20 percent of the total project cost, depending on how complex the work is

Cost of as-built drawings — United States · Guide checked October 25, 2026

Source: LegalClarity — Can You Get a Building Permit After the Fact: Steps and Costs · Date pulled: October 25, 2026

A second LegalClarity guide says to expect several hundred to several thousand dollars for this step alone. Get a quote in writing.

Step 5: File the application and pay the penalty fee. File with your drawings and any calculations the department asks for. The review works like a normal permit review, though some departments look at retroactive files more closely.

Step 6: Open the work for inspection. The inspector has to see behind the walls. That can mean taking off drywall or ceiling panels so framing, wiring, and plumbing can be checked. LegalClarity warns that for a finished basement or a redone kitchen, this open-and-rebuild cycle can cost nearly as much as the first project.

Step 7: Fix what fails, get checked again, and close the permit. Correct anything that does not meet current safety rules, then book a new inspection. The work counts as legal only after it passes and the department closes the permit. Keep that closed record for a sale, a refinance, or an insurance claim.

What if the work cannot be legalized?

Some work cannot be fixed with a permit. The main block is zoning: building code governs how a thing is built, zoning governs where it can sit and how big it can be. An addition in a setback or over a height limit is a land-use problem.

The path then is a zoning variance, a request to be excused from a rule. LegalClarity warns that variances for work already built are very hard to get: boards ask for a hardship from the property itself, not from a choice you made, and building first is a textbook self-created hardship. If no variance is granted, the department can order the work removed.

Who pays for this — you or your contractor?

As the owner, you do, at least at first. LegalClarity is direct: the owner is responsible for making sure work on the property is permitted, even if a contractor did the work. The fines attach to your address, and the retroactive application is your file to open.

That does not let a contractor off the hook. Under standard contracts the contractor usually must obtain permits and book inspections. If yours skipped that step, you may have a breach-of-contract claim, but LegalClarity notes that fight is separate and does not pause the city’s case. Licensed contractors who work without permits can also face discipline, from fines to losing the license.

One habit prevents most of this: ask to see the permit application before work starts. A contractor who pushes back on pulling a permit is telling you how they work. If you are checking a house you want to buy, our guide on how to look up building permits by address for free shows the free search.

Frequently asked questions

What happens if you build without a permit?

The city can issue a stop-work order, charge double or triple the normal fee for a retroactive permit, and fine you for each day the violation stays open. Unsafe or zoning-breaking work can be ordered torn down at your cost.

How do you fix it if you already built without a permit?

Stop work, call the building department, and apply for a retroactive permit. You will need as-built drawings, an inspection that may require opening walls, fixes for anything that fails, and a final passed inspection.

How much does it cost to fix unpermitted work?

The permit fee is often the small part. Expect double or triple the normal fee, plus fines, plus as-built drawings at 5 to 20 percent of the project cost, plus the cost of opening and closing walls. No honest total exists until an inspector has seen your project.

Can you be forced to tear down work built without a permit?

Yes. If the work is unsafe or breaks zoning in a way that cannot be fixed, the city can order it removed at your cost. LegalClarity says this happens most often with additions, decks, and converted garages.

Does unpermitted work become legal after a few years?

No. Work that was legal when built can be grandfathered when codes change. Work that was never permitted was never legal, and cities can enforce years later.

My contractor said no permit was needed. Who is responsible?

You are, as the owner, in the city’s eyes — even if the contractor was supposed to pull the permit. You may have a claim against the contractor, but that fight does not stop the city’s case.

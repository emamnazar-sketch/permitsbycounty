How much does a building permit cost in Detroit and Wayne County?

By PermitsByCounty Research Team · Published November 26, 2026 · Guides & Answers

QUICK ANSWER

The building permit cost Detroit projects pay is set by the value of the work, not by the size of the building. Under the City of Detroit BSEED fee schedule effective January 1, 2024 and modified July 18, 2025, a building permit costs $271.43 flat for work valued at $2,000 or less, then rises per $1,000 of project cost — about $544.15 at $10,000 of work, $1,055.57 at $25,000, and $2,895.29 at $100,000. Trade permits and plan review are charged on top of that. Detroit permits come from the city, not from Wayne County — each other city in Wayne County runs its own counter with its own fees.

TL;DR — the whole post in 20 seconds

  • Detroit charges by project value: $271.43 flat up to $2,000 of work, then a set amount for each extra $1,000 or part of $1,000.
  • A $10,000 job runs about $544.15 for the building permit alone. A $50,000 job runs about $1,668.82. A $100,000 job runs $2,895.29.
  • Electrical, plumbing and mechanical permits are separate, and plan review adds more when your project needs it.
  • PermitCalculator, working from the city’s published schedule, puts a Detroit bathroom permit at $830.65, a kitchen at $1,071.62, a roof at $748.69 and an HVAC permit at $130.
  • Wayne County does not issue your building permit. Inside Detroit, the city’s BSEED department does. Outside Detroit, your own city does, under its own schedule.

First question: city or county?

This is where a lot of people lose time. Detroit sits inside Wayne County, so it feels natural to look for a Wayne County permit office. There is not one for building permits like this.

Inside the City of Detroit, building permits come from BSEED — the Buildings, Safety Engineering and Environmental Department. You file through the city’s online system, eLAPS, or with help from the Development Resource Center. The phone number BSEED lists for permit help is (313) 224-2372.

If your project is in another Wayne County city — Dearborn, Livonia, Canton, Taylor or any of the others — that city runs its own building department and its own fee schedule. The Detroit numbers in this post do not apply there, even though you share a county. Check the city on your address first. That one check picks your fee schedule, your forms and your inspector.

PermitsByCounty groups its records under Wayne County (Detroit) because Detroit is the permit system we track there. Read that label the right way: the records are Detroit records, filed under the county name so you can find them.

How Detroit charges: by the value of the work

Detroit does not charge by square foot, and it does not charge one flat rate for a house. It charges by project cost — the value of the work, including design and construction cost.

The schedule works in brackets. The first $2,000 of value costs $271.43 flat. From there to $25,000, you add $34.09 for each $1,000, or part of $1,000, over $2,000. From $25,000 to $100,000, the base is $1,055.57 and you add $24.53 for each $1,000 over $25,000. From $100,000 to $500,000, the base is $2,895.29 and you add $27.82 for each $1,000 over $100,000. Higher brackets keep going for larger commercial work, up past $50 million, but most home and small-business projects live in those first four brackets.

That part-of-$1,000 wording matters. Detroit rounds up. A job valued at $10,100 is charged as if the part over $2,000 were 9 full thousands, not 8.1. Small round-ups like that are why two similar jobs can land a few dollars apart, and why the final invoice in the portal is the number that counts.

Your stated value is not just taken on trust for bigger work. For new buildings and additions, the city can work the value out from its square foot cost table. If you low-ball the value to lower the fee, you risk a correction later, and the permit you hold may not match the work you did.

Building permit cost Detroit: worked examples

Here is the math on real project sizes, using the city’s own brackets.

A small repair valued at $2,000 or less costs $271.43. That flat fee is the floor in Detroit. Even a very small permitted job starts there, which surprises people who have paid $75 or $100 for a small permit in another city.

A job valued at $8,000 costs $271.43 plus six times $34.09, so $475.97. A job valued at $10,000 costs $271.43 plus eight times $34.09, so $544.15. PermitCalculator puts a typical Detroit residential building permit fee at $476 in its city table, which lines up with a job in that $8,000 range — a useful check that the bracket math matches what its calculator sees across the schedule.

A job valued at $25,000 sits at the top of its bracket at $1,055.57. A $50,000 remodel costs $1,055.57 plus twenty-five times $24.53, so $1,668.82. A $100,000 project costs $2,895.29. A $150,000 project costs $2,895.29 plus fifty times $27.82, so $4,286.29.

Read those numbers as the building permit alone. They do not include trade permits, plan review, or any zoning or certificate fees your project needs. A kitchen that needs electrical and plumbing work will pay more than the building line on its own, and the next sections show why.

What common Detroit projects pay in total

Most homeowners do not ask for a bracket. They ask what a bathroom, a kitchen or a roof costs to permit. PermitCalculator answers that from the city’s published schedule, adding the building and trade pieces a normal job needs.

Its Detroit totals are $830.65 for a bathroom remodel permit, $1,071.62 for a kitchen remodel permit, $748.69 for a roof permit and $130 for an HVAC permit. Those are handy sanity checks. If a quote for a plain bathroom permit in Detroit is far above $830.65, ask what else is in the number. If it is far below, ask what was left out.

The HVAC figure shows the other side of Detroit pricing. A single trade job with no building work can be cheap. The $271.43 building floor only bites when a building permit is part of the job. A furnace or air-conditioner swap that needs only a mechanical permit is priced on the trade schedule, not the building brackets.

Trade permits: the stack on top

A building permit does not cover the trades in Detroit. Electrical, plumbing and mechanical work each carry their own fees, priced by the item rather than by project value.

Electrical work starts with a $66 base fee. A new or changed circuit is $20 each. A rough inspection for a one- or two-family home is $59. Residential fixtures are $1.17 each. An electrical service of 100 amps or less is $59, and a service over 100 amps up to 200 amps is $117.

Plumbing starts with a $73 nonrefundable application fee, then $44 for each fixture, stack, sump and similar item on the schedule. A bathroom that adds a tub, a toilet and a sink is not one plumbing charge — it is the application fee plus a charge for each item.

Mechanical work for a homeowner’s gas-fired gear is priced by the unit. A central heating unit, a conversion burner or a floor furnace is $203. A room or wall heater is $130 for the first unit and $59 for each one after that.

None of these numbers is large on its own. The stack is what adds up. A full kitchen can carry a building permit, an electrical permit with several circuits, a plumbing permit with several fixtures and a mechanical line for a range or a furnace — four fees where a homeowner expected one.

Plan review and the smaller lines

When a project needs plan review, that review is not free, and it is not folded into the building permit.

Under the BSEED schedule, building, structural and zoning plan review is taken as a deposit of 35% of the building permit fee. Electrical, mechanical and plumbing plan review each add 7% of the building permit fee when those reviews are required. On a $100,000 project with a $2,895.29 building permit, the 35% deposit alone is about $1,013 — a line many first budgets miss completely.

A few smaller lines are worth knowing before they surprise you. A change of use, building only, is $249. A permit needed because of a historic district — the schedule gives exterior painting and fences as examples — is $106, and so is a residential barrier-free ramp. A reinspection, including a visit where the inspector cannot get in or the work is not ready, is $176.

And if you do work that needed a permit without getting one, the schedule lists a failure-to-obtain-a-permit fee of $176 per occurrence for a homeowner and $351 for a contractor. That fee sits on top of the permit you still have to pull. Getting the permit first is the cheaper path, every time.

Detroit building permits are generally valid for six months, about 180 days, from issue. If your project stalls past that window, ask BSEED about your options before the permit lapses rather than after — an expired permit is a problem you can often head off with one call.

How to get your real number before you start

Start with the city, not the project. If the address is in Detroit, use the BSEED schedule in this post. If it is in another Wayne County city, find that city’s building department and its schedule first. The county name on your mail does not pick your permit office.

Next, put a real value on the work. Add design and construction cost together, because that is the value Detroit prices. Then run the brackets: $271.43 to $2,000, $34.09 per extra $1,000 to $25,000, $24.53 per extra $1,000 to $100,000, and $27.82 per extra $1,000 after that. Round each part of $1,000 up.

Then list the trades. Count the circuits, the plumbing fixtures and the mechanical units your job touches, and price each one from the trade schedule. If your project needs plan review, add the 35% deposit and any 7% trade reviews that apply.

Last, check the portal before you lock a budget. Fees are set in eLAPS when you file, and the schedule can change. The Development Resource Center can confirm your project’s fees at (313) 224-2372 if your job does not fit the plain brackets — historic districts, a change of use and mixed-use buildings are the usual cases where a call saves a second trip.

Write your estimate down next to your contractor’s quote. If the contract carries a permit allowance, you now have the city’s own schedule to check it against, line by line, instead of a guess.

Frequently asked questions

How much does a building permit cost in Detroit and Wayne County?

Inside Detroit, the building permit is $271.43 flat for work valued at $2,000 or less, then rises per $1,000 of project cost — about $544.15 at $10,000, $1,055.57 at $25,000 and $2,895.29 at $100,000, plus trade permits and plan review. Wayne County does not issue building permits; outside Detroit, your own city issues the permit under its own fee schedule.

Does Wayne County issue building permits for Detroit?

No. The City of Detroit’s BSEED department issues building permits inside Detroit, through the eLAPS system and the Development Resource Center. Other Wayne County cities run their own building departments. PermitsByCounty files its Detroit records under Wayne County so they are easy to find.

What does a bathroom or kitchen permit cost in Detroit?

PermitCalculator, working from the city’s published schedule, puts a Detroit bathroom remodel permit at $830.65 and a kitchen remodel permit at $1,071.62. It puts a roof permit at $748.69 and an HVAC permit at $130. Your total moves with the value of the work and the trades involved.

Are trade permits included in the Detroit building permit fee?

No. Electrical, plumbing and mechanical permits are charged apart from the building permit. Electrical starts at a $66 base fee plus $20 per circuit. Plumbing starts with a $73 application fee plus $44 per scheduled item. Plan review, when required, adds a 35% deposit plus 7% for each trade review.

How long is a Detroit building permit valid?

Most Detroit building permits are valid for six months, about 180 days, from the date they are issued. If your work will run past that window, contact BSEED before the permit expires to ask about your options.

What happens if I do work in Detroit without a permit?

The BSEED fee schedule lists a failure-to-obtain-a-permit fee of $176 per occurrence for a homeowner and $351 for a contractor. You still have to pull the permit for the work, so working without one costs more than getting it first.

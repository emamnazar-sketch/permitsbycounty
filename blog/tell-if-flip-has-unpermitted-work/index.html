How can I tell if a flip has unpermitted work before I make an offer?

By PermitsByCounty Research Team · Published November 7, 2026 · Guides & Answers

QUICK ANSWER

You can tell by comparing three records before you offer: the listing, the county assessor's record, and the permit history for the address. If the house has more finished space, more bathrooms, or a converted garage that none of the records explain, treat that part as unpermitted work until someone proves otherwise. iBuyer, a national home buyer, says a gap of 200 square feet or more between the assessor's number and the real house is worth investigating; LegalClarity's permit guide gives 300 square feet as its example. Both guides were checked on November 7, 2026.

TL;DR — the whole post in 20 seconds

  • Three records, one comparison: listing, assessor record, permit history. Work in the house but in none of the records is your suspect list.
  • A square-foot gap of 200 square feet or more deserves a hard look before you price the deal.
  • Read every permit's final status. A permit that never reached "finaled" or "closed" is an open job you would inherit.
  • Price the fix before you offer. Legalizing work means as-built drawings, penalty fees, and sometimes opening finished walls.
  • Four moves when you find a problem: seller legalizes it, a credit, a price cut, or walk away while your contingency lets you.

Why does unpermitted work hit a flip harder than a normal purchase?

A flip runs on two numbers: what you pay, and what the finished house sells or appraises for. Unpermitted work attacks both.

On the buy side, the work becomes yours at closing. A code problem follows the property, not the person who did the work. LegalClarity's after-the-fact guide puts it plainly: there is no time limit that makes old unpermitted work safe, and a buyer who closes inherits the problem along with the deed.

On the sell side, the space may not count. Appraisers measure a house against its records, and space that was never permitted can be left out of the gross living area — the finished, heated square footage your resale price is built on. If your margin sits in an addition an appraiser will not count, you do not have a margin. After closing, the same facts stop being a negotiating point and become a bill.

What counts as unpermitted work in a flip?

Unpermitted work is work that needed a permit and never got one — or got one and never passed a final inspection. The second kind fools people, because a permit number exists. A permit pulled and left open is not an approval. It is a job the city never signed off on.

The work that almost always needs a permit is what flippers care most about: room additions, garage and basement conversions, new bathrooms and moved plumbing, panel upgrades, load-bearing wall removals, and decks above a low height. Paint and flooring usually do not need one.

Aim your eyes at the rooms that carry the resale story: the added suite, the finished basement with a bathroom, the garage that is now a den. Each should have a paper trail.

How do I run the three-record check?

First, the listing. Write down what it claims: square feet, bedrooms, bathrooms, and every improvement it brags about. Words like added, converted, and expanded each name a job that should have a permit behind it.

Second, the assessor's record. The county assessor keeps the official version of the house: living area, bedroom and bathroom counts, year built, and often a footprint sketch. It is the baseline every appraiser starts from.

Third, the permit history. Search the address in the city or county permit portal — records are public, and most portals search by address or parcel number at no cost. List every permit: what it was for, when it was issued, and how it ended. Our earlier post on checking a house's permit history before you buy walks through the lookup itself.

Now line the three up. Every visible improvement should appear in the permit history, and the finished space should roughly match the assessor's living area. Anything in the house but in neither record goes on your suspect list, with the room name and a rough size.

If the online search comes back thin, don't stop there. Older work can sit in records never put online. LegalClarity's guide notes that departments hold older files in person and through public records requests, at a few dollars a page to roughly $5 to $30 for a certified copy. A margin can turn on one missing permit.

What does the square-foot gap actually tell me?

It tells you where to dig, not what you have proven. Assessor records can lag a legal remodel, and listings can be sloppy. A gap is a question. Its size tells you how loud it is.

iBuyer's seller guide draws its line at 200 square feet: a difference that big between the assessor's listed square footage and the home's actual square footage is worth investigating, because it is typically space added without permits. LegalClarity's guide uses a bigger example — about 300 square feet — and calls that a strong sign of an unpermitted addition, since unpermitted work never gets reported to the assessor's office.

A discrepancy of 200 square feet or more between the assessor's listed square footage and the home's actual square footage is worth investigating

How do I read the permit history line by line?

Don't just count permits. Read the ending of each one.

A permit marked finaled or closed means the work passed its inspections and the city signed off. That is the only ending that clears a room. A permit still open or expired means the sign-off never happened, and closing it later can mean new inspections under newer codes. LegalClarity's guide also warns that a department can hold new permits on a property until old ones are closed.

Match scope while you read. The permit description is the approved job in a few words — kitchen remodel, 200-amp panel, deck addition. Hold it against the room. A water heater permit does not clear a bathroom addition that happens to contain one.

What should I look for on the walk-through?

Records first, then the house — because now you know what you are looking for.

In the suspect rooms, look for the seams. A change in ceiling height or floor level where an addition meets the old house. A garage door still on the outside with a finished room behind it. A bedroom cooled by a window unit while the rest of the house runs on central vents. A second kitchen nobody mentions.

Ask your inspector to flag anything that looks added later, and know the limit of that ask. Inspectors describe what they see; they don't pull permit records. iBuyer's guide makes the same point — the permit check is a records job, and it is yours. Bring your suspect list and have the inspector spend time in those rooms. A sign moves a room from "looks nice" to "show me the permit," before your money is committed.

What does the seller's disclosure tell me — and what does it miss?

Read it against your suspect list. In most states a seller has to disclose known unpermitted work, including work done by earlier owners once the seller knows about it. iBuyer's guide is blunt that disclosure is not optional, and that leaving known work off the form can bring a misrepresentation claim after closing.

But a disclosure only carries what the seller knows and admits. Flips often come from estates or owners who never lived in the house and can honestly write "unknown" down the page. Unknown is not a clearance — your three records carry the whole weight.

Price the house as if the work is unpermitted until the records prove otherwise. And remember: whatever you learn before you buy, you will have to disclose when you sell.

What will it cost to make unpermitted work legal?

Price it before you offer, with ranges, not hopes. Legalizing work after the fact usually means an application, as-built drawings showing the work as it stands, inspections, and fixes for anything that fails.

The drawings alone are a real line item. LegalClarity's after-the-fact guide puts architect or drafter fees for as-built drawings at 5 to 20 percent of the total project cost. On a $40,000 addition, that is $2,000 to $8,000 before a single inspection happens.

As-built drawings for a retroactive permit generally run 5 to 20 percent of the total project cost

Fees stack on top. Many places charge a penalty on after-the-fact applications — commonly double the standard permit fee — and some add daily fines that can reach several hundred dollars a day. The expensive part is usually the correction work: opening finished walls so an inspector can see wiring, plumbing, and framing, then paying to put them back. The work must also meet the code in force when you apply, not the code from the year it was built.

iBuyer totals the seller's side: retroactive permitting can take 2 to 18 months, with correction costs of $5,000 to $50,000 or more. Read that as your inherited range when you buy the problem yourself. Some work cannot be legalized at all — an addition that breaks a setback or height limit can be denied, and a denied job can end in a removal order.

How does unpermitted space change what the house is worth?

Run your resale math on the permitted house, not the photographed house. If the suspect space were left out of the living area tomorrow, what would the smaller, legal house sell for? That is your ceiling for value.

The appraisal is where this stops being theory. Under Fannie Mae's Selling Guide, section B4-1.3-05, an appraiser who finds an addition without the required permit has to comment on the quality and appearance of the work and on its effect on market value. The square footage may be excluded from gross living area or reported separately as nonstandard space, as LegalClarity's appraiser guide notes. Do not underwrite a resale on space the appraisal may not count.

When an appraiser identifies an addition without the required permit, Fannie Mae's Selling Guide (B4-1.3-05) requires the appraiser to comment on the quality and appearance of the work and assess its impact, if any, on market value

iBuyer's worked example shows the swing. A 500-square-foot unpermitted addition, in a market valuing permitted space at $150 a square foot, loses $75,000 if the space is excluded — 500 times $150. At flip margins, even a $20,000 appraisal gap is often the whole profit.

How do I write the offer when I find a problem?

You found suspect work. The house still pencils if the problem is priced or fixed. Four moves, in the order most buyers try them.

First, ask the seller to legalize it before closing, or at least to open the retroactive permit and reach an agreed milestone. This is cleanest when the problem is an unfinaled permit — closing an open permit is often a scheduling and inspection job, not a rebuild.

Second, take a credit or an escrow holdback sized to your priced fix — drawings, fees, opening and restoring walls, and a cushion for what the inspector finds.

Third, cut the price and take the problem yourself. This is the normal investor move, and it works when the discount beats the worst honest fix cost. Remember that your exit buyer will run this same check on you.

Fourth, walk away inside your inspection or due-diligence window. Some findings earn it: structural work with no permit and no way to see inside it, an addition over a setback line, a septic system sized for a smaller house. No margin survives buying a removal order.

What should I not trust on this check?

Don't trust age as a defense. Old work is not grandfathered work. Unpermitted work never had legal status, so there is no status for time to protect — iBuyer states that flatly, and LegalClarity's guide adds that enforcement has no expiration date.

Don't trust a permit count as a clearance. Three permits on a house with four projects is a gap, not a pass. And don't trust the listing's square footage as a measurement — listings repeat seller numbers, while the assessor's record is the figure your appraiser starts from.

Don't trust your own eyes for what's inside the walls. A room can look well built and still hide undersized wiring or unsupported spans; that's why legalizing finished work can mean opening it up. If the suspect work is structural, electrical, or plumbing and you cannot price the unknown, the honest price is a deep discount or a pass.

Frequently asked questions

How can I tell if a flip has unpermitted work before I make an offer?

Compare the listing, the assessor's record, and the permit history. Finished space, extra bathrooms, or a converted garage in the house but in none of the records is suspect. A gap of 200 square feet or more is worth investigating before you price the deal.

How big a square-foot difference points to unpermitted work?

iBuyer flags 200 square feet or more; LegalClarity's guide gives about 300 square feet as an example of a gap that strongly suggests an unpermitted addition. A gap is a reason to check, not proof — assessor records can lag legal work.

Does an open permit mean the work was approved?

No. An open or expired permit means the final inspection never happened or never passed. Only a permit marked finaled or closed clears the work, and a buyer who closes takes over open permits.

Will an appraiser count unpermitted square footage when I resell?

Don't count on it. Fannie Mae's Selling Guide (B4-1.3-05) requires the appraiser to comment on an unpermitted addition's quality and effect on value, and the space may be excluded from gross living area. iBuyer's example: 500 square feet at $150 a square foot is $75,000 potentially excluded.

How much does it cost to legalize unpermitted work?

Budget in ranges. As-built drawings run 5 to 20 percent of the project cost, after-the-fact applications commonly carry a penalty — often double the standard fee — and correction work can mean opening finished walls. iBuyer puts retroactive permitting at 2 to 18 months and $5,000 to $50,000 or more in correction costs.

If the seller writes "unknown" about permits, am I protected?

No. A disclosure only covers what the seller knows, and estates or absentee owners may truly not know. Your own record check has to do the work, and anything you learn becomes your disclosure duty when you sell.

How long is a building permit good for before it expires?

By PermitsByCounty Research Team · Published November 15, 2026 · Guides & Answers

QUICK ANSWER

Most building permits are good for 180 days at a time. Under the model building code rule a lot of places use, a permit becomes invalid if the work doesn’t start within 180 days of issuance, or if the work stops for 180 days after it starts. A passed inspection usually proves the work is moving and starts a fresh 180 days. Some cities give a full year instead, so check the date printed on your own permit. Angi, updated July 9, 2026, puts it this way in plain terms: a permit is good for six months until you start, and then you generally have one to two years to finish.

TL;DR — the whole post in 20 seconds

  • The common clock is 180 days — to start work, and again between signs of progress.
  • A passed inspection is the usual proof of progress, and it resets the clock in many places.
  • Not every place uses 180 days. Some give 365 days, and some trade permits are shorter.
  • Ask for an extension before the permit expires, in writing. After expiry, the choices get narrower and cost more.
  • An expired permit isn’t the end of the project, but work shouldn’t continue on it until the building department says how to fix the status.

How long does a building permit last, in plain terms?

A building permit doesn’t usually come with one fixed end date for the whole job, the way a driver’s license does. It comes with a clock. The clock says: start the work within a set number of days, and do not let the work sit still for that same number of days once you have started.

The number most people meet is 180 days. That comes from Section 105.5 of the International Building Code, the model code many cities and counties adopt. Maricopa County, Arizona, explains its version clearly: permits expire 180 days from the date issued or from the last completed inspection.

So a permit can stay alive for a long time on a big job, as long as progress keeps showing. A house that passes an inspection every few months keeps resetting its clock. A permit that sits in a drawer with no work and no inspection doesn’t.

Angi gives the homeowner version of the same idea. In most cases, a permit is good for six months until you start your project, and then you have one to two years to complete the project before the permit expires. That one-to-two-year finish window isn’t a promise in the code. It is what happens in practice when inspections keep passing and the local office keeps the permit active.

Read your own permit first. The printed expiration date, or the date your city portal shows, beats any general rule in this post. Local rules vary, and the office that issued your permit is the one that decides its status.

The two clocks running on your permit

It helps to think of two clocks, not one.

The first clock starts the day the permit is issued. If no work begins within the window — 180 days in many places — the permit becomes invalid. Starting work means real, permitted work, not just buying materials or clearing a lot for a different job. In many offices, the first passed inspection is the clearest proof that work has started.

The second clock runs after work starts. If work is suspended or abandoned for 180 days, the permit can expire even though you started on time. EngineerFix describes the same two-part timeline: a deadline to start, and a separate need to show ongoing progress until the final inspection.

That’s why a project can look busy and still be at risk. If the crew worked for two months, then left for seven months with no inspection, the second clock may have run out. The work you see on site isn’t the measure. The inspection record is.

A failed inspection deserves care here. Some offices count only a completed or passed inspection as progress. Maricopa County, for example, says that during its free extension period, a passed or failed field inspection with substantial progress made will extend the date — but that is Maricopa’s wording, not a national rule. Don’t count on a failed inspection resetting your clock unless your own office says so in writing.

What resets the clock?

In most places, a passed required inspection is the reset button. It tells the office the work moved forward and was checked. The new window then runs from that inspection date.

Other steps sometimes count, and sometimes they don’t. Ordering materials doesn’t count. Paying a contractor doesn’t count. A site visit that isn’t an inspection doesn’t count. If you think an activity kept your permit alive, ask the office to confirm the current expiration date and write down what they tell you.

Keep your own simple log. Write the permit number, the issue date, each inspection date and result, and the next deadline you were given. Put a reminder on your phone 30 days before that deadline. Most expirations aren’t surprises. They’re dates nobody tracked.

Places that do not use a plain 180 days

The 180-day rule is common. It isn’t universal.

The City of Piedmont, California, tells applicants their permit is valid for 365 days from issuance. Each successful inspection then extends the expiration to 180 days from that inspection. So Piedmont starts with a year, then moves to the inspection clock.

Some permits are shorter by design. Small trade permits, or permits not tied to a larger building permit, can carry shorter start windows in some codes. Some large commercial jobs are handled case by case, with phasing plans and agreed schedules.

Florida follows the same basic shape through its own code. Florida guidance explains that a permit generally becomes invalid if work does not start within 180 days, or if work is suspended or abandoned for 180 days, and that an approved inspection in the window is usually treated as proof of progress. Local Florida offices still apply the details their own way.

The lesson is simple. Use 180 days as your planning number until your permit or your building department gives you a different one. Never plan around a longer window you haven’t seen in writing.

How extensions work

An extension is a request you make while the permit is still alive. That timing matters. Many offices will not extend a permit that has already expired. You’d be asking for a renewal or a new permit instead, which is a different process covered in our expired-permit guide.

Maricopa County gives one free extension on prior written request, for an additional 180 days from the original expiration date. That is a clean example of how the process often works: ask in writing, ask before the deadline, give a reason, and get the new date confirmed.

Other offices charge for extensions. Angi notes that permit extension fees can reach up to 50% of the original permit cost. That figure is a ceiling Angi reports, not a standard price. Your fee, if any, is set by your city or county schedule.

A good extension request is short. It names the permit number and address, says why the work paused or has not started, says when work will start or resume, and asks for a specific new period. Keep the reply. If the office grants the extension by phone, follow up with an email that repeats the new date and ask them to confirm it.

Don’t ask for an extension you don’t need and then waste it. Some places allow only one. Maricopa’s free extension is one. If you use it, the next deadline is the one to protect.

What happens when a permit expires?

An expired permit means the permission to do that work has lapsed. It doesn’t mean the work you already passed inspection on disappears, and it doesn’t automatically mean anyone is in trouble. It does mean you should stop and fix the status before more work is done under that permit.

Working on an expired permit can be treated the same as working without a permit. That can bring stop-work orders, added fees, and problems at sale time, when an open or expired permit shows up in a search. Our guides on open permits and closings cover how buyers and title companies react to those records.

Your fix depends on how long the permit has been expired and on local rules. Maricopa County may renew a permit within 360 days of expiration for half the original building permit fee, giving another 180 days. A permit expired for more than 360 days there cannot be extended or renewed. Other places use different windows and fees, and some require a new permit under the current code.

If the work is substantially complete, some states give the building official a way to close the permit without a brand-new one. Florida law, for example, allows closure in defined cases, including permits that are years old with no apparent safety hazard. That decision belongs to the local agency, not to the owner. Ask. Don’t assume.

How to keep a permit from expiring

Start work promptly. The safest first step is to schedule the first inspection as soon as real work is ready to be checked. That turns a paper permit into an active job in the office’s eyes.

Keep inspections moving. Even on a slow job, a passed inspection every few months protects the permit. If a phase will sit for a while, talk to the office before the clock runs out, not after.

Watch trade permits separately. On a remodel, the building permit and the electrical, plumbing, and mechanical permits may not share the same status. One can be finaled while another sits open. Check each number.

Tell the office when plans change. A change of contractor, a long material delay, or a pause for financing is easier to explain before a deadline. Offices deal with delays every week. What they can’t fix easily is a permit that expired months ago with no contact at all.

If you buy a property with an open permit, ask for its status and last inspection date during your permit-history check, before you close. You may be taking over someone else’s clock.

Frequently asked questions

How long does a building permit last after it is issued?

In many places, 180 days to start work, with the clock resetting after progress such as a passed inspection. Some cities give a full year to start. The date on your permit and your local rule control, so check those first.

Does a building permit expire if I start work on time?

Yes, it can. Starting on time beats the first clock, but a second clock runs while you work. If work stops for the full window — often 180 days — with no progress or passed inspection, the permit can still expire.

What resets a building permit expiration date?

A passed required inspection is the usual reset. It proves the work moved forward. Buying materials, paying a contractor, or doing uninspected work usually does not reset anything. Ask your office what it counts.

Can I extend a building permit before it expires?

Often, yes. Many offices grant one written extension if you ask before the deadline and show a good reason. Maricopa County gives one free 180-day extension that way. Fees and limits vary by place.

What happens if my building permit expires?

The permission to work under that permit lapses. Stop further work under it and ask the building department whether the permit can be renewed, reactivated, or must be replaced with a new one. The window and fee depend on how long it has been expired.

How long is a building permit good for on a house remodel?

The same clocks usually apply to a remodel as to new construction: about 180 days to start and to keep showing progress in many places, longer in places that give a year. Each trade permit on the remodel can carry its own status, so check every permit number.

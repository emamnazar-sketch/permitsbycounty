First, fix the issuer in your head

Tarrant County doesn’t issue building permits. The county says so on its own development page: “Tarrant County does NOT issue building permits or certificates of occupancies.” It has no authority to enforce building codes in unincorporated areas.

The permits you want are City of Fort Worth permits, issued by the city’s Development Services Department. Inside Fort Worth city limits, a permit is a city record. In Arlington, Keller, or another Tarrant city, that city issues its own permits and keeps its own records. There is no single county drawer.

So read the title the honest way: a subcontractor who works in Tarrant County gets Fort Worth building permits from the city’s records, the day the city issues them.

What “the day they’re issued” really means

A permit becomes public when the city publishes the record, not when the builder tells anyone. Three clocks run after that.

The city’s own clock comes first. Fort Worth posts its permits through Accela Citizen Access, where you search permit records, and through its open-data table, which feeds outside tools. Checking the source each morning is the fastest free habit.

The daily-report clock comes second. PermitsByCounty pulls the Fort Worth feed every day — the live report shows the source last updated November 2, 2026 and our last pull November 3, 2026. A permit issued yesterday is in the report today, counted once and sorted by trade.

The paid-email clock comes third. Tradewind, a lead service built only for this metro, pulls the new Fort Worth and Arlington permits each morning and emails subscribers their trade’s matches before 6 a.m. Paid subscribers, in its own words, “see a permit the morning Tradewind indexes it, usually the day after the city publishes it.” Anything older than 48 hours is public on its site, and free accounts get a seven-day delay.

Nobody honest can hand you a city record before the city publishes it. What you can buy, or build as a habit, is seeing it the first morning it exists — instead of the next week, when the job already has its subs.

How much work Fort Worth actually issues

This isn’t a thin feed. Fort Worth issues thousands of permits a month, and most of them name work a sub can do.

In the 12 months ending November 2, 2026, the city issued 66,166 permits, with $7,338,130,617 in reported value on the building permits that carry a value. October 2026 alone brought 4,179 permits and $48,782,149.

The trade split is why a morning routine pays. Plumbing led the last 12 months with 16,213 permits, then electrical with 14,713, then HVAC with 12,137. New construction added 6,552 permits and most of the reported value, $6,031,087,125. A trade shop that skips a week is skipping hundreds of fresh records in its own trade.

One counting note. Fort Worth dates its records by the application, not the issuance, so PermitsByCounty counts each permit once, by permit number, in the month its application was filed — and recent months grow as filed applications finish review. We only count a permit once its status proves it was issued. Applications still in review don’t count, and neither do design-review approvals — those don’t let anyone build.

Your morning routine, step by step

Do this at the same time every workday. It takes minutes, not an afternoon.

Step 1: Open one source, not five. Start with PermitsByCounty’s Tarrant County report or the city’s records — one place, every morning. Five tabs in a random order is how permits get missed.

Step 2: Filter to your trade first. Plumbing, electrical, and HVAC are separate Fort Worth permit families, and each ran into five figures last year. Pull your trade’s newest permits before general building permits.

Step 3: Sort newest first, and set a floor. Work yesterday’s permits before last week’s. Set a minimum job size that fits your crew, and skip everything under it. A short list worked clean beats a long list skimmed.

Step 4: Read the scope, not just the type. The description tells you when your work comes up. A new build needs rough-in weeks before trim. Tradewind reads each scope against 17 trades and dates a window for each — you can do the same read by hand on a short list.

Step 5: Note who is named. Write down the builder, general contractor, or owner named on the filing, plus the address and stated value where the record carries one. That party is who you call. A permit doesn’t hand you a phone number.

Step 6: Call in the window, not at random. Early trades call early — site, concrete, and framing in the first days. Finishing trades belong later. One call at the right week beats three at the wrong one.

Step 7: Log it and repeat. Note the date, the permit, and what was said. The subs who win this feed are there every morning.

How to read a Fort Worth record in 60 seconds

Open the permit and answer five questions. Address: is it inside Fort Worth, and inside your ground? Type: is it your trade, or a building permit whose scope calls for your trade? Status: does it prove issuance — issued, finaled, expired, or closed after issuance — or is it still an application in review? Date: how fresh is it, keeping in mind Fort Worth dates records by application? Named party: who filed it, and is that a builder you want to work for?

Two traps. One project can show on several rows in the city’s table — PermitsByCounty counts it once by permit number, and you should too. And a stated value sits on building permits, not on trade permits, so a plumbing permit with no value is normal, not a small job.

A record that passes goes on today’s call list. One that fails gets dropped. Dropping fast keeps the routine at minutes.

When a paid morning email is worth it

What you’re paying for is the sorting. A paid feed reads each scope for you, works out when your trade’s work comes up, and lands in your inbox before your first call.

Tradewind’s prices, checked November 3, 2026, show the shape. Free is $0 — one trade, one county, weekly, delayed seven days. Pro is $99 a month — one trade, up to three counties, daily. Multi is $199 a month — every trade, every covered county, daily. It covers Fort Worth and Arlington today, and says counties are added only once a feed proves it updates daily — Dallas is left out because its open-data permits stopped refreshing in 2024.

That honesty is the test for any feed. Does it cover your city? Does it arrive the morning after the city publishes? Does each row match the city’s record when you spot-check it? If not, the free route is better — same record, no markup.

Don’t pay for homeowner contact data a seller can’t trace to a record. A Fort Worth permit carries the address, the scope, the dates, and who is named on the filing. It doesn’t carry a verified homeowner phone number.

What not to do with a same-day list

Don’t wait for a county list that will never come. Every morning spent hunting a county portal is a morning the city list went to someone else.

Don’t work Arlington or other Tarrant cities off the Fort Worth list and call it coverage. Each city keeps its own records. If you work Arlington, add its records on purpose.

Don’t call every permit the day it posts, whatever the stage. A permit is filed 2 to 6 weeks before ground breaks, and your stage may be months later. Date your call to your window.

Don’t treat a stated value as your bid. It is what the filer declared. Use it to sort — big job, small job — and price the real scope yourself.

Frequently asked questions

Does Tarrant County issue building permits?

No. The county says it doesn’t issue building permits or certificates of occupancy. Inside Fort Worth, the city’s Development Services Department issues the permits, and other Tarrant cities issue their own.

Where does the City of Fort Worth publish new permits?

Through its own permit records in Accela Citizen Access and its open-data table. PermitsByCounty pulls that city feed daily into its Tarrant County report, and paid lead services pull the same records each morning.

How many building permits does Fort Worth issue?

In the 12 months ending November 2, 2026, the city issued 66,166 permits. October 2026 alone brought 4,179. Plumbing was the top trade, with 16,213 permits in those 12 months.

Can I really see a permit the day it is issued?

You can see it the first morning the city has published it. A paid service says subscribers see a permit the morning it is indexed, usually the day after the city publishes it. A daily morning check of the city records or the PermitsByCounty report gets you the same first-morning view, free.

Does a Fort Worth permit give me the homeowner’s phone number?

No. A permit carries the address, the scope, the dates, the stated value on building permits, and who is named on the filing. It doesn’t carry a verified homeowner phone number.

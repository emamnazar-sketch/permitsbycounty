Do I need a permit to install an EV charger — and how much does it cost?

By PermitsByCounty Research Team · Published October 31, 2026 · Guides & Answers

QUICK ANSWER

Yes, in most places. If you add a new Level 2 charger on a new 240-volt circuit, you need an electrical permit, and the permit fee is often $50 to $300 for one home charger, per PermitFlow, checked October 31, 2026. A Level 1 charger that plugs into an outlet you already have usually needs no permit. The charger and the work cost far more than the fee: plan on about $1,000 to $3,000 all in for a normal Level 2 job if your panel has room.

TL;DR — the whole post in 20 seconds

  • A new Level 2 circuit needs an electrical permit in most cities and counties. A Level 1 plug-in on an existing outlet usually does not.
  • The permit itself is the small line. Many places charge $50 to $300 for one home charger, some up to about $800.
  • The full job is the big line. A charger is often $300 to $600, and the install is often $800 to $3,000 before any panel work.
  • A panel upgrade is what surprises people. About 1 in 5 homes needs extra work first, and a full upgrade can run $3,000 to $5,000 or more.
  • Get the load check first, and ask if a load manager or a shared circuit lets you skip the upgrade. Get the permit as its own line on the quote.

The short rule: new circuit, new permit

A home EV charger permit is a normal electrical permit. The city is not approving the car. It is checking the new circuit: breaker size, wire, grounding, and whether your panel can carry the load.

PermitFlow puts the split plainly, checked October 31, 2026. A Level 1 charger plugs into a standard 120-volt outlet, with no permit for that type. A Level 2 charger needs a 240-volt source. If you have to add that source, you will need a permit.

Qmerit says the same, checked October 31, 2026: most places require an electrical permit for a new circuit, so the work follows the National Electrical Code and local code. The common exception is plugging into an existing, code-ready 240-volt outlet.

So ask this: am I adding or changing electrical work? If yes, plan on a permit and a final inspection.

How much the permit itself costs

There is no one national fee. Each city sets its own schedule: a flat fee for a simple charger, a fee based on the value of the work, sometimes with a plan check fee on top.

In Lemoore, California, the master fee schedule lists $160 for an EV charger permit, per GreenLancer’s 2026 guide citing that schedule, checked October 31, 2026. Your city will differ. Ask for its number in writing.

Commercial work costs more: $200 to over $1,000 per PermitFlow. This post is about homes.

California tries to keep charger permits fast. Under AB 1236, cities must create an expedited, streamlined process with a checklist. An application that meets the checklist is deemed complete and must be approved. That law is about speed, not a statewide fee.

What the charger and the install cost

The permit fee is not the job. Keep three piles apart on a quote: the charger, the circuit work, and any panel work.

The circuit work is next. An electrician adds a dedicated breaker, runs wire to the garage or driveway, and mounts the charger or a new outlet. Distance matters a lot: next to the panel is a short run; across a finished house or to a detached garage costs far more.

Installation costs range from $800 to $3,000, with an average of $2,442 in the Qmerit data EnergySage cites

NeoCharge, from Qmerit and EnergySage figures checked October 9, 2026, lands in the same place: about $1,000 to $3,000 all in, made up of roughly $300 to $700 for the charger and $750 to $2,500 to install it. If a quote is well above that and your panel has room, ask what is driving it.

Why Level 2 needs the bigger check

A Level 2 charger is not a small load. EnergySage notes most need a dedicated 240-volt circuit with 40 to 60 amps. That is about the draw of an electric dryer, but a car may draw it for hours, night after night.

That is why charging counts at 125% in the load math, per NeoCharge: a 40-amp charger needs a 50-amp breaker. The inspector checks that breaker, wire, and panel match. Qmerit warns a plug-in charger on a NEMA 14-50 outlet needs a higher-grade outlet than a dryer does.

This is routine work for a good electrician. The permit is how the city confirms it.

The panel question decides the real price

Most homes with a modern 200-amp panel can add a charger with no upgrade, if it has open breaker space. The trouble starts in older homes.

About 20% of homes need an electrical upgrade before installing an EV charger, per Qmerit data

A 100-amp panel was standard for decades. It may still work, but it has less room for a big continuous load. A 60-amp panel is too small, and EnergySage says it will definitely need an upgrade.

The electrician settles this with a load calculation. Do not agree to an upgrade without that math in writing, and do not skip one the math says you need.

A complete panel upgrade typically runs $3,000 to $5,000 or more, counting panel, labor, permits, and utility work

A panel upgrade may need its own permit line and utility coordination. Ask whether the two permits are filed together or apart. Our who-pulls-the-permit guide explains why the licensed contractor should file.

Ways to avoid a panel upgrade

An upgrade is not the only answer when the panel looks full. EnergySage and Qmerit both point to smaller fixes first.

A load management system slows or pauses charging when the house needs the power. Under the 2023 code rules NeoCharge cites, if it caps what the charger can draw, the load calculation counts that cap instead of the full rating.

A circuit-sharing device lets two big loads share one circuit safely. The dryer runs, the car waits. The dryer stops, the car charges. The circuit never carries both at once.

A subpanel adds breaker space when the amps are there but the slots are not. A lower charger setting helps too: 32 or 24 amps still adds plenty of range overnight.

None of this removes the permit. A new circuit with a load manager is still a new circuit. It just may remove the panel job behind it.

How long does the permit take?

For a simple home job, PermitFlow puts permitting at 1 to 4 weeks, checked October 31, 2026. A job that also needs a panel upgrade or utility work will take longer, because more steps have to line up.

Residential EV charger permitting typically takes 1 to 4 weeks; commercial permitting can take 4 to 12 weeks or more

The papers that slow people down are the ones PermitFlow lists: a sketch of where the charger goes, the electrical plan, a load calculation, the spec sheet, and the installer’s license details. Ready on day one, there is less back and forth.

One money note. The federal 30C credit does not apply to property placed in service after June 30, 2026, per NeoCharge. Utility and state rebates still exist in some places. Check your power company before you buy, because many programs require a listed model and a final approved permit.

How to get the permit and the price right, step by step

1. Start with the outlet you already have. If a good 240-volt outlet is already where you park, ask an electrician to check its grade and condition before you pay for a new circuit.

2. Get a load calculation in writing. Ask for it with and without a load manager. That page tells you whether you need an upgrade, a smaller fix, or nothing extra.

3. Make the quote split its lines. Charger, labor and wire, permit on its own line, and any panel work on its own. You can only check the permit line against your city’s schedule if you can see it.

4. Check your city’s fee and checklist. Look up the fee, or call the building department. In California, ask about the streamlined checklist the city must use.

5. Let the licensed electrician pull the permit. The electrician files, does the work, and calls for the inspection. That keeps one name responsible to final sign-off.

6. Wait for the pass. The job ends at the approved inspection, not at the mount on the wall. Keep the sign-off with your home papers for a future sale or insurer.

Common mistakes that raise the bill

Paying for a panel upgrade with no load math. The math, not the sales pitch, should decide.

Buying the fastest charger first. A 48-amp charger needs a 60-amp hardwired circuit and more panel room than a 40-amp unit. For most daily driving, the slower setting charges the car overnight with room to spare.

Treating the permit as an extra to cut. Skipping it can bring fines, an order to redo work, and trouble at sale. PermitFlow lists citations and hefty fines for working without the right permit.

Using a worn dryer outlet for nightly charging with no check. The outlet, the wire, and the breaker all need to suit hours of continuous use, not a short dryer run.

Forgetting the rebate rules until after the install. If your utility offers a rebate, check the listed models and the permit proof it wants before you order anything.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a permit to install an EV charger?

Yes, if you are adding a new circuit. A Level 1 charger on an existing outlet, or a plug-in charger on an existing good 240-volt outlet, usually needs no new permit.

How much does an EV charger permit cost?

Often $50 to $300 for one home Level 2 charger. Some places charge up to about $800. Your city’s schedule is the only number that counts.

How much does it cost to install an EV charger at home?

About $1,000 to $3,000 all in for most homes with panel room: roughly $300 to $700 for the charger and $750 to $2,500 for the install. A panel upgrade adds more on top.

Do I need a panel upgrade for an EV charger?

Not in most homes. A modern 200-amp panel usually has room, and about 20% of homes need extra work first. A load calculation gives the answer.

Can I plug an EV charger into my dryer outlet?

Often, yes, if the outlet is in good shape and the right grade for long runs. A sharing device lets the dryer and the car use one circuit, one at a time. Have an electrician check it first.

Is there still a federal tax credit for a home charger?

No. The federal 30C credit does not apply to property placed in service after June 30, 2026. Some utility and state rebates are still open.

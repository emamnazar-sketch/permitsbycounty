Which contractors are busiest in my territory right now?

By PermitsByCounty Research Team · Published November 13, 2026 · Guides & Answers

QUICK ANSWER

You find the busiest contractors in your territory by counting issued permits, not by asking around. Take one full month of permits for your county, group them by the contractor name on each record, and rank the names by count. In Travis County, Texas, in October 2026, that count covers 4,925 issued permits, and 4,516 of them name a contractor, per PermitsByCounty records pulled November 11, 2026. The busiest name that month was Base Power, with 255 permits, all of them electrical. Two warnings come with every list like this: the same firm can sit in the ranking twice under two spellings, and some counties name no contractor at all — in the Fort Worth and San Antonio records PermitsByCounty holds, the contractor field is empty on every permit, so no honest busiest list exists there yet.

TL;DR — the whole post in 20 seconds

  • Rank one full month of issued permits by contractor name. A partial month favors whoever filed early.
  • Count each permit once, by permit number, and date it by the day it was issued.
  • Merge spelling variants before you trust the order — one firm under two spellings can hide its real rank.
  • Busy means volume, not quality. Read the trade mix and the job sizes before you act on a name.
  • If the county record names no contractor, there is no list. Do not let anyone sell you one built on air.

How do you build a busiest-contractors list from permits?

You build it in five steps, and the steps matter more than the tool. A spreadsheet can do this. So can a county report page.

Step one: pick one county and one full month. A full month is the smallest window that treats every firm fairly. A firm that pulls permits in a steady weekly rhythm looks quiet in a ten-day window and busy in a full month, because you caught more of its rhythm. October 2026 is the worked example in this post because it is the last full month in the record.

Step two: take issued permits only. An application is a plan. An issued permit is approved work with a start in front of it. Counting applications mixes real work with projects that may stall in review and never happen. If your source mixes the two, your ranking mixes them too.

Step three: count each permit once, by its permit number. Some records carry more than one line for the same job — a building line and a trade line, or a revised line next to the first one. If you count lines instead of permits, the firms with the messiest paperwork climb your list for the wrong reason.

Step four: group by the contractor name exactly as the record spells it, and count. That gives you a first ranking. Step five is the one people skip: read the ranking for spelling variants of the same firm before you use it. There is a full section on that below, because it changes who sits at the top.

One more choice sits under all of this: whose name counts. A permit can name the general contractor, the trade contractor who pulled that trade’s permit, or both on different lines. In Austin’s record, the name on a plumbing permit is usually the plumbing firm, and the name on an electrical permit is usually the electrical firm. That is good news if you sell to trades. It means the ranking answers the question you actually have: who is doing the work in my trade, in my county, right now.

Read that head the way a supplier reads it. Base Power is not a general builder having a big month. Every one of its 255 permits is electrical work. That is a firm wiring job after job, most likely tied to new homes and battery or panel work at scale. Victory Plumbing and IES Residential tell the same kind of story in their own trades. The list is not really a list of the biggest companies in Austin. It is a list of the firms touching the most job sites this month — and touching job sites is exactly what a supplier or a sub needs to know.

The tail matters too. Most of those 1,293 names appear once or twice in the month. A one-permit name may be a small crew that does careful work, or a builder whose big project lands next month. The ranking tells you where the volume sat in October. It does not grade anybody.

That shape — a busy head, a long quiet tail — is why a monthly list beats a yearly one for selling. A yearly list buries the firms that got busy last month under firms that were busy last spring. If you deliver material or sell trade work, last month is the territory you can still act on.

Why do spelling variants change the ranking?

Because the record is written by people, and people spell the same firm more than one way. The permit clerk types what the application says. The firm itself may file under a short name on one job and its full legal name on the next. Both spellings land in the data as two different contractors, and a plain count splits the firm’s real volume between them.

Travis County’s October list has a clean example sitting in plain sight. Radiant appears twice: Radiant Plumbing & AC with 77 permits, and Radiant Plumbing and Air Conditioning with 46. Counted apart, the first spelling ranks fifth. Added together, the firm holds 123 permits — second place, ahead of every other name on the list except Base Power. Nothing about the firm changed. Only the counting did.

“Radiant Plumbing & AC” 77 permits and “Radiant Plumbing and Air Conditioning” 46 permits in the same month — 123 permits for one firm, split across two spellings in the record

Chicago’s October list has the same trap at the very top. The busiest name in Cook County is Sunrun Installation Services with 94 permits — and Sunrun Installation Services Inc sits further down the same list with 50 more. Together that is 144 permits, well clear of the next firm. A reader who stops at the first ranking understates the busiest firm in the county by a third.

The fix is boring and worth it. Before you rank, scan the list for names that look like the same firm — a short form and a long form, an “Inc” that comes and goes, an “&” that becomes “and.” Check one or two of the addresses under each spelling. Same firm, same kind of work, same neighborhoods: merge them and say you merged them. Different firms that happen to share a word: leave them apart. Paid directories do this cleaning for you — Shovels says it cleans and deduplicates its contractor profiles — but if you build your own list, the cleaning is your job, and skipping it is how a good count turns into a wrong ranking.

What does busy actually tell you — and what does it leave out?

Busy tells you three things well. First, who is touching the most job sites, which is the right first question for anyone selling material, equipment, or trade labor. Second, what kind of work the volume is: a firm with 90 plumbing permits is a different call from a firm with 90 permits spread across whole-house builds. Read the trade mix under each name before you rank your call list. Third, busy tells you who is likely to need you soon. A firm that pulled 40 permits this month has 40 starts in front of it. Our guide to how suppliers find job sites before ground breaks covers the timing — permits are filed weeks before work starts, so a busy month is a pipeline, not just a score.

Busy leaves out just as much. It says nothing about whether the firm pays on time, runs a safe site, or finishes what it starts. It says nothing about job size: one firm can hold 100 small service permits while another holds five permits for one large project, and the second firm may spend more on material than the first. And a single big project can put a quiet firm at the top for one month and drop it off the list the next. That is why the honest way to use a busiest list is as a first screen, not a verdict. Our guide to researching a builder before you bid their job covers the second screen — license, history, and payment record — and it starts where this list stops.

Treat any ranking that hides its counting rules the same way. If a list will not tell you which county, which month, which date it counts by, and whether names were cleaned, it is not a ranking. It is a sales page wearing one as a hat.

Where does this method break?

It breaks where the record does not name a contractor at all. That is not a rare edge case. It is whole counties.

In the Tarrant County records PermitsByCounty holds — the City of Fort Worth permits, 67,450 of them over the last twelve months — the contractor field is empty on every single record. Bexar County, San Antonio, is the same: 68,054 records, no contractor named on any of them. The permits themselves are rich — type, trade, value, address, dates. But the name you would rank by was never in the record the city published.

Contractor name present on 0 of 67,450 Tarrant County (Fort Worth) permits and 0 of 68,054 Bexar County (San Antonio) permits held by PermitsByCounty — the published record carries no contractor field to rank

So the answer to “who is busiest in Fort Worth?” from permit data today is: nobody can tell you from the public record, and anyone who hands you a ranked list for that county built it from something else — or made it up. In Travis County, about 92 of every 100 issued permits name a contractor, and in Cook County about 97 of every 100 do. The method works where the record carries the name. Where it does not, the honest product is the permit list itself — which builders and trades are active, street by street — not a ranking of names nobody recorded.

This is also why “my territory” has to mean a county, not a metro. Two counties in the same metro can sit on opposite sides of this line. Ask the county question first: does this county’s record name the contractor? The answer decides whether a busiest list is even possible.

How often should you rebuild the list?

Every month. A busiest list is a snapshot, and construction moves fast enough that last quarter’s snapshot misleads. The firm that topped October may be finishing out those jobs in November while a new name climbs. A monthly rhythm also matches how the paid side of this market works: Shovels refreshes its contractor directory monthly, re-processed from its permit database and state license files.

Build yours on the same rhythm and keep the old months. Three months side by side tell you more than any single month can: who’s steady, who spiked on one project, who’s climbing. A firm in the top ten three months running is a territory fact. A firm that appears once is a project. Sell to them differently. Keep the counting rules fixed from month to month — same county, same window, the same date rule and name cleaning — so the months can be compared at all.

Frequently asked questions

Which contractors are busiest in my territory right now?

Count one full month of issued permits in your county and rank the contractor names on the records. In Travis County in October 2026 the busiest name was Base Power with 255 permits, all electrical, out of 4,516 permits that name a contractor. In Cook County the busiest single spelling was Sunrun Installation Services with 94. Your county’s answer takes the same count — if its records name contractors at all.

How do I find the busiest contractors near me for free?

Start with your county’s issued permits for the last full month and group them by contractor name, or read the county report on PermitsByCounty, which counts them from the same public records. Group the names yourself in a spreadsheet if you want your own trade or ZIP cut. What you cannot do for free is skip the name-cleaning step — spelling variants will move firms up and down the list.

Why is the same contractor on my list twice?

Because the record spells the firm two ways — a short name on some permits and a full legal name on others, or an “Inc” that comes and goes. In Travis County in October 2026, one plumbing firm sat at 77 permits under one spelling and 46 under another; together it held 123 and ranked second. Check the addresses and the kind of work under each spelling, then merge the ones that are plainly the same firm.

Does the busiest contractor mean the best contractor?

No. A permit count measures volume — how many job sites a firm touched in a month. It says nothing about payment habits, safety, or finish quality, and it treats a small service permit the same as a large project permit. Use the busiest list to decide who to look at first, then check license and history before you bid, supply, or hire.

Why is there no busiest-contractors list for Fort Worth or San Antonio?

Because the public permit records for those counties name no contractor. In the records PermitsByCounty holds, the contractor field is empty on all 67,450 Tarrant County (Fort Worth) permits and all 68,054 Bexar County (San Antonio) permits. A ranked list needs names, and those records do not carry them — so the honest answer there is permit activity by trade and street, not a ranking.

How often does a busiest list go stale?

In about a month. One big project can lift a firm for a single month, and steady firms move up and down as jobs finish and start. Rebuild the list monthly, keep the same counting rules, and compare three months side by side: a firm in the top ten three months running is steady work, while a one-month name is usually one project.

How do I get a certificate of occupancy after my final inspection passes?

By PermitsByCounty Research Team · Published November 1, 2026 · Guides & Answers

QUICK ANSWER

Passing the final inspection does not hand you the paper by itself. The building official issues the certificate of occupancy after every required trade has its final sign-off, all fees are paid, and no open application or violation is left on the property, per the NYC Department of Buildings Code Notes, checked November 1, 2026. In some cities there is no extra step: Austin issues it on a successful final inspection, and Philadelphia issues one with the building permit at no extra charge. Until that certificate, or a temporary one, is issued, the building may not legally be occupied.

TL;DR — the whole post in 20 seconds

  • A passed building final is one sign-off, not the whole closeout. Trade finals, fees, and paperwork must close too.
  • Some cities issue the certificate with the final inspection. Others want a request, a review, or a paid application.
  • In New York City, a final certificate needs construction, plumbing, electrical, and elevator sign-offs where one applies, plus no open applications or violations.
  • A temporary certificate lets you occupy a safe part of a building while small items finish. It always expires, often in about 90 days in New York City.
  • Read the certificate when it comes. Check the use and address before anyone moves in.

Passing the final inspection is not the last step

Most people hear “final passed” and expect to move in the same day. Often they cannot. The gap is the rest of the file: a building final can pass while an electrical final is still open, a survey is missing, or a balance is still owed.

The rule underneath is the same in most places. Section 111.1 of the International Building Code says a building shall not be used or occupied, and a change of occupancy shall not be made, until the building official has issued a certificate of occupancy for it. Section 111.2 says the official issues it after inspecting the building and finding no violations. The order is fixed: finish, pass every inspection, close the file, get the certificate, then occupy.

What the certificate actually says

The certificate is the city’s record of what the building legally is. New York City puts it plainly: it describes the legal occupancy limits, layout, and allowable use of a building.

That is why lenders, buyers, and tenants ask for it. It is proof the use itself is legal.

New construction needs one before anyone occupies it. A change of use needs a new or amended one, for example a warehouse turned into homes. Work that changes exits can need one too. Minor work often ends with a certificate of completion instead, which closes the permit but grants no new occupancy.

Philadelphia lists the same triggers, checked November 1, 2026: new construction, additions, alterations that impact exits or fire ratings, and changes of use or occupancy.

The closeout list: what must be true before it issues

Think of it as a checklist the city runs. First, every trade final must be in. Building, electrical, plumbing, mechanical, and fire protection each close on their own record. Raleigh’s page describes the same order: complete all required systems, pass each final, and collect a compliance certificate per trade. One point from Raleigh is worth copying: that certificate confirms the work is done. It does not give permission to occupy. Only the certificate of occupancy does that.

Second, the paperwork must match the building: survey, plans, and stated use have to line up. Third, money must be settled: unpaid fees hold the certificate. Fourth, the property must be clear: open applications and violations at the same address can block it.

How to get a certificate of occupancy, step by step

1. Ask which route your city uses before the final. Some cities issue it as part of the building permit. Others want a request after the final. Asking late can cost a week.

2. Close every trade final, not just the building final. Pull the permit record and read each trade line. Anything still open, pending, or failed is a hold.

3. Fix corrections fast and get the re-inspection done. The certificate cannot issue over an open correction.

4. Turn in the last documents. Final survey, test reports, and compliance statements. In New York City, the review request and its certifications are part of the process, not extras.

5. Pay every balance on the permit. Small balances hold big certificates every week.

6. Clear open items on the property. If an older item is yours, close it. If it predates you, ask what the department needs.

7. File the request your city requires, if it requires one. Portal, review, or standalone application. Keep the confirmation.

8. Read the certificate when it arrives. Check the address, the use, and the number of units. A wrong use line is the legal use of the building, and fixing it later is slower than catching it now.

Three cities, three different last steps

In Austin, the end is simple. The city’s page, checked November 1, 2026, says a certificate will be provided upon a successful final inspection for new construction or significant renovations. If it does not arrive, you email the department to request one. Copies of existing certificates work the same way: email the address and unit number, and allow 1 to 5 business days.

In Philadelphia, a certificate is issued upon completion of some building permits, with no separate application and no extra charge. Without a building permit, you apply and pay.

$253 per building or space, plus a $100 filing fee that is non-refundable and applied toward the final permit fee, a $3 city surcharge, a $4.50 state surcharge, and a $4 record retention fee per large page

Philadelphia says standalone applications are processed within 20 business days, then you schedule an inspection, and the certificate issues on successful completion. Two limits matter: no certificate is required for existing one-or-two-family homes, or when only the owner or tenant changes and the use stays the same.

In New York City, expect a folder review. The borough office checks pass-final inspections, settled dues, and whether the filed schedule matches the building. A mismatch must be amended first.

Raleigh is the fast end. Once all inspections pass, the certificate issues the same day, and you view it in the permit portal, in the attachment tab on the building permit.

Plan move-ins, lease starts, and closings around your city’s route, not the inspection date alone.

The temporary certificate: occupy now, finish the small items next

Sometimes the building is safe but not fully done. One floor is ready while another is not. That is what a temporary certificate of occupancy, or TCO, is for. Section 111.3 of the International Building Code lets the official issue one before all work is complete, if the part being occupied is safe. The official sets how long it is valid. It is a bridge, not a finish line.

A TCO typically expires every 90 days, costs a $100 fee unless exempt, and needs temporary or final construction, plumbing, and electrical sign-offs, plus a temporary elevator sign-off where one applies; if the space sits empty more than 30 days, a new TCO is needed to re-occupy it

Philadelphia prices its temporary certificate at $379. It needs an approved building permit, fire protection and egress in place, and the required certifications. Single-family homes cannot get one there. After a passed temporary inspection, you have 10 days to request and pay in eCLIPSE, and it issues within three business days after payment.

Raleigh is stricter: reapply every 30 days, for a maximum of 180 days. Austin allows one for commercial and residential property alike.

Two warnings travel with every temporary certificate. When it expires without a final certificate or a renewal, the legal right to occupy ends. And if you buy into a building still on one, you buy the remaining closeout work and its deadline too. Get the open list before you sign.

What blocks it, and the mistakes that cost weeks

The most common block is an open trade final nobody owned. The builder thought the electrician called it in. The electrician thought the builder did. Pull the record yourself.

The second is a mismatch between paper and building. The schedule says one use, the rooms say another. The fix is a filing, not an argument.

The third is old business at the same address. New York City is blunt: no final certificate with open applications or violations on the property.

The fourth is moving in early. It can bring fines, a vacate order, and insurance trouble, and it does not speed the paper up. Ask for the temporary route early instead.

The fifth is setting hard dates off a passed final. A closing that assumes the certificate is in hand slips the first time a review finds one open item. Build in slack.

If the certificate does not come

Start with the record, not the phone. List what still shows open: a trade final, a document, a fee, a violation. Most delays are one line on that list. Then ask one specific question: what is the one item holding this certificate? A named item can be fixed.

In Florida, one rule is worth knowing if you used a private provider. Under Florida Statute 553.791, private-provider cases carry a short statutory window, after you present the request with a certificate of compliance and all other approvals, for the official to issue the certificate or name specific deficiencies, with the request deemed granted if no notice is given in time. Read the current statute for the exact days, and keep your presentation date in writing.

Keep copies of everything: the certificate, the final record, and the closed permit. Getting a copy later takes days.

Frequently asked questions

How do I get a certificate of occupancy after my final inspection passes?

Close the rest of the file: every trade final in, last documents filed, all fees paid, and no open application or violation on the property. Then follow your city’s route.

Is a passed final inspection the same as a certificate of occupancy?

No. A passed final is one approval. The certificate is the city’s statement that the whole job is complete and the building may legally be used for its stated use.

How long does it take to get a certificate of occupancy?

It depends on the route. Raleigh issues it the same day once all inspections pass. Philadelphia processes a standalone application within 20 business days, then inspects. One open item can reset the wait.

What is a temporary certificate of occupancy?

A short-term certificate that lets you occupy a safe part of a building while the rest finishes. It always has an end date, typically 90 days in New York City.

Can I move in or open for business before the certificate is issued?

Not legally, unless a temporary certificate covers your space. A building shall not be used or occupied until the official has issued a certificate for it. Moving in early risks fines and a vacate order.

What does it cost to get a certificate of occupancy?

Often nothing extra. Philadelphia charges no extra fee with a building permit. A standalone certificate there costs $253 per building or space, plus a $100 filing fee and small surcharges.

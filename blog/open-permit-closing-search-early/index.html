Can an open permit kill a closing — and how early should I search for one?

By PermitsByCounty Research Team · Published November 10, 2026 · Guides & Answers

QUICK ANSWER

Yes, it can. Find an open permit early and it rarely kills the deal. Find one late and that’s a different story. So search before you write the offer — or on the first day of your inspection period at the very latest — and put the search in the contract. A standard title search does not disclose open permits, so nobody may be looking for one until the lender or title company checks, often 10 to 14 days before closing in South Florida, per South Florida Agent Magazine, August 10, 2026. By then a simple close-out that takes 2 to 4 weeks, or 6 to 10 weeks for older and harder cases, per MKC Construction & Engineering, checked November 10, 2026, no longer fits before the closing date.

TL;DR — the whole post in 20 seconds

  • Yes, an open permit can delay a closing, force a holdback, or end the deal — above all when it turns up in the last two weeks.
  • A title search alone will not find it. Open permits sit at the building department and need their own search.
  • Search before the offer if you can. If not, search on day one of the inspection period, while you can still object.
  • A missed final inspection often closes in 2 to 4 weeks. Older, complex, or skipped-inspection cases can take 6 to 10 weeks.
  • If the search is not in the contract, with a named person paying and a deadline, an early find still may not get fixed.

Can an open permit closing problem actually kill a deal?

Yes, it can — and most of the time it’s the clock that does it, not the permit itself. An open permit was issued but never closed out with a final inspection. The work may be finished and perfectly fine. The record just isn’t. And before any money moves, three parties get a say over that record: your lender, the title company, and the insurer.

Lenders do not like uninspected work. LegalClarity puts it plainly: lenders are reluctant to finance homes with unresolved permit issues because the value is uncertain and the legal exposure is real. An appraiser who finds uninspected work may cut the value by the cost of bringing it into compliance, and a lower value can break the loan even after buyer and seller have agreed on a price.

Title companies get a say too. In Florida, MKC Construction & Engineering reports that most title insurance companies will not issue a clean policy over an open permit without one of three things: the permit closed before closing, money held in escrow until it is closed after closing, or a specific exception in the policy, which means less coverage. Any of those three can be worked out with time. None of them is quick in the final week.

Most Florida title insurance companies will not issue a clean policy on a property with open permits without the permit being closed before closing, funds held in escrow until the permit is closed after closing, or a specific exception noted in the title policy, which means reduced coverage

Source: MKC Construction & Engineering, Open Permits Are Killing Florida Real Estate Deals · Date pulled: November 10, 2026

On its own, the permit is a small problem. What hurts is how little time is left once it turns up. Find one early and you’ve got choices. Find one with eleven days left and you’re in a crisis — and that’s what ends deals.

Why doesn’t the title search catch an open permit?

Because that is not what a title search does. This is the single fact most buyers get wrong, and it is the reason the search has to be asked for by name.

Title searches disclose encumbrances such as liens, mortgages, easements and restrictions, but they do not disclose open permits, which must be independently searched at the local government

The gap a title search leaves — United States · Law-firm guidance originally published in Law360, republished on Mondaq, updated October 27, 2026, checked November 10, 2026

Source: Lowndes, Drosdick, Doster, Kantor & Reed, P.A., via Mondaq, Open Permits Can Open Up Issues For Property Owners · Date pulled: November 10, 2026

Read that again if you are counting on your closing team to catch everything. Liens, mortgages, easements, restrictions — yes. Open permits — no, not unless somebody runs a separate permit search or a municipal search that includes one, and asks for permits in words. Some closing packages include that extra search as a convenience. Many do not. You will not know which kind you have unless you ask.

That gap also explains why sellers can honestly say they knew nothing. A permit pulled ten years ago for a water heater or a roof can sit open in a city portal the seller never opened. Our guide to checking a house’s permit history before you buy walks through the address search itself. This post is about timing: when that search has to happen for the result to do you any good.

When does an open permit usually surface — and why is that too late?

Left to the normal closing flow, it surfaces near the end. In South Florida, South Florida Agent Magazine describes the pattern in one scene: eleven days before closing, the title company sends an email with the subject line “issue found” — a water heater replaced in 2019, permit pulled, never finaled, and nobody knew it was there.

Open permits typically appear during the title search — 10 to 14 days out from closing; running the address at listing or before writing the offer gives about 60 days to resolve one instead of 11

When the bad news normally arrives — South Florida, Florida · August 10, 2026, checked November 10, 2026

Source: South Florida Agent Magazine, Open permits: What South Florida real estate agents need to know before closing · Date pulled: November 10, 2026

Ten to fourteen days sounds like time until you price the fix. The seller has to find the old permit record, hire a contractor if the original one is gone, schedule an inspection with a busy building department, fix anything the inspector flags, and pass a re-inspection. That is the 2-to-4-week simple path described below, and it does not fit inside eleven days. The harder path does not fit inside a month.

So the late find leaves only bad options: rush a close-out, renegotiate a deal that was already agreed with a holdback, or walk away. Every one costs somebody money or trust. The early find costs a search. The Lowndes guidance even tells of an open permit found the day before a refinance closing, the same day the old loan came due — resolved in time, but told as a warning, not a plan.

How early should you search for an open permit?

Before the offer is the best answer. Day one of the inspection period is the last good answer. After the inspection or due-diligence period ends is the answer that leaves you with the bill.

Before the offer, a search costs you almost nothing and buys you the longest runway. You search the address, and the parcel number if the address search looks thin. If you find an open permit, you can price it into the offer, ask the seller to close it before closing in the contract, or pass on the house with nothing lost but an hour. Sellers can use the same timing in reverse: search before you list, and a permit you close on your own schedule never becomes a buyer’s lever.

If a buyer skips the permit search during the due diligence period, they may lose the ability to object later; a buyer who assumes the search is someone else’s job may have the lender discover an open permit after the window has closed, and at that point the buyer is often stuck closing anyway and inheriting the problem

Why the due-diligence window is the line — United States · Guide checked November 10, 2026

Source: LegalClarity, How to Check Open Permits on a House and Close Them · Date pulled: November 10, 2026

Inside the window, do not save the search for the last day. Order it with the home inspection, on day one or two, so a find still leaves time for the building department’s answer, a contractor’s rough cost, and an objection while the contract lets you. Florida’s standard contract shows the clocks in days: Bayit Title’s guide to the Florida Realtors/Florida Bar form, revised February 2026 and reviewed October 16, 2026, lists 15 days to inspect and a title commitment due 15 days before closing when the blanks are left empty.

On the Florida FAR/BAR standard form with blanks left empty, open and expired permits are closed by the seller, up to the Permit Limit, 5 days before the closing date; the Permit Limit is one of three repair limits set at 1.5% of the price each if left blank, and a government delay extends closing up to 10 days, after which either side may cancel

The Florida standard-form clocks — Florida, United States · Form Rev. 2/26, guide reviewed October 16, 2026, checked November 10, 2026

Source: Bayit Title, Florida FAR/BAR and AS IS contract deadlines (Rev. 2/26) · Date pulled: November 10, 2026

That form is one state’s example, not a national rule. Read it as a shape: a window to look, a way to object, and a date after which the problem becomes the buyer’s. Run the search inside your window, whatever its length. If you are not sure what your contract says about permits, ask your agent or attorney before the window closes.

What happens if you find an open permit early?

You get normal choices. First, learn what the permit needs. Call the building department and ask: a final inspection only, a correction and a re-inspection, a renewal, or a new permit because the old one expired? A lot of open permits are a finished job and a missed phone call. Those are paperwork problems, and paperwork problems have prices and dates.

Second, put the fix in writing while the contract lets you: who closes it, who pays and up to how much, by what date, and what happens if the city is slow — an extension, a credit, or escrow until the permit closes. LegalClarity and the Lowndes guidance give the same advice: write the search, its cost, and seller closure before closing into the contract.

Third, you keep the early-only options: a price cut that covers the fix, or your inspection window used the way it was meant to be used. Our guide to closing an open permit before you sell a house covers the seller’s steps. This one is the buyer’s side: the same permit, with a deadline attached.

What happens if you find one late — or not at all?

The Central Florida case in the Lowndes guidance is the cleanest warning on record, and this post uses it exactly as the guidance tells it. A buyer could search for open permits and object to any it found, as part of due diligence. The buyer told its own counsel it would handle that search itself. It did not do the search in time.

In a Central Florida sale, the buyer failed to perform the permit search during the due diligence period; the open permit was discovered when the buyer’s lender arranged for a search, by which time the due diligence period had expired, and the buyer was forced to proceed to closing, stuck with the open permit and the cost of closing it afterwards

The buyer who skipped the search — Central Florida, Florida · Law-firm guidance originally published in Law360, republished on Mondaq, updated October 27, 2026, checked November 10, 2026

Source: Lowndes, Drosdick, Doster, Kantor & Reed, P.A., via Mondaq, Open Permits Can Open Up Issues For Property Owners · Date pulled: November 10, 2026

Nobody hid that permit and nobody lied. The buyer ran out of window, and the right to object ran out with it. The lender still had to be talked into a post-closing closure, with a guarantee it would get done in time. That is a lot of persuading for a search that takes an address and a parcel number.

After closing there is no window at all. The permit stays with the property, and the new owner is who the building department talks to. Florida law helps an arm’s-length buyer in one narrow way: a local agency may not fine that buyer solely because a prior owner failed to close a permit, and a current owner can close a permit someone else applied for, under Florida Statutes section 553.79. It does not close the permit, excuse the inspection, or stop a lender or insurer from caring. An old permit can also block new work — the Lowndes guidance notes a government can withhold new permits over open ones, and tells of a shopping center where more than 200 open permits, some 20 years old, got every new application rejected.

How long does closing an open permit take?

Plan on weeks, and plan from the honest end of the range. MKC Construction & Engineering, a Florida contractor that closes permits for sellers, splits it by how clean the case is.

For permits where the work was clearly completed, closing an open permit often takes 2 to 4 weeks; for older permits, complex work, or situations where multiple inspections were skipped, allow 6 to 10 weeks; most open permit issues resolve in 4 to 8 weeks with the right contractor

How long a close-out takes in Florida — Florida, United States · Guide published March 1, 2025, checked November 10, 2026

Source: MKC Construction & Engineering, Open Permits Are Killing Florida Real Estate Deals · Date pulled: November 10, 2026

The steps are plain: pull the record, get a licensed contractor to look at the work, schedule the final or missing inspections, fix what the inspector flags, pass, and the permit closes. Each step waits on somebody else’s calendar, which is why one inspection still eats two weeks. Older permits add risk: codes change, so old work may be judged against newer rules, and the original contractor may be gone. A new contractor can usually take over, but needs time to trust work he did not do.

Lay those ranges over the closing clock. Found 60 days out, a 6-to-10-week case is tight but workable. Found 30 days out, the simple case fits and the hard case does not. Found 11 days out, nothing fits, and you are negotiating a holdback instead of closing a permit. Search early enough that the hard case still fits.

A plain routine for searching early

Start before the offer. Search the portal for the city the property is actually in — city and county systems are separate in a lot of places, and a mailing address can point at the wrong one. If the search looks thin, search by parcel number. For a condo, search the base building address too, without the unit number, because building-level work lives there.

Write down every permit not marked closed or finaled: number, date, work named, and status in plain words — open, expired, or revoked. Do not judge it from the portal line alone. Shorthand hides whether a permit needs one inspection or a rebuild.

Call the building department for each flagged permit. What does it need to close? Can the current owner close it, or does it need the original contractor? How far out are inspections booked? Write the answers next to the permit numbers, with the date you called. That page is what your agent, lender, and attorney all need.

Then get the contract to match. If the search is clean, keep the page. If it found something, get the fix in writing before your window ends, and check any permit clause’s limit and deadline against the rough cost and the booking time. Search once more a few days before closing — permits can change between searches, and a fresh check is cheap insurance. Frequently asked questions

Can an open permit kill a closing?

Yes. On its own it rarely stops a sale. But your lender may refuse to fund the loan. The title company can also refuse a clean policy, and an appraiser might cut the value. If you find it late, there’s no time left to sort any of that out — and that’s how deals die.

How early should I search for open permits?

Before you write the offer is best. On day one of your inspection or due-diligence period is the last good time. After that period ends, you may lose the right to object, even if your lender finds the permit later.

Does a title search show open permits?

No. A title search shows liens, mortgages, easements, and restrictions. Open permits sit at the local building department and need a separate search. Ask in words whether your closing package includes a permit or municipal search that covers them.

What if an open permit is found a week before closing?

Tell the lender, the title company, and the other side at once, in writing. Ask the building department what the permit needs. If it cannot close in time, the usual fallbacks are an extension, a price credit, money held in escrow until it closes, or a policy exception — each one needs the lender and the title company to agree.

Who pays to close an open permit found before closing?

Whoever the contract says. On Florida’s standard FAR/BAR form, the seller closes open and expired permits up to the Permit Limit, 5 days before closing. On an AS IS form, the seller cooperates but does not have to spend money. Read your own form, and get any deal you make in writing.

If I buy a house with an open permit, does it become my problem?

Yes, in the practical sense. The permit stays with the property. Florida law protects an arm’s-length buyer from being fined solely for a prior owner’s failure to close one, and a current owner can close a permit someone else pulled, but the inspection and any correction work still have to get done, and the cost lands where the contract left it.
